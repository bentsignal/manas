As though I could go out fawning,
Is my saintly lord here?
My beloved mother,
Before I look upon my khan and elder's face,
I wish I had never been born.
Wretched me, still in mourning—
Before I look upon Khan Bakai's face,
Let wretched me die before my destined hour!
My champion Bakai, my sultan—
When I see that mighty lion,
Modesty will leave my face!
My elder Bakai, favored by Khizr—
Going out before him so boldly,
How could I stand in his presence?
Let me lie and die without seeing him;
My elder, Lord Bakai—
How could I look upon his face?
My beloved mother,
My precious hair grown down my back—
How could I braid it down in front?
My noble Lord Bakai—
How can I look upon his face?
Rather than go and show myself to my elder,
I shall lie here and die like this!”
So saying, Kanykei
Made her voice heard by God.
Your fierce wolf of a mother, Kanykei,
Fell face down then.
Then your mother Kanykei
Broke into sobbing wails
And wept with piercing cries.
That dear mother of yours, Chyiyrdy, said:
“Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
You are a shameless person!
Would someone with honor act like you?
Could one prosper by calling you honorable?
Did you load seven gelded camels with gold,
And ride a pacing horse in high spirits?
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
Have you come on a pleasure outing?
Where the cuckoo calls melodiously,
Is your planted garden in Talas here?
Saying, ‘I will not see Bakai's face,’
And making such a fuss—
Are those old days in Talas still here?
Your rows of gardens in Keng-Kol are gone;
Saying arrogantly, ‘I will not see him,’
And giving yourself airs—
Those days of yours in Keng-Kol are gone.
The support on which you relied is gone;
We are out in the empty wilderness.
Kanykei, my child, do not act heedlessly without cause.
We fled here without turning back;
You have no night like the Night of Power.
Drawing the curtain and trembling,
Turning away and shaking with sobs,
Refusing to see anyone—
Those former days of yours in Talas are gone!
We have rubbed our calves raw;
We have hurt the soles of our feet.
Kanykei, your good fortune is gone;
Yet you flaunt your airs in the wilderness.
Those days of yours in the royal court are gone!”
The venerable Chyiyrdy
Spoke such words then.
Bulky as a great boulder,
The orphan against her breast—
From Kanykei's arms
She reached and pulled him away.
Making his orphaned state apparent,
The boulder-like child
She made flail his limbs and laugh.
Coughing with great, repeated coughs,
The child, like a stuffed cushion,
Grief-stricken Chyiyrdy
Lifted and carried upon her neck,
Heading toward Khan Bakai,
Carrying her support, the orphan boy,
Your dear mother Chyiyrdy
Let her hem trail on the ground.
The child that noble man left behind—
Making his orphaned state apparent,
Straining forward with every step,
Your poor mother Chyiyrdy
Stumbled as her strength failed.
When she tried to walk, what could she do?
Her poor back twisted in pain;
Chyiyrdy felt she could walk no more.
Rejoicing at the sight of her Bakai,
Her heart burned and burst
As she pleaded with Khan Bakai.
When she swung a leg to take a step,
Her poor knees crackled;
Twelve sinews and eighty joints—
Every single one of them creaked.
Her dear eyes, like blackcurrants,
Grew clouded and filled with discharge.
Her poor chest—what could it do?—
Rattled with every step.
Her strength gone, exhausted,
Your mother Chyiyrdy
Stared helplessly with both eyes.
Her spine, bones and limbs
Lost their strength and hung slack.
To the gray bank of the Syr Darya,
Your dauntless mother Chyiyrdy,
With Semetey tucked beneath her arm,
Barely arrived, her eyes pleading.
Then Bakai the leopard—
A white hound, an arrowshot ahead,
Had come down in front of him;
Chasing it on his gray steed,
Bakai had followed it.
Chyiyrdy standing upon the hill,
And Semetey in her arms—
When he saw those two,
Your uncle Bakai, the leopard,
Was deeply distressed.
Bloody tears flowing from his eyes
Poured down over his collarbones.
His thoughts were badly scattered,
His eyes filled with discharge:
“You who pledged your hand to twelve spiritual guides—
Oh, dreadful day! Manas, if only you had not died!
Orphan, if only you had been spared humiliation!
Since you have come to such a state,
My orphan, who could escape such a fate?
When my lion Manas died,
I wished to die for honor's sake.
The reason aged Bakai
Remained alive instead of dying
Was that Semetey still lived.
Was I to hide myself in Talas,
And come here worn out?
While still a babe in the cradle,
Were you to suffer plunder?
I, Bakai, your caring uncle,
Hiding myself in Keng-Kol—
Was I to arrive too late?
Suffering plunder while so tiny,
Were you, orphan, to be driven away?
I, Bakai, enduring sorrow—
Was I to tear out my beard?
With all those riches proving utterly faithless,
My beloved orphan,
Were you to flee to Samarkand?
Unable to find word of you anywhere,
Was I to tear out my mustache?
Fleeing without a swift horse, wandering on foot,
Were you to go to Bukhara?
Sobbing, unable to find news of you,
Was I, Bakai, to wail?
The support I cherished in my heart,
My little lamp, attained after such suffering,
Why did I not refrain from receiving you
From my champion and friend Manas?
Suffering hardship in this place,
Why did I not avoid being left behind?
Fluttering like a crow,
Your surviving presence, Semetey, remains entrusted to us.
You were a lofty ridge none could cross;
My Magnanimous Manas, take me with you!
While my fierce wolf Manas lived,
Kara-Shaar and Kambyl—
When I held them under siege,
The Kalmyks, Shibe and Chinese—
When I was beating them,
Why did I not die then?
The red banner with its golden crescent—
When I was waving it,
Why did I not die then?
In Beijing, ruled by its khagan,
For six months I was governor and khan;
When I lived in joy,
In the wide world's straitened times,
While my sultan lived,
Why did I not die then?
Taking elephants by the thousand as tribute,
Taking gold loaded upon those elephants,
Thimble mouths and camel-calf eyes,
Glancing dark eyes—
Taking a thousand dark-eyed women,
Golden coins and ruby stones,
Taking abundant taxes and tribute,
As I moved, my marten-fur cap swaying—
When I wore it,
Upon the Chinese as upon crows,
When I descended in attack,
The Chinese with rank-stones on their caps—
Beating their finest men,
When I ruled over them,
Why did I not die then?
Having risen to the golden throne,
When I sat upon it,
Those who answered me defiantly—
Measuring their backs by the span,
When I slashed them,
In the straits of that terrible age,
While my sultan lived,
Why did I not die then?
The Muslims who obeyed my word—
Frightening them with my speech,
The dark unbelievers who would not obey—
Frightening them with my strength,
Like a house built upon a ridge,
Ruling as khan myself,
When I stood secure,
Why did I not die then?
From the orphan and widow,
Needlessly parted, with an evil name—
My Manas, gone to the other world,
Take your Khan Bakai with you.
At Kokotoy's funeral feast,
Before the great assembly,
Seven thousand different peoples came,
As the earth shook and swayed,
My spring flowing through the valley,
Having received a saint's blessing,
My little lamp whom I had asked for,
My morning star in the sky,
The lambskin and otter fur at my collar,
The cherished adornment upon my head,
From fierce-wolf King Manas,
My mirror that I kept,
My heir left by the mighty steed,
My little lamp left by noble-goshawk Manas, the fierce wolf!
My banner fastened to the spear,
My gift left by the noble one,
If I call this waking life, is it a dream?
If I call it a dream, is it waking life?
Have I died before my destined hour,
