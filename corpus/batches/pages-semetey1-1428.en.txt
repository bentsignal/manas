The bulletproof Akolpok—
Your formidable son now put it on.
There was no hole in Akolpok;
For the young son of the bloodletter,
No foe who challenged him would prosper!
The armor left by his father Manas
He pulled on over the rest.
Its buttons numbered nine;
Across his very breast
He matched up all the buttons.
Its fastening cords numbered nine;
He secured every cord.
The gray kise that fell from heaven
Your valiant hero now buckled at his waist.
His face turned wrathful,
And the lion-warrior’s Zulpukor
He fastened at his left side.
When he slung on that fine thing,
It was a mirror upon his shoulder,
And when he entered battle with the foe,
A shield for his entrusted life.
Almabash, the keenest iron,
Made no distinction between far and near;
When your lion wielded it in war,
It would not dull for sixty days.
Look closely: this was the treasure,
The keen blade inherited
From Manas, the gray wolf of old.
Your noble hero’s Akkelte
He now slung upon his shoulder.
Brimming over with fierce spirit,
He covered the matchless stallion
Carefully with its cloth,
So that no stray bullet would strike it.
Wary of the Chinese side,
Upon the great dapple-gray
He fitted the white saddle.
Into the gold-soled stirrups
Your young champion, the noble lord’s son,
Stretched tall and mounted his horse.
He buckled on the scabbardless sword,
And saying, “Mighty Allah, grant me a road!”
Turned toward the khan.
Lamenting in many ways,
Your uncle Bakai, the lion-warrior,
Was left standing gaunt upon the ridge.
The splendid warrior, the valiant man’s son,
Rode proudly toward Beijing.
His eyes were broad as the reaches of a lake,
As though they could devour anyone he saw.
The great Buurul,
That ill-fated, hapless steed,
He made buck and strain;
The peerless Buurul
The leopard-prince made heave and plunge
As though its master had died.
Its tail stood apart, its mane was low;
The poor hero’s Buurul
Had a forelock that did not hang long,
But seemed tied to fit its eyes;
Everyone who saw it
Would burn with longing,
Wishing, “If only that horse were mine!”
When one looked from behind,
The breadth of its croup
Was like a separate open plain;
The size of its hoof
Was like an upturned basin.
Its spreading haunches were like a ravine;
The matchless Arbuurul—
No wind could match it at a trot,
No beast could match its pace.
It flung its forelock to the sky,
And drove its hooves into the earth;
When it bounded, its great stride
Hurled a road’s length, broad as a house,
Stepping on with its rolling gait,
