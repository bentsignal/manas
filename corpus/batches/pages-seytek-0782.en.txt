Beating it into submission—
Why did I not die then?
My chest rattles,
My eyes are festering;
Sixty sinews and eighty bones
Tremble, finding no remedy.
The guiding thought has left me,
My tall, plane-tree stature is gone;
The cords of my eyes are severed,
Courage has fled my breast,
And I have come to the point of death.
The black earth, deep as a bowl,
Will not stir as I am made
To endure this misery.
Old age has come and laid me low;
Skill has left these cherished hands,
The blazing strength has left my arms,
Power, courage, and vigor
Have vanished utterly, leaving nothing.
With a bleached skull for my pillow
And my head turned toward the qibla,
I cannot die though I long for death,
Nor can my proud and precious soul
Bring my hand to end it.
Like spring water flowing down a hill,
My joints fail, and I cannot gather myself.
Tasseled quiver and ornate arrow—
While youth still remained,
I filled the world with tales;
Now I have no fortune left to display.
Fatherly child, Kuyaly,
Though you may now enter the struggle,
My spirit holds countless cares;
That cherished strength like a blue flame,
However much I wish for it, is gone.
This is the hour when age drives courage away,
When ordinary, crumbling earth
Covers the cherished soul.
My mind remains, but I have no means;
Everyone’s turn comes one day—
Damn this filthy world!
My darkened eyes have filmed over;
My two hands, once fearless before the world,
Hang paralyzed and cannot rise.
My whole body has grown numb,
Every joint is constricted,
And dropsy has left every limb
Unable to stir.
All torments have come upon me;
I long to die, but cannot,
And before God, whose decree is stern,
I cannot walk there by myself.
Thus, through old age,
My time of ruin has come.
Courage has fled my breast;
My time of utter emptiness has come.
When I was drunk with liquor,
When youth cultivated wisdom,
When a fierce battle-frenzy seized me,
I would bring dreadful strife
Upon the warring Chinese.
Thus would I raid and conquer
Kakan, stricken by slaughter.
The ways that once were mine
Have become strange to me.
I have reached the hour
When I must cease to be.
Bakai stood there distraught,
Spread his palms, and prayed.
Your uncle Bakai, the gray wolf,
Your forefather who had seen so much,
His eyes had become blind,
His ears deaf,
And the poor man remained
No better than the black earth.
Burdened with grievous suffering,
Though only yesterday he held sway
Over all the teeming multitude,
The aged gray wolf Bakai
Had once charged like a blue wolf.
