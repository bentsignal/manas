Your blue-maned one rescued

Valiant, blue-maned Almambet,
The mighty army charged.

Swept forward thundering;
Their monks from one side
Also entered with cries.

Struck Algara with his whip;

When he thrust his spear,

Took Zhoykuma in his hand;
Your tiger, fierce Alake,

Swift as a flying bird,
No soul knew where he came from,
Syrgak, Ulak Khan's son,
Entered their midst.
His shout split the sky;
He fought without mercy.
The grim-faced one of the Chinese,
In wrath then,
Not sparing his own life,
Kongurbay of the Chinese
Charged at all three.
From beyond him,
Ay Jangjung entered beside him;
Behind him,
Forty officers entered too;
From the rear, a thousand monks
Entered together shouting.
Swift runners and the One-Eyes
All entered in a mass;
They swept into their midst;
Almambet, Chubak and Syrgak
They took among them.
Amid the dust, Kongurbay,
Moving against all three alike,
Thrust his spear without mercy.
However often he thrust his spear,
However many swords struck,
They neither leaned nor stirred;
Their buttocks upon their saddles
Neither tilted nor budged.
Through their center, Almambet
Bored his way with repeated blows;
On both flanks, hero Syrgak
Drove them before him, slaughtering.
With strain upon his life,
The leopard went on fighting.
Where they were could not be known;
Not a single Kyrgyz
Came into their sight.
When thought exhausted, they blazed up;
They grew stronger from their roots.
The earth's surface swarmed;
Armies of every kind came;
From one flank to the other,
They came in numbers beyond sight.
If one looked and watched,
Such was their multitude:
A flowing river, a lake came,
Seething, more than human beings,
A flood like ants came.
When the Chinese swept upon them,
The forty companions, born fierce,
Shouted their battle cry,
Bellowing, “Khan Manas!”
They charged into the vast host.
Becoming tangled in confusion,
Striking and thrusting,
They entered the melee.
So many had their blood spilled;
The multitude lost its strength;
Dividing into two sides,
As the people broke into separate groups,
Ezkara, khan of the Manchus,
Ay Jangjung, khan of the Chinese,
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
Came out apart from the people,
Looming like Mount Opol.

Chubak, Khan Baltay's son,
Reached Ay Jangjung.

Knocked him down as he passed.

From his flank, Kongurbay
Also came charging up;
Upon reaching Chubak,
From atop Kögala,
He knocked him down as he passed.

Before he could cut off his head,
Cow-rumped Sarala,
Almambet made wheel and whirl;
Clinging close, he stood stiff,
His two eyes glaring;
Those who saw him were unsettled;
Flames poured from his mouth.
In the blink of an eye,
He reached Kongurbay.
The grim-faced one from the flank,
“Upon the ribs,” he said,
He struck squarely as he passed.
That grim-faced Kongurbay,
The warrior left his saddle;
As he cried, “Oh!” from his mouth
Blood came in separate gushes.
Courage fled his body;
Strength left the grim-faced wretch.
As the dear champions turned,
As they were about to seize his coat's hem,
As they were about to lay him out lengthwise,
The black horse beneath him,
His black horse—has it, I say,
Wings upon its flanks?
When that grim-faced Kongurbay
Had a dark day fall upon his head,
Always Algara
Had the habit of rescuing him.
The mountain-sized grim-faced one,
Speeding like a flying bird,
It rescued and carried away.
When Kongurbay was wounded
And struggling for his life,
The Chinese, people of forty khans,
Had no support in whom to trust,
Had no lion, the grim-faced champion,
To give them courage,
Had no hero like the grim-faced one!
“Khan Manas!” the forty companions cried,
Entering among the Chinese,
And brought red slaughter.
Chubak, Akbaltay's son,
Survived on that occasion;
Almambet had rescued him.
Through the vast host that had surged in,
Chubak entered shouting,
Among the Chinese from one flank.
Making their horses dart like arrows,
Almambet entered their midst;
Entering beside him, hero Syrgak
Slaughtered the Chinese, making them crawl.
Into their center, Almambet
Bored his way.
From the flank, hero Chubak
Pressed in and enclosed them;
From one side, hero Syrgak
Slaughtered and drove them away.
Beijing in Kakan-Chin,
Before a moment had passed,
Making the Chinese bellow,
Like jackdaws struck by a falcon,
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Driving them back toward Beijing,
Drove them inside;
Along a journey of twelve days,
They pursued without stopping.
Cotton-sashed, broad-booted,
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
Thought deeply upon a plan.
Ay Jangjung and Kongurbay
Sent an envoy between the sides.
As envoy from their people,
A thoroughly eloquent man,
A knowledgeable Chinese went.
Begging Khan Manas,
Pleading again and again,
He asked for seven days' respite.
Then Changtiybes said:
“You came desiring Beijing;
We raised as khan
A leopard, a champion such as you!
From Beijing untouched by Solomon,
Return having ruled it!
Gold and silver,
Return unable to load it all.
Fair young wives and beautiful girls,
Return unable to manage all that booty!
Consent to my request;
I have come begging and pleading;
Give us seven days' respite!
If you rage, Khan Manas,
If you slaughter again, here are the people!
If you kill the envoy who has come,
Here before you am I, a Chinese!
Until the swaying stalks ripened,
Until the autumn gossamer flew,
We stood firm and fought;
Warriors in whom we trusted died;
So many valiant lions died;
From the common soldiery,
Uncountable troops died.
Give us seven days' respite;
Let us consult with the people,
With a mighty one like Karykan.
Let us take counsel and advice;
Taking you to Beijing,
Upon Karykan's throne,
Let us seat you too;
Putting a golden crown upon you,
Let us raise you as khan!”
Saying this, Changtiybes,
As he kept chattering and pleading,
Now won consent to his words;
Magnanimous Manas, the leopard,
Granted seven days' respite.
“Manas has granted a respite,” they said;
All the Burut
Slept freely;
Bored, they played knucklebones;
Played chess with a clatter;
Played wildly, running mad;
Young and old,
All lay at ease.
Untiring when out scouting,
Untroubled when left with the baggage train,
Almambet, Azizkan's son,
Came to Manas:
“Beijing, seven months away,
You called a journey of seven days;
The Chinese you besieged,
You called warriors without courage.
You have granted seven days' respite;
Farewell, my friend Manas;
We shall not withdraw safely from here;
Not one of us shall return alive.
We who came shall not return safely;
We shall not reach Talas alive!
When I speak with my tongue,
You will not accept my words;
You mean to end my days;
You mean to dig my pit;
Never taking my advice,
You mean to make my misery boil!
You mean to bring upon my head
Strange marvels from far away;
You mean to let them take as spoils
My peerless horse Sarala!
You have granted seven days' respite,
Letting them prepare far and wide,
Letting them gather from end to end;
You mean to strengthen the infidels.
It is God's doing;
You mean to spill my blood!
I shall speak my last wishes:
If you reach Talas safely,
If you see the people safe,
My horses in the mountains number nine thousand,
Sturdy chargers and swift steeds together.
If torment comes upon one's head,
Where might a lone head not be left!
You granted respite without consulting me;
My regret remains—what can I do?
Tears flow from my eyes like a lake!
My horses in the mountains have no master;
Gather my horses in the mountains.
She who went together with me,
Aruuke, Akkan's daughter,
My wedded wife, without good fortune,
Marry her to Mazhik!
Beside my horse there is a bridle;
In Aruuke's womb,
There remains a three-month child.
If a boy is born,
Slaughter ninety for his feast;
Name him hero Külchoro!
The horse he rides will be gray;
His right shoulder will be broad;
His world will be complete;
He will be a greater lion than I!
He will be an ember glowing red;
Entering a vast host, he will be a shot!
He will turn white deer into cattle;
He will throw those who grapple with him into confusion!
Setting grass-grown land ablaze,
He will set fire to river valleys;
He will make a way into fortified defenses;
If an enemy stands against him,
Before any mortal, he will attack!
When I turned Sarala's head,
When we summered in Ürbüs,
When the Argyn gathered and encamped,
My betrothed Aruuke,
When she craved lion's flesh in pregnancy,
At Ürbüs's head, rocky hills,
I scouted the pass of Muzdak;
Shooting a lion and bringing it down,
I killed it and split its belly.
Frying its heart,
I gave it to Aruuke.
‘If male, he will be my support,’ I said;
‘If female, one day
She will find her match and leave,’ I said.
From that day until this,
I have cherished that hope!
If it is a girl, not a boy,
Shoot a wild ewe and eat it;
Almambet, gone to the hereafter—
‘My hawk, Almam, my soul's friend,
Passed without a male heir,’ say that!
I shall not return safely to my people;
Now I shall not withdraw alive!
If I die at Chinese hands,
Grasp me by the waist and fold me;
Do not leave my bones upon the plain;
Load me upon Sarala!
When you reach a burial ground,
Hollow out my grave spaciously;
Slaughter Sarala;
Digging my grave and commending me,
Lay me there with your own hands!
This Great Beijing standing here,
The place where my mother bore me,
The place where my bleeding cord was tied,
The place where my shirt was taken off and washed,
The place where my navel blood fell.
In this place, Khan Manas,
My body's blood will be spilled.
The reason it will be spilled:
Kojozhash, Karazhoy's son,
When I was a young child at play,
Wildly unruly and reckless,
When I reached six years of age,
Honored among the Chinese,
To the uplands called Suuk-Tör,
To the land of the One-Eyes,
Before the dragon,
In the first year, to Beijing,
They sent six thousand children;
Six thousand children perished
From the dragon's venom;
Six children and I remained.
Seven thousand children went together;
Among the seven thousand,
Seven children and I remained.
Ten thousand children went together;
Only ten children and I remained.
While I was studying,
He saddled a gray young mare;
Kojozhash, Karazhoy's son,
Studied about six months before me.
He remained knowing how to fly;
I remained knowing how to travel.
I heard news from those who came:
For eight years, without dying or perishing,
I heard he suffered a lingering, incurable illness.
If he recovers from his illness
And comes here,
He will leave none of us well;
He will spare none of us unharmed.
An acrobat on horseback, a swift runner,
Who emerges when China is breaking apart,
The state's great marksman!”

Before he could close his mouth,
Pale, mottled pennants and a red banner,

An uproar of cries arose.

For the Chinese to rejoice,

The spiritually powerful marksman Kojozhash
Had truly arrived.
Khan Manas's forty companions,

Serek and Syrgak, the two lions,

The massed Chinese,

Shuutu, with his cap like a yurt's felt covering,
Ajybay, Argynkan's son,
Yraman's Singer's Son,

Struck by bullets when death came,
To die while fighting—

Misery had been set to boil;
They never tired of swarming in,
The peoples of forty khans together.
A lion supported by sacred powers,
Alöökö's Kongurbay
Was a hero born exceptional.

The countless Chinese behind him
That hero born exceptional, Kökchö,
They surrounded on every side.

Without looking around,
Without sparing his life at all,
In the opening and closing of an eye,
Before a word could be said,
Striking each of them a blow,
He plunged among the Chinese,
Drove them fleeing, attacked them;
Kökchö, taken captive—

Have you heard of him?
A trick rider, swift on foot,
The state's great marksman,
Not to be subdued by a weakling,
In darkness, a needle's

Kojozhash, Karazhoy's son,
Clad in green, came rushing up;
Aydarkan's Kökchö
He struck through the temple,
Bringing Kökchö down with a bullet.

The Muslims grieved bitterly;
The Chinese, peoples of forty khans,
Laughed aloud in their joy.

Plunging among them on Sarala,
Your Alma began the slaughter.
Kojozhash, Karazhoy's son—
With his fearsome sword in hand,
He slashed him lengthwise.

A coral stone the size of a flask
Shattered into fragments.
Unable to withstand his fury,
The countless Manchus, the many Chinese,
Fled in a scattering rush.
The Chinese khan Ay Jangjung
Drew across his path;
With golden forelock, tasseled belt,
Azizkan's only son
Jangjung, barring his way,
He flung aside as he passed.
Behind him, the hero Syrgak,

Could lift him onto a horse,
From Jangjung, the Chinese khan,

No man's strength could match him,
No lion's teeth could pierce him,
Tall of body, broad of shoulder,
Chubak, Akbaltay's son—
Ezkara, riding a great bay,
Was the khan of the Manchus;

Chubak, Akbaltay's son,

Kanjar Kol, riding a swift runner,

Before he could save him alive,
Your fierce Alma reached them.
Ezkara, the Chinese khan,
He cut into forty-two pieces,
And sixty of his noble officers
He struck down along with him.
Look at the dust rising underfoot,
Look at the flowing blood,
Mixed with blood, face down,
Look at the fallen men!
You cannot catch sight of them.
As though tumbleweeds had blown away,
You cannot tell where they are.
The sky is clear, the earth overcast,
Not a single blade shows on the ground.
Earth flies up, dust boils,
Meeting them in countless clashes,
They hold them back, misery boiling.
Winged steeds die in swathes,
Mixed with the earth,
Ashen gray, heroes die.
When the Chinese swarm over them,
When they inflict terrible torment,
Spearheads clash together,
Swords clash against swords;
Without turning back, the noble heroes,
Without rest by night or day,
Slaughter one another, fighting.
Among the beasts, the livestock die;
Look closely: in Beijing,
Everyone they meet is killed.
(Seen today, gone tomorrow—
So deceitful is this world,
Damn your father's grave, this wicked world!)
The people thin out, one by one,
Those they meet are slaughtered,
They crush whoever faces them,
Send them floating in bloody water.
Amid utter turmoil,
The heroes keep fighting,
Eating their meals on horseback,
Without turning their steeds aside;
All those white hawks,
Without pausing to watch a moment—
Of the Chinese with rank buttons,
When many khans had died,
Kongurbay's spirit broke,
His courage left him;
With Kojozhash his source of strength,
He turned Algara aside.
That cursed cultivator of wisdom
Stood pondering a plan:
“They have slaughtered my Chinese people,
Ravaged my land of Beijing;
Those daughter-divorced Buruts
Have brought so many of my lions low.
They have conquered and destroyed
Those who never submitted to another,
Who never suffered humiliation,
My precious Chinese people!
They broke my defenses and opened a road,
Attacked my people without restraint.
First they raided my horses,
They wiped out my people,
Plundered so many of my possessions;
Giving me no rest by day,
They robbed me of sleep at night.
They plundered and took much of my wealth,
The Buruts wrought great devastation.
When springtime comes,
They will not let the earth be opened,
Will not let seed be scattered by handfuls;
The many Chinese, out of fear,
Cannot step beyond the fortress.
They torment us and drain our strength;
As though our wealth were not enough,
They take slaves, women slaves, and men's heads.
There is no justice in the Buruts,
Those daughter-divorced ones, corrupt in their greed;
Whenever the chance comes,
They spill red blood,
Take heads that bear no guilt.
Of the Buruts who have done this,
Unable to bear the humiliation,
The Chinese, peoples of forty khans,
I shall gather every one.
Though I gather them all together,
They have no strong support to rely on;
My excellent champion Kojozhash,
They have no lion like you.
The Chinese are a vast multitude; I stand alone.
For lack of a fierce champion,
I have lost many heroes.
Why should I speak of the others?
They dug my pit deep;
That Syrgak standing there
Has shown me many dreadful marvels.
So long as Syrgak lives,
He will never leave us whole.
He is twenty-one years old,
He has reached his swelling prime.
His skill is terribly formidable;
It has reached my marrow,
It has pierced my every bone—
The skill of that slave Syrgak!
Young Syrgak is a hero,
A lion as great as Manas.
Unless you contrive to save yourself,
He is a hero who will kill you.
The forty Chinese tribes, all these people,
Let us gather without looking back;
You are the mighty marksman,
Let us bring on Judgment Day!
Let us slaughter them all;
If their fierce lion Manas remains,
Let us bind him alive.”
With cotton belt and broad boots,
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
A hero skilled among spearmen,
Who knew every trick of war,
A lion truly excellent in wisdom,
Prepared with his champions,
Chose his marksmen, massed them;
His swordsmen never flinched,
They plunged fiercely into battle.
Those skilled at thrusting spears
He set apart in a separate group;
His marksmen who never missed
He set apart as well.
He grouped those of fine appearance separately;
The Chinese, peoples of forty khans,
He stretched out in forty divisions.
Amid a wild uproar,
Amid a roar of cries of “Woe!”

The Chinese surged forward.
So numerous were these Chinese,
They rolled in waves like a lake.
At the forefront of the Chinese,
The state's great marksman,
Kojozhash had arrived.
They had girded themselves, readied themselves,

The handful of Muslims,
Against the surging Chinese:

Cut Kongurbay off from his host,
Take my vengeance far away!” they said.
Against the boiling Chinese,
“I shall charge and thrust my spear!”

Struck by bullets when death came,
“I shall drive them as far as Beijing,
On Beijing, untouched by the great flood,
I shall wreak slaughter without stopping;
I shall kill the khans of Beijing,
Kakan-Chin's golden throne
I shall seize back!” they said.
Taking salt to season with,
Pursuing them all the way to Beijing,
Taking beautiful, beautiful girls—
Among horses, the winged steed is piebald-headed;
Almambet and Chubak are names alike.
Who never turned back when death came,
Who never drew their heads from death,
Syrgak, Ulak Khan's son,
Ajybay, Argynkan's son,

Khan Manas's forty companions
Shouted from forty sides,
Slaughtering without stopping,
Crying the battle cry “Manas!”
Muzburchak, Buudayyk Khan's son,
With a host that could not be turned,
Along a winding, bull-piss road,
People swarmed over the earth.
A red banner with a golden crescent—
The one who took and raised the banner
Was a khan like Muzburchak.
Their champions grew frenzied,
Every one of the heroes
Raged beyond restraint.
Crossing many, many ridges,
Treading many wondrous lands,
Fighting with gunfire in Beijing,
Battling in many places,

Slaughtering his father, laying him low,
Was this not your Alma who had come?
Kara-Too, where tea is gathered—
Was this not your Alma who crossed it?
Great stores of yellow gold,
Not valuing them at half a hair,

Almambet now saw.
Despairing of his borrowed life,
Karykan's weather stone,
The spell that brought down rain,
He drew out from his side.

He dropped it with a plop.
Rattling away in Kalmyk,
Babbling away in Chinese,
Sarala, with a cowlike rump,
Almambet sent whirling;
He pranced and pranced about,
He made the rain fall.
Clouds swept across the sky,
Covered the face of the earth;
Clouds thundered in the heavens,
The earth whirled with a roar.
Almambet worked his weather magic.
He turned a summer day to winter,
The thinly clothed Chinese
He made whimper like girls.
Turning the mountaintops to ice,
Whirling without cease,
Slaughtering people, working weather magic,
With a sword clanking in his hand—
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
Dismounted, shivering.
To drive his people onward,
To work weather magic one more day,
To drive them back into Beijing—
That was Almambet's intent.
Of the Chinese with rank buttons,
He smashed their very finest;
Of the Chinese with red tassels,
He laid their strongest low,
Working mighty weather magic.
