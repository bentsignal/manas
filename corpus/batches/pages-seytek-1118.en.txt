Tempered with poison, its edge honed keen,
Forged from the hide of a black male camel,
Its blood-drinking edge put to the test,
The wondrous Ach Albarst, unlike any other—
So the storytellers have sung of it.
If you go to battle, that is the weapon;
If you eagerly confront the foe,
I reckon its thrill is beyond measure.
When you draw it slantwise,
This sword will be your companion till Judgment Day.
When you gloriously drive the enemy back,
Its steel-gray edge turns red.
When you swing it in wrath,
You cleave through ill-fated foes,
And it lengthens to a spear’s reach.
When you brandish it in turmoil,
Your treasure rests secure in your hands;
When you strike the advancing foe,
It will satisfy your laughter.
There were nine pieces of battle armor;
Clad in his battle armor,
The fortunate hero
Revealed the bearing of a tiger.
When he rode against the enemy,
Before that fortunate slayer
The two keen beasts, Koibos and Taibas,
Slunk along ahead,
While the giant black bird Zumuruk,
Stretching out its talons grandly,
Sported high in the sky.
His nine guardian spirits
Were then supporting him.
Taking a shortcut along the narrow path,
The dervish named Khizir
Had taken the hero
And led him by the bridle for a year.
When the destroyer was about to leave the horde,
With the true seer at their head,
Six sorcerers from China
Had come to test the noble one.
The judges who came to test him,
Those who watched from afar,
Would observe tomorrow
His courage and his worth,
How far his strength might reach,
And the full measure of his power,
Then, omitting not a single word,
Would report it to their khan.
Those who watched from afar,
Those who secretly kept watch,
Those charged to deliver a single report,
Those two-eyed magicians,
Those shamans who came from Beijing,
Trusted men of the vast Chinese nation of forty tribes,
Men in whom they placed firm faith,
Men with eyes that watched from afar,
Men skilled in polished words,
Spies who watched in concealment,
Judges who could assess a man—
When Seytek was mounting his horse,
Fastening all his gear,
And Akbermet was being handed to him,
They clearly witnessed the fate
Of the ill-starred Zholtoi.
The two called Dökörchü and Mangi,
Seeing Seytek’s conduct
And grieving at what he did,
Said, “He is a tiger indeed!”
Then both of them died,
Their hearts bursting together.
The spy who meant to observe died,
Saying, “He is truly ill-starred!”
The judge who meant to test him died;
Seeing the gray-maned hero’s deeds,
The watcher standing afar died.
Zhöökülük watched it all—
Zhöökülük stirred up great turmoil,
Fled in frantic confusion,
And Zhöökülük, who had come to test him,
Despaired of his entrusted life.
