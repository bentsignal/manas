Sending word to Beijing,
The many smiths there
He gathered, every one.
When his smiths arrived,
He gave stern commands.
Those who knew the nature of sharp steel,
The most skillful, he assembled.
His smiths came together;
Having gold drawn over its handle,
Having poison spread upon its face,
They went on making the battle-axe,
Delighting all who saw it.
In a dragon's venom
They steeped it six or seven times.
“Let death arrive when it strikes;
Let its victim burst and shatter!
The enemy it encounters,
Let his death come in this way!”
When Manas mounted his throne,
Karykan was not dismayed.
When many of his men were slaughtered,
Karykan did not waste away.
He had long been a khan;
Except that he was an infidel,
He was a man of sacred power.
Disaster that would come before him,
Six months in advance, Karykan
Was able to foresee.
The whole city of Beijing
He was the khan who held in his sway.
To Karykan himself
He was a close associate.
If you considered his foresight,
Kongurbay, son of Alöökö,
Was the equal of Karykan.
A fitting battle-axe,
After six months' work, they completed.
Taking the axe, Kongurbay,
Resting neither by day
Nor sleeping through the night,
Kept prowling, that Kongurbay.
With Koshoy and Bakay at their head,
They proclaimed his khanship.
“Manas has become khan of Beijing
And mounted the golden throne!” they said.
Setting him upon Zhelmaian,
Hero Shuutu, with his felt-roof-sized hat,
They sent to announce the good news.
To Kanykey's presence,
The broad-humped black camel,
Hero Manas's Zhelmaian—
No wind could match his trot,
No beast could match his walk.
Zhelmaian, swiftest among beasts,
Escaped the eye when one looked;
His running could not be perceived.
Then hero Shuutu
Spread the news among the people,
To the Argyn and Nogoy in Talas,
To Kaynar's dark-eyed beauty.
Bearing good news, he went
To Kanykey herself.
“Upon the Chinese emperor's Beijing
He swept and waged war.
Fighting six months without rest,
We destroyed Beijing's palace;
We slaughtered its shaven-headed priests.
From Beijing we seized
Many a splendid pacing horse.
We raised the dust of Beijing;
We slaughtered many of its khans.
Of famed Chinese Beijing,
We felled all its willows;
We pushed its buildings down.
Beijing where no prophet had trodden,
We entered at its head and made a road.
Beijing that no one dared touch,
We entered and attacked.
Besides launching our attack,
We took much splendid booty;
We took gold coins in elephant-loads.
We took a thousand dark-eyed ones.
Beijing that Alexander never attacked,
We found a way and assaulted.
Beijing that Chalyar could not reach,
We overturned, entering within;
Our strength sufficed, and we took it.
Beijing untouched by the Flood,
Scattering its people to pieces,
We crushed and conquered.
We smashed it to fragments;
We bloodied our lance points;
We turned heaven and earth to dust.
Passing among the Chinese,
We slaughtered, destroyed and crippled them.
Putting a golden crown upon him,
Magnanimous warrior Manas
We made khan of Great Beijing.”
After Shuutu had gone with the news,
After he had delivered the news,
Kanykey, the khan's wife,
The beauty of the dusky-complexioned,
The radiant one among Muslims,
When Shuutu came with the news,
Stood weeping with piercing cries.
Her tears flowed in ten streams;
She stood sobbing and wailing.
How could Kanykey rejoice?
Far from rejoicing, she grieved.
“Instead of saying he mastered Beijing,
Say he has come to know hardship!
Instead of saying he mounted a golden throne,
Say his appointed death has come!
At Beijing untouched by torrents,
His shouting lance attack
Means the people will be left slaughtered.
Beijing the Flood could not cover—
Did he say he would enter without stopping?
Though I tried to stop him, he would not listen.
A young son remains behind;
Did he mean to cast me into hell?
Beijing where no prophet trod—
Did he say his worth would suffice?
My forehead bone was curved;
After my sovereign passes away,
My means of living would be scant.
My people's taking Beijing
Means this wretch is left in trouble!
Beijing, whose ridges are costly,
He called a journey of five days.
My champion called himself mighty;
He called the people of Beijing lowly.
If he stays in Beijing as khan,
If he keeps delighting in Beijing,
All his keen warriors
He will let the Chinese take as gifts!
What I have heard of Beijing—
Those who went there did not turn back;
Those who met it did not return safely!
If one pleaded and asked,
He who stays in Beijing would die.
Consider it, hero Shuutu:
When would he see his child?
If he dies while staying in Beijing,
On the hills are blaze-faced horses;
A young son remains behind.
There is no kinsman to lean upon;
All the relatives are enemies.
In Talas, the Argyn and Nogoy are their own masters.
If he dies in Beijing,
The tears in my eyes will never dry!”
That night, as Kanykey lay,
She saw something unimagined;
She dreamed a dreadful dream.
Fire rose from Talas and burned;
All the ridges collapsed.
The vast lake and river dried up;
The plane tree withered!
Startled awake from sleep,
She stood weeping and shrieking.
Kanykey, the khan's daughter,
Stood sobbing and wailing.
Her ribs torn with grief,
Tears pouring from her eyes,
Her heart grievously broken,
The broad world grown narrow,
That imposing Manas—
She longed to see him once.
The reason for her longing:
When the Beijing campaign began,
After she had borne Semetey,
He departed ten days later.
The joy of Manas's company,
Had she not been denied it?
Yearning for her lord,
Kanykey, embracing him—
Had she not been denied lying so?
“If he stays heedlessly in Beijing,
My champion may suddenly die.
United with him when tiny,
Joined to him when thumb-sized,
When will he see this wretched woman?”
So Kanykey stood grieving.
From dark eyes like blackcurrants,
Tears ran a whip's length;
From dark eyes like a saker falcon's,
Tears fell with every blink.
Weeping, too weak to stand,
Unable to find a way,
To Shuutu with the felt-roof-sized hat
She gave six swift horses,
And a letter the size of her palm.
Crying out her sorrow,
Sobbing, burning with grief—
Khan Saykal, daughter of Karacha Khan,
Had given Arbuudan as a gift.
Sixty foals had been killed
To foster and rear Arbuudan.
Owl feathers were fixed to his head;
He was allotted to Semetey
And tended by the forty companions.
Taibuurul, who was with Manas,
Was reaching his fifth year,
Was fit to ride on campaign.
Each of his limbs was exceptional;
He was unlike other beasts.
Twisting his neck like a stag,
If a pit a target's distance wide appeared,
He bounded without hesitation.
Hearing of his swiftness,
Offering gold by the pood and ornaments,
The khan of Bukhara had asked for him.
They would not give him when asked.
Far from giving Buurul away,
No one without ritual ablution rode him.
Twisting his neck like a wild sheep,
If a pit a rope's length wide appeared,
He bounded unwaveringly.
His neck curved like an apple,
If a cliff a lance's height appeared,
He sprang and bounded like a roe deer.
A hardy Karabayyr warhorse,
Sieve-like lungs and copper forelegs,
No mother claimed to have borne him,
No stallion father had sired him;
A pure child of the unseen realm.
Semetey, still a babe in the cradle,
She dressed in mail armor.
As an auspicious rite, the tiny child
Semetey she mounted upon the horse.
Gold ingots and rubies
She placed in his saddlebags.
From his grandfather Jakyp,
To receive a pure blessing,
Leading Semetey,
Kanykey set out.
At his grandfather Khan Jakyp's house,
Grain beer stood in a wooden tub.
Within that grain beer,
Potent poison was mixed as well.
At that moment Khan Jakyp—
“There is deceit in his eyes;
He means to trip me up.
There is crookedness in his eyes,
There is guile in his words.
If he can find a way, without mercy,
He means to act against me.”
Looking and speaking together,
His senior wife Bakdöölöt
And he consulted.
“As a wife she ruined the people;
As a maiden, the hussy
Shook the age and wrecked the land.
When that wretch was a maiden,
The world saw the trouble she caused.
When Manas went to marry her,
With hungry steel, Manas's
Arm she pierced and wounded.
A great quarrel that will never heal
She is coming to contrive.
Her tiny wriggling creature
She has brought along, strapped on.
Something from us
She is coming to ask.”
When Jakyp said this,
As though pierced in a gland,
Bakdöölöt started in alarm.
“Wretched dried-up head, you will never prosper!
Always chattering away,
You will not stop your mischief!
If you go on like this, dried-up old man,
You will never prosper at all!
With your beard already white,
At the time when death is near,
You still cannot leave such words alone!
Your banner fastened to a lance,
If Semetey lives safely,
He is your gift born of magnanimous Manas.
Your hoof born of a splendid steed,
From your goshawk son Manas,
Your only little darling born.
When infidels and Muslims
Stood facing one another,
He was your keen one conceived by a blessing.
Consider it, you dried-up head!
While this Manas is alive,
There is no boat upon the dark water;
Among the people over all the earth,
There is none to defy you!
While your son Manas lives,
You have rinsed your mouth with honey;
You have become a great man.
Your herds grew, and for the people
You became a plane tree and a willow.
If Manas closes his eyes,
The plane tree will be felled.
The khanship you hold now
Will be quenched as if with water.
Refusing to obey your word,
The Argyn will move elsewhere.
Refusing to heed what you say,
The Nogoy will go another way.
If I live, I shall see it: from those six dogs
You will suffer incurable torment.
“Fierce-maned Manas, my morning star!”
You will lament then, dried-up old man,
And die before your appointed death!
Your wish unfulfilled,
You will wail, dried-up old man;
Your bones will remain unburied.
Take heed, dried-up old man:
Common iron is no tempered steel.
Do not trust them as your sons, dried-up head;
Your six sons are no people to support you!
Patched-together iron is no tempered steel;
Those rabid dogs are no people to support you!
When magnanimous Manas dies,
Your gold will not be worth charcoal.
Tormenting you, those six wretches,
Once grown fat, will raid your camp.
Your silver will not be worth iron.
After your sun Manas dies,
Those six accursed wretches,
Once grown fat, will raid your people.
Your mail will not be worth felt;
They will not value you at a penny.
If the falcon closes his eyes,
Will your fostered foals not devour you?
“Stop fidgeting, useless beard!
Sit still!” will they not say?
Think, alas, dried-up old man!
Will those words not eat at you?
I do not rate a hollow as a plain;
I do not rate those six fellows of yours
As I rate your champion son Manas.
The marten-fur cap upon your head
She is coming to make you toss with joy.
Having mounted her child upon a horse,
Seeking your blessing from you,
With goodwill, bringing gold,
She is coming to give you joy.
Winning your heart,
She is coming to set you at ease.
Kanykey, who knows proper custom,
In your old age, useless beard,
Is coming to make you rich.”
(Such a senior wife as Bakdöölöt!)
Dear daughter-in-law Kanykey,
Gleaming in mail armor,
To ask Jakyp's blessing,
Came walking, radiant.
“Khan-father, give me your blessing to receive;
To the son who has gone on a journey,
Then let me send the news—
Your child who has gone far away!
After sending word to him,
Semetey, whom I have cradled,
My morning star beside the moon,
My moon, my sun, my sultan,
My only one, whom I hold as my companion!
My morning star above my head,
My precious fur and beaver at my collar,
The one I open my mouth to kiss,
The one I open my eyes to see,
My spring flowing through the valley,
My little one granted when I was near death,
My dark-marked hunting hawk,
My plane tree granted by fate,
My falcon among the winged,
My splendid steed among the four-footed—
I have come to ask your blessing;
Khan-father, I beg it of you.”
Thus Kanykey spoke then.
(The gold and rubies
Kanykey took out and gave.)
Lifting her child,
Bowing in homage,
Bending to ask a blessing,
Kanykey stood looking at him.
His grandfather Khan Jakyp
Looked over the rubies.
Ill-intentioned Khan Jakyp,
To the only child Semetey,
Gave a half-hearted blessing.
Receiving Jakyp's blessing,
Poor woman Kanykey,
Walking with a sway and a rustle,
Returned to the royal camp.
When she reached the royal camp,
“Beijing where no saint would go—
If he stays heedlessly, he will die.
My lord, my lion, my magnanimous one,
When will he return to the royal camp?
Beijing the Flood cannot cover—
If he stays there, he will die.
My champion, sultan, leopard,
When will he see his wretched woman?”
She cried, weeping and shrieking.
She gave six swift horses;
Setting him upon Taibuurul,
“Go back quickly!” she said.
Entrusting the charge to Shuutu,
She gave him a palm-sized letter.
While Kanykey was speaking thus,
Aruuke, daughter of Agynay, said:
“Little remains until my month is due;
I am about to give birth.
From the anguish of longing,
Before bearing the child,
On the journey to Judgment Day
I feel I am about to depart.
My patterned golden kerchief,
My champion, warrior Shuutu,
Take this as a gift.
‘She is about to give birth,’ say,
And from my noble-born lord
Receive your reward for the good news!
The bridle kept for his horse—
Who knows whether boy or girl?
The child in the womb is nearly full-grown.”
Hearing this, Shuutu,
Mounted Buurul and made him surge,
Urging him like a widow bereaved,
Taibuurul beneath him
Raced with flying birds.
The white reins tied in a rounded knot
Were gripped tight in his right hand.
With exceptionally swift Buurul,
He struggled against the horse's mouth.
Hooves clattered upon the ground;
The black steel bit
Clanked at his throat.
Appearing for a moment,
Then vanishing as he stretched away,
Breaking away like blue smoke,
Shattering the stones he trod,
Speeding like a flying bird,
He had lost flesh.
The extraordinary steed Taibuurul—
If you ask the whole account,
Since leaving broad Talas,
Traveling six days,
Covering much ground without holding back,
Near Beijing
Hero Shuutu had now come.
His foresight surpassed
All the people of the age;
Kongurbay, son of Alöökö,
“News will come from Talas,” he thought.
At the base of Innermost Beijing,
Sleeping neither at night
Nor resting by day,
Watching the roads in ambush,
He was lying in wait.
“This year, on Beijing's throne,
The time has come for him to settle.
The discerning woman Kanykey
Was a cultivator of wisdom.
It is time for her to learn this;
She will send someone between them.
From the Buruts staying in Beijing,
The woman will obtain news.
If he hears word from Kanykey,
Manas will strengthen his defenses.
The Chinese multitude in Beijing,
Even worse than this, the Buruts
Will cast into further trouble.”
So Kongurbay, khan of the shaven-headed ones,
Whose strength could match the whole world,
Whose reach extended to Beijing,
Had become a khan of full understanding.
Dust rose wherever he went;
Lives departed at his voice.
His cunning was great and skillful;
His might was beyond any man's reach.
Everything favored him.
Thinking over schemes and stratagems,
He was watching Manas in ambush.
This year was his time to take his prize;
Shuutu, who came with news,
He was watching for.
The Fierce One, huge as a hut,
Would spare nothing against Shuutu.
When Shuutu looked carefully,
He lost his reason and went numb.
When he saw Kongurbay,
Unable to find a single plan,
He stood utterly bewildered.
When he looked at Kongurbay,
His eyes were like mouths of graves,
As though sucking in all they saw;
His eyes were like hollows of a lake,
As though swallowing all they saw.
His nose was like a mountain ridge;
He himself was like Heaven above.
Between his brows and eyelids
Was like a grave whose roof had fallen.
Broad forehead, wide and flat,
Like a camel standing apart,
The war-wolf, hero Fierce One,
Without mercy where they met,
Made the steed Algara surge,
Urging him like a widow bereaved,
His eyes flashing like a tiger's,
His broad, door-sized teeth
Crackling as he clenched them,
Fire flashing from his eyes,
Wind issuing from his mouth,
Unable to contain his wrath,
Alöökö's Kongurbay,
Like a crag falling from a mountain,
Came thundering along.
When he saw Kongurbay,
Shuutu, the servant bringing news,
Lost the strength in his arms,
Lost the casing of his heart.
There was no way to turn back:
Kongurbay had met him head-on.
Unable to withstand his wrath,
Unable to endure his heat,
His black rawhide whip
He grasped and swung around,
And struck Buurul, entering his fifth year,
Across the hocks.
When the whip struck his hocks,
Taibuurul beneath him
Broke away like a mirage,
As though he had taken flight;
The dark earth turned over,
Folding away behind him.
The face of the earth shimmered,
His four feet clattered;
No one could tell where he was,
His hooves never caught the ground:
Taibuurul went flying.
The dark earth his hooves struck
Was hollowed like a hearth-pit;
The ground he trod crumbled,
And the thunder of four feet
Roared right overhead.
He bolted, wild with excitement,
Appearing now and then,
Appearing, yet eluding the eye,
Breaking away like a cloud,
Taibuurul drew far ahead,
From a steed like Algara
Springing a whole calling-distance clear!
No wind could catch his trot:
Taibuurul was accomplished indeed.
No beast could match his walk:
Taibuurul was of another kind.
On the ridge of Yrgayty,
As they moved to and fro,
Swaying against each other,
When Almambet truly joined battle
When the beautiful Ak Saykal
Mighty Manas, lion among men,
Seized her from her horse,
In fear for her entrusted life,
Saying, “I shall be yours hereafter,”
She gave Taibuurul as tribute.
The extraordinary warrior maiden Saykal
Gave Taibuurul as a gift;
With his mane and tail streaming,
She sent him Taibuurul.
“Let him ride him on campaign,
Let him know his prowess,” she said,
Giving Taibuurul after trying him.
High-backed, yet low in stature,
He would not falter racing through hills;
Until sixty years of maturity,
His molars would not wear with age.
Ridden back and forth, he never tired;
For seventy days and nights,
Even ridden without a break,
He would show no weakness or stumble.
Without glancing behind,
Shuutu the servant fled fiercely.
Without looking around,
Not sparing his life a penny's worth,
Shuutu the servant fled in earnest.
When he looked ahead,
There appeared through the haze,
Its surface foam and misty vapor,
Directly across his path,
Black mud and dark water.
To slaughter him at the dark water,
To take the Buurul beneath him
As captured booty:
Coming up from behind,
Kongurbay was thinking,
“I shall load him with gold and silver,
The Buurul beneath him;
Having struck Shuutu down,
I shall present him to Karykan.
The Buurul beneath him,
With shields spread wide,
Is a horse for a governor to ride;
With his waist-guard fastened,
A lord of Beijing should ride him.
To a luckless Burut,
How did such a horse come?
For riding at leisure,
This roan horse has no equal.
A golden horse-cloth—
Would one not cover such a beast with it?
Within a fortress with golden gates,
Would one not groom him each month
And tend such a beast?
To keep his feet from treading stones,
Would one not have gold horseshoes bent
And nail them on such a beast?
To Buurul's forelock,
Would one not attach it?
Would the khan of Kakanchyn
Not delight in such a beast?”
Burning within, all aflame,
Coveting Taibuurul,
His two eyes turned to four;
He raced along behind,
Urging Algara onward:
Kongurbay went on.
He pursued in earnest,
His saddle-flaps fluttering,
A deep-blue bead-stone, flask-sized,
Bobbed upon his head.
Among the Chinese with rank-buttons,
He was one of the finest;
Among the jacket-wearing Chinese,
He was a vat-sized great man,
He was Alöökö's son.
Besides possessing wisdom,
He was a judge of horses.
His words were clear when he spoke,
His eyes were clear when he gazed,
His face was open among men.
His right shoulder was broad;
Kongurbay was no ordinary man,
A hero worth both left and right.
Trapped before the muddy water,
That Shuutu was stranded now.
“Buddha has given me the Burut!”
The lacquered spear upon his arm
He took into his hand.
Bent low at the waist,
His eyes blazing and staring,
His battle-axe-like moustache
Divided into separate strands;
The earth split and shimmered:
Alöökö's Kongurbay
Was drawing near.
As Kongurbay approached,
About to heap torment on him,
That hero Shuutu's
World shriveled in dread.
Unable to find any means,
Looking from side to side,
Licking his lips and nose,
The reins tied in a round knot
He did not take in a proper grip;
Straight toward the dark water
He let Buurul go.
He curved his neck like a stag;
Across the dark water, a target's distance,
That marvelous steed Buurul,
Bearing brave Shuutu,
Ran without hesitation.
As he gathered himself to spring,
The mouth of his belt-pouch opened;
The letter of greeting Kanykey wrote
Scattered into the dark water.
His gazelle-like eyes narrowed;
The accomplished steed Buurul
Struck the bank of the dark water,
Landing once upon the farther edge.
Kongurbay, who knew his craft,
Along a winding, bull-urine track,
Along the ground that curved away,
Not letting Buurul draw far off,
Came racing like a mirage;
Was his mount not a marvelous steed too,
Hard upon Taibuurul's heels?
“I shall be captured!” he thought;
Brave Shuutu fled,
Cracking his whip as he struck.
With golden forelock and fiery sash,
Well versed in Beijing,
Azizkhan's Almambet
Hastily performed his prayers
And saddled Sarala.
“The Chinese of Kakanchyn
Have reached their hour of strength;
Perhaps their cunning has prevailed.
If Kongurbay takes the notion,
For someone coming from home
He will lurk in ambush on the road;
He will seize him in a secluded place.
If the messenger falls into his hands
(The messenger who left Talas),
He will slaughter him as a sacrifice.”
His courage surpassed
That of other men in those days;
He scouted the road, reconnoitered the land,
And went out to meet arrivals.
Finding no mountain to fling himself against,
Finding no enemy to fight,
Stretching like a lion,
Azizkhan's Almambet,
Riding a horse like Sarala,
Wearing yellow leather trousers,
Rode with an inkling of danger.
That Shuutu had taken flight,
That the Fierce One, Kongurbay,
Had driven him ahead,
Almambet saw from afar
(That precious hero, fulfilled in every way,
That lion of superior birth).
Unable to turn Sarala aside,
When he saw Kongurbay,
Unable to bear standing still,
He shouted, “Almambet!”
And raised the battle-cry, “Manas!”
The swift Sarala stretched out,
And Almambet upon him
Loomed like a mighty mountain.
When he saw Almambet,
Alöökö's Fierce One
Was torn by fear within.
“Algara has gathered speed;
Before I can rein him in,
Before I can get ready,
That accursed one coming from the flank,
If he charges and thrusts, will leave me ruined.
He was born a capable lion,
He is a hero of great strength;
If he thrusts from the flank, I am undone!”
So saying, Kongurbay
Pulled the swift steed Algara
Around now.
Not facing Almambet,
Not daring to grapple,
He suddenly turned back,
Slipping away like a fox:
The Fierce One took flight.
The strong fort built in Beijing:
Kongurbay, hurrying in earnest,
Was penned against the fort now.
When the Fierce One was hemmed in,
The surpassing hero Almambet,
His shout splitting stones,
His roar splitting heads,
Made Sarala surge,
Made him spring and plunge,
Whipped him from side to side,
Spurred right and left,
His eyes blazing like fire.
“While he is hemmed in,
I shall run the Fierce One through!”
His two eyes turned to four;
As he arrived like an arrow,
The lion-born Almambet,
At Alöökö's Kongurbay
Upon Algara's back,
Was about to drive his thrust.
That fellow skilled among spearmen,
That capable fellow versed in enemy tricks,
Shouted, “Come! Come! Come!”
Raising “Come!” as his battle-cry,
The true steed Algara
He struck once across the hocks.
Bending his neck like a gun-trigger,
That hemmed-in Fierce One fellow,
The accursed man, struggling for life,
Over the high-built, mighty fort
Made him leap and fled.
Clouds gathered in the sky;
Alöökö's Fierce One,
From right before Almambet,
Escaped in plain sight.
When the Fierce One had fled,
Drawing out the drum's booming,
The tiger-hero Almambet
Lamented his unfulfilled wish,
Beat his palms together,
Grew furious and wild,
Turned his horse and came back,
And the messenger from Talas,
Shuutu, he came to meet.
“Peace be upon you,
Brave Shuutu of noble bearing!
Where the cuckoo sings in the willow,
Where the nightingale pours out its song,
Tamarisk-grown Keng-Kol and broad Talas:
Is your homeland safe and well?
Your prosperous people, the many Nogoy,
Argyn and Kyrgyz mingled:
Are your people alive and well?
With Kaynar's dark-eyed daughter,
With that very plane tree,
Is your household safe and well?
There along the Talas,
His white turban like a cauldron,
His shout like the call to prayer,
Is your mullah safe and well?
With heavy braid-ornaments and broad hips,
Is your craftswoman safe and well?
With rooster-like neck and flowing hair,
Is your maiden safe and well?
Spread broadly like a river,
Is your village safe and well?
I have no male heir behind me:
Agynay's daughter Aruuke,
Is my wife safe and well?
Kanykey, mother of the people,
Bearing her people's sorrows,
Is our sister-in-law safe and well?
The spring flowing from his river,
The little son Manas gained so painfully,
For whose feast ninety beasts were slain,
Who was named brave Semetey,
When we set out on our journey,
The Russians ate his feast and left,
Saying, ‘If Semetey lives,
