Her heart burned, her breast was embers;
Her apple-like head was in torment—
No woman was ever so afflicted.
Grieving in sorrow,
Parted together with her hero
From Akjoltoy, who had been her support,
Aichürök now remembered
That God might strike her,
That the Jediger would take her as spoil,
And, if enraged, the Jediger
Would cruelly take her life;
Only then would your pale one rest,
For they would show no mercy.
She bent her mind and formed a plan,
Thinking Kyyaz and Kanchoro
Might come within her reach.
The raging woman could not stand still.
When the enemy surrounded her,
Her ribs seemed torn apart;
Her mind broke loose from grief.
In anguish she could not stand,
Yet could not take an innocent life.
Inside that palace
Stood a grove of plane trees,
And towering at its center,
Plain as a camel ridden by a prince,
Was a golden throne.
Beneath that throne hung
Tassels made of jewels,
Installed in splendor
For the bloodthirsty lion.
Before the throne lay a great carpet,
Its border like a camel-load.
If you sat and studied its meaning,
It seemed the very image of fortune;
Its warp was yellow gold,
Its patterns countless as the palate’s fancies.
So much beaten gold was poured
Into the making of that carpet
That the bloodthirsty hero’s treasury
Was greatly diminished.
For many years it did not stir;
Its edges neither rotted nor softened,
For it was meant to lie before the throne.
Silk-edged, with golden ties,
It was a precious and magnificent carpet,
Too fine to spread for a slave;
Soaked in water for eighty years,
It still would neither fray nor rot—
The great carpet of the leopard.
The Jediger, greedy for a carpet
That no one would surrender on request,
Stretched out plundering hands to it.
They attacked the treasuries on either side,
Plundering their stores;
They split whole sable pelts in half,
Wrapped them around their calves,
And lay there plundering
Red gold and white coin.
They cut down the plane trees
Before the throne and laid them flat.
The Jediger shouldered aside
The girls and young women in the palace,
Boasting loudly of their might.
The palace’s eighty maidens,
Sixty ladies and forty brides,
Young and old alike,
They dragged off in ragged disarray
And made spoil of them all.
The red-robed, slender-waisted,
Quivering beauties
They drove all together in lamentation.
