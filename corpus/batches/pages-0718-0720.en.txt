When the forty companions rode away,
All the earth's dust
Flew billowing into the sky.
They drew alongside Jaman-Suu;
Winged steeds and racers together
Were shrouded in pitch-black dust.
Their mouths gaped like chasms,
Their bodies grew hot,
Their heads bent to the ground;
All those thoroughbred steeds,
Their bodies growing hot—
The forty nobles' forty steeds—
Their hooves thundered on the earth,
Their black-steel bits
Clashed deep in their mouths.
Tossing their forelocks skyward,
As though about to fly,
All sped onward side by side.
From the broad ground where trumpets sounded,
From the wide ravine where no bird flew,
They sent dust curling mountain-high.
Those forty mountain-sized wolves
Galloped on without a halt,
Their waists firmly girded,
For valiant, wolf-maned Manas,
They were born to bring good fortune.
Whenever foes came out to face them,
They raised an uproar.
They streamed along the bank,
Swaying shoulder to shoulder.
When they came to certain places,
Their spearheads struck together;
With their peerless racing steeds,
They wrestled at the reins.
Banners fluttered in their hands;
The sweat that ran from chosen steeds
Splashed against the stirrups.
Into a saddle-shaped pass
They bent low and rode;
Into Bötönchü's marsh
They had just made their way,
With wolf-like Bakai at their head.
The champions risked their lives without stint;
Even an early word seemed late.
To reach the warrior Alööke,
To strike him with their spears,
To fight it out to the end,
They strained their eyes with longing.
Lion Manas upon Akkula
Readied himself in wrath;
He drew the gaze of all who saw him,
His wolf-maned valor plain to see.
Along the broad river valley
The warriors arrived
At the hollow of Kum-Daryya.
They followed hard upon his trail,
Pursuing with relentless longing.
They found it was Kum-Aryk,
Known as the river Lob.
They lay hemmed in there.
The moon was dark, the day overcast;
Nothing could be caught by the eye.
Thus it flowed, dark and lowering;
They could not tell which way it lay.
When dawn began to swell,
When the first true light appeared,
The land grew faintly visible.
Your discerning elder, Khan Bakai,
Did not lose the route they had taken.
Dark willow shoots and many tamarisks—
This was the bank of Kum-Aryk.
Along Jangyryk's Jar-Kyya,
A trail for horses in single file,
The warriors made their way.
When they reached farther on,
The water divided among the shallows,
And the raised ford came into view:
Shyta-Kechüü, Tash-Kechüü,
The easy ford Alööke used.
They waded in his tracks
And crossed Kum-Aryk now.
Those who saw it had no cause for regret:
While wolf-maned Manas lived,
He brought great strife upon the Chinese.
To the headland where the mountains ended
Bakai rode,
Scouting the patrol's route.
Your valiant elder Bakai
Climbed up to keep watch.
When he looked out carefully,
Beside Böyrök and Ak-Chiy,
From the mountain called Akchyk,
In its descending hollow,
Beside the great Ak-Chiy,
That swine's tent stood pitched,
With its golden fastening cords.
He had halted for a day's rest,
The warrior Khan Alööke.
Smoke rose in billowing streams;
Alööke's countless troops
Could be seen encamped.
They slept propped on their elbows,
Sounded trumpets and rested at ease.
From a mule that had a foal
They slew her foal, leaving her alone,
“From bloodthirsty Manas the warrior,
We have escaped,” they thought.
The mighty Torghut and Manchu men
Lay sprawling without a stir,
At the thought, “An enemy may come pursuing,”
Untroubled, without a stir.
There lay Alööke;
Countless stores of gold and silver,
Taken as booty from the Burut,
Alööke had loaded up.
They were surely no ordinary horses:
The beasts he kept were his weapons.
Every one of those accursed creatures
Had hooves of cast iron.
All his equipment was complete.
That accursed Alööke
To all eighteen thousand worlds,
He seemed invincible.
No beast could catch them in pursuit;
His beasts flew like clouds.
