They had no choice but to ford it now.
Ögüz-Ashuu, Tay-Ashuu,
Kichi-Jyldyz, Chong-Jyldyz,
They traversed them all now.
Beyond lay Ak-Talaa;
They reached Ak-Talaa now.
Ah, dear Ak-Talaa,
No land their forefathers had trodden!
Its people were Torgut, many Kalmyks,
A people seven forefathers had never seen.
They reached Ak-Talaa and camped;
That day they stayed the night in Ak-Talaa.
When the dawn broke clear,
When light reached and touched the earth,
When the true dawn broke,
When the dawn swelled forth,
Stars trembled overhead,
One by one turning toward the qibla;
Stars parted from the sky,
Dimmed and veiled in haze.
The glow of dawn appeared,
Dawn's cold wind whistled,
The reed tops rustled sharply,
Young larks twittered,
The surface of its sand was scored,
The tops of its willows bent;
Thus lay Ak-Talaa.
It was a land of rolling ridges,
An exceedingly beautiful place.
It had pools and ponds and marshes,
Ducks and geese in separate flocks.
Its apples ripened and fell to mulch,
Its ripe walnuts filled the ravines,
Its poppies swayed,
Its sorrel and wild rhubarb
All ripened and swayed luxuriantly:
Thus lay Ak-Talaa.
In its midst were gushing spring waters;
Its wormwood glistened,
Its season was full of delight.
Its river valley lay along the water;
No human foot had come to this place,
Its lands lay open and free.
Its shoots were like plane trees,
Its plane trees like towers;
If you looked at its little birds,
They were like snowcocks crying in the mountains.
Its lizards were like snakes;
If you looked at its snakes,
They were like ropes nine armspans long.
Almighty God Most High
Had created living things of every kind;
Such was the land of Ak-Talaa.
Its plain lay open and free.
There was a creature called börsö;
From its belly, wobbling out,
Its young would emerge.
Animals of every kind,
Every creature no human had seen was there.
Wild human creatures and wild camels
Were there upon its hills.
It was a land lying open and free;
Its far end, they called Altay.
Inhabiting that Altay,
The Black Kalmyk and Manchu peoples
Were a teeming multitude.
With Baltay and Jakyp at their head,
Their hands bound in hardship,
Forty households out of the Kyrgyz
Were driven away to Altay.
The wretched ones driven there
Found the excellent land of Altay,
Found the Black Kalmyk people.
They wandered away from the Kyrgyz and were lost;
All the many mourned,
Parted from their people in suffering.
They were called Black Kalmyks;
They were a worthy, admirable people,
Dwelling in broad Altay,
Before their doorways
Tethering barren mares by the thousand.
Beyond were Torgut and many Kalmyks;
They had horses grown old without a bullet striking them,
Horses frisking freely.
Vast herds of all four kinds of livestock
Your mighty Kalmyks kept.
The forty households driven there,
With Jakyp and Akbaltay at their head,
Forty Kyrgyz households, the wretched people,
Hired themselves as milkers and drank milk;
Thus they sustained their lives now.
They grazed horses and rode working mounts;
That way they sustained their lives now.
From the Black Kalmyks and Manchus,
Eating congealed goat fat,
Finding a way to eat a young barren filly,
Thus they sustained their lives now.
Since their arrival among these Kalmyks,
Twelve months had now passed.
Among the forty Kyrgyz households,
The wise man Akbaltay,
The gray bull brought from the Kyrgyz—
They slaughtered that gray bull;
The many sorrows filling their hearts,
They banished those many sorrows.
Calling hoarsely like a young goose,
His white beard gleaming,
Your elder Baltay to the Kyrgyz
Spoke wise counsel with a bright face:
“Forty households come here as culprits,
Far away remain the Muslims,
The sheikhs with their courts and fine boots;
We Muslims driven into exile,
Our land is called Altay.
In Altay your Black Kalmyks
Are a very fine people.
Lads, let us cast away every sorrow;
Grieving will not find the Kyrgyz for us.
Let us dig the gold mines,
Let us hollow out the earth with hoes,
