Great Zholoy, cursed by the spirits,
Sprang like a hungry tiger.
The horse he rode was Achbuudan,
High-backed, with a low saddle,
Unwearied by six months of riding,
Short in forelegs, long in hind legs,
The mount of raging Zholoy.
Taking seven thousand soldiers,
Zholoy rode in a frenzy;
Into the desert of Kürpüldök
The army's vanguard entered.
At the spur where the mountain ended,
At the blunt, projecting headland,
Zholoy arrived in his frenzy.
He turned Achbuudan's head now;
With piercing cries and hollow cheers
He stood and shouted now.
He bellowed at the Manchus,
He summoned the champions.
Their arm guards spreading broad,
All their khans came forth.
Mountain-sized warrior Zholoy
Began to speak his mind.
“Had you heard of this?
My khan, you stand here;
Ezkara, khan of the Manchus,
You too stand here!
Giant Döödür and Karazhoy,
You also stand here!
Gray rolling hills and dark ridges
Are no mountains to cross unscouted.
Leopard Manas, bloodthirsty lion,
Is no ordinary mother's son,
No slave who will let us prosper!
Let us scout his road with care,
Let us measure out his land.
Those with nets of iron rope,
Those all armed with daggers,
Footmen wielding rough-hewn clubs,
Giants no horse can carry,
Those skilled in thrusting spears,
Those deft at striking with battle-axes,
The strong who show no mercy,
Those ready at the word of battle,
Those who know the enemy's devices—
The seasoned men—
Let us drive them to the front.
Those who draw their bows like rain,
Those who fire their shots like hail,
Every one of those skilled men,
Let us send along the ridges.
That daughter-damned bloodthirsty one,
That deceiver who has wrecked the world,
Let us overwhelm him together;
With that grim-faced slave
Let us settle matters to the end!
These were your khan's own words:
That bloodthirsty son of Jakyp—
He ordered us to bring him bound!
The command our khan has given,
Let us hold it firmly in mind.
Fierce battle and slaughter—
Do not flee, champions—
Let us inflict them on this Burut!
Jakyp and Akbaltay,
Let us devour them, leaving neither alive!
Brave but without sense,
Caring nothing for anything,
That troublesome bloodthirsty Manas,
Let us capture him alive,
And before Esenkan
Let us lay him as a gift!
‘For this people of forty Chinese tribes,
Here is the enemy you feared,’ we shall say,
Making an open spectacle of it,
And ask for his khanship!
Those forty households in Altai,
Those daughter-damned Buruts,
Are enemies, are foes;
They have grown rich and run mad.
Have you seen that formidable hero?
He has little of an envoy's courtesy.
Those forty Burut households—
Let us plunder their possessions,
Let us crush the trouble firmly,
Let us slaughter their courageous men;
Their women and their children,
Let us take them as spoil!
Let us parch their seed like salt;
Of those daughter-damned Buruts,
Leaving not so much as a hair,
Let us tear them out by the roots.
Let us raid their countless cattle,
Let us return with their livestock as spoil.
Let us slaughter their mighty men,
Let us take their daughters captive.
Let us seize their giant Manas;
Let us scatter that Burut's
Brains if he draws near!
His oppression has gone too far;
Let us endure no more
Of Manas's humiliation!
Let us scout the road ahead;
Where might the bloodthirsty one be?
Let us learn where Manas is.
If that bloodthirsty one lies unguarded,
Let us charge into a raid!
Firing guns and drawing bows,
Thrusting spears and shouting,
Let us launch the onslaught!
Standing on either side,
Ripping lungs and cutting heads,
Let us slaughter without mercy!
Chong-Özön in the Altai mountains—
Let us trample the Chong-Özön road.
Spare yourselves nothing, champions;
Let us all descend together!
Let us find some means;
Those daughter-damned Buruts,
Let us attack and slaughter them!
Let us enter their abundant wealth,
Let us slit their scalps.
Crafty Jakyp seems an unbeliever;
Concealing the truth, deceiving the khan,
This Jakyp seems a trickster.
Not fighting squarely face to face,
He is a damned one indeed.
‘My son has become a hero,’
This Jakyp has boasted.
Instead of slaughtering the Buruts,
Why did they drive them here,
To these parts of Altai—
That people called the Buruts!
He is one who should burn;
From those daughter-damned Buruts
A mighty son, Manas, was born.
Now is the time to slaughter them.
Those champions who play the hero,
Those who have come out of Beijing,
Have you observed or have you not
That bloodthirsty one's strife,
How every village headman
He has cut down and slaughtered!
He bound those who went there—
Such was the bloodthirsty one's strife!
No mortal can bend him down and take him;
Those who challenge him cannot survive.
Of middling height and broad of shoulder,
Both champion and lion—
I was the one called Zholoy.
Ah, no such warrior is born in the world!
Wide-mouthed, with cliff-like brows,
His face like oiled wheat,
His eyes like twilight mist,
His charge is full of wrath,
He pours venom into combat.
He splits his challengers in two;
He has the look of a boar.
Chewing up those who challenge him,
He has the bearing of a lion.
He girds his waist alone.
Through countless troops that fill the earth
He enters alone and attacks!
His courage is unlike all others'.
When you see his fierce wolf's bearing,
Do not fluster and lose your heads;
Like men struck down by slaughter,
Do not all break and flee!
If we stand on either side,
Thrusting spears and striking axes,
If we fight with crashing blows,
Hurrying, scurrying, floundering—
If you panic, you shall suffer!
From that lone valiant Burut,
If you flee, you shall suffer!
I have spoken; hear me, all of you!
If you do not heed these words,
From the rough champion Zholoy
You shall take a thorough beating.
If you turn back, you shall die;
For fleeing, you shall answer to me!”
Zholoy spoke these words now.
“So let it be for anyone who flees,”
The countless charging soldiers
Made their pledge now:
“From that bloodthirsty bringer of trouble,
Rather than turn away and flee,
Let us die at that deceiver's hand.
Let the spearmen charge;
That Burut, his mother's only son,
Let us all assail together.
Any champion who flees—
Let us seize him in our wrath;
If he breaks the ranks,
Let us crush and kill him like a dog!
Whatever son this Burut may be,
Let not one of us fail to attack!
Let our strong men overwhelm him;
That Burut has grown mighty—
Let us clash with him!
“Champion Zholoy, brave warrior,” they said,
“What you have spoken is wise,” they said.
Gathering and supporting him,
Their idols hammered out of bronze,
Those clattering idols,
They struck against their chests:
“May this idol strike us down!” they said.
They listened to the mighty command,
They stood together in a crowd.
They heard great Zholoy's words,
They deemed them right.
They divided off the champions;
Their courageous and their strong
They placed at the very front.
Their great boots jutting out,
The dense army launched its charge,
Looming like Mount Opol.
Their saddle flaps fluttered;
Black hair thick as a hand-mallet
Swung upon their backs.
The Chinese rose in a clamorous frenzy;
As though they would overwhelm all,
The emperor's people shouted,
Thundering in their wrath.
From the champions' fury
The earth broke into a rumble.
Their champions were extraordinary,
Their heroes were many;
They had strong men and giants,
Their valiant men had gathered might.
Leaders of ten wheeled about;
Seven standards became their banners,
Their uproar filled the earth.
Axemen stood at the front,
Their heroes on the flanks,
Their mighty men on the flanks,
Swordsmen at the front,
Thick-armed and full of courage.
Their war cries deafened ears;
Their roaring split the rocks!
Dust billowed as they marched;
Esenkan's many soldiers
Shouted out their songs.
An army of countless kinds—
May God spare us the sight—
More than a man could bear to see,
They surged in all their strength.
Warrior Zholoy at the front,
Ezkara at the rear,
They hemmed the army in, pounding along.
Dividing into troops of a thousand,
They came swarming forth.
Giant Döödür and Karazhoy
Came driving them forward.
The troops they drove that day—
You could not tell them from insects—
Came swarming forth.
Their spears appeared like reeds;
Their courageous and their strong
Were singled out to go ahead.
Like tumbleweed in flight,
Too swift to catch the eye,
Dust rising in a black fog,
They came swarming forth.
“Ride! Quickly! Go, go!” he cried;
At the army's rear, Ezkara,
Beating them with his battle-axe,
Came driving them forward.
An army of a thousand multitudes,
Great Zholoy at its head,
They skirted the Altai mountains,
Of the gray, rolling, gullied hills,
They skirted the lower slopes,
To the strange mountain's spur,
To its projecting, jutting headland.
The army overran Keng-Özön,
An immense host, no small band,
Skirting Kyyan-Kyr.
Their coursers, mules and rhinoceroses,
Every one of them they had
Shod and made ready.
Spear tips flashed,
Brass horns rang out,
Their strong men thundered,
Their champions pounded along,
As though to slaughter the world.
The army marched, the host swarmed,
As though not merely warrior Manas,
But the whole throng of Buruts,
Their people, they would bind.
Great Döödür on his great mule,
The oldest of the physicians,
He drove along that day,
All those clanging, jingling men.
In flapping, flouncing, broad robes,
The Chinese people then
Buzzed together in a separate throng.
There were Black Kalmyks and Manchus;
May Kydyr and God protect us—
So many souls had swept upon us!
There were khans who guarded thrones;
Those who slashed whomever they seized,
All the blood-drinkers were there.
Taking their command and raging,
Hero Manas, the wolf-man,
To bind him securely,
They were fiercely determined.
Careful over terrain, knowing the roads,
Their accomplished champion Zholoy
The narrow paths of gray, rolling hills
He had trodden thoroughly.
“The Buruts must be here,” he said.
Warrior Zholoy, born resourceful,
Had taken up the lead!
Keeping a home beneath gray yurt felt,
Was it not Zholoy who had grown up so?
Scouting, ranging over country,
Was it not Zholoy who was used to it?
Rich broth and fatty meat,
Was it not Zholoy who had eaten them?
“Where is there any trouble for me!”
Was it not Zholoy who said it?
On green, mottled meadow turf,
Was it not Zholoy who had grown up?
Migrating and making camp since the beginning,
Was it not Zholoy who was used to it!
Knowing the land, a great traveler,
He had gone and settled in Beijing.
Because he knew the roads, that swine
Had gained the name of Zholoy.
If you see Zholoy, do not approach;
That great glutton has ways of his own.
Countless fescues, eighty grasses—
Following tracks through yellowed land,
Countless troops swept over the earth,
Coming to hem them in.
Forty Kyrgyz households, wealthy Jakyp—
The tracks they left, warrior Zholoy
Came pursuing.
Jagged and uneven to the eye,
On this side of Altai,
A gaping mountain gorge—
Their grief was great, their plight was hard;
The forty migrating Kyrgyz households
Voiced many a lament!
Yesterday, when young Manas, Khan Jakyp's son,
Had done what he had done,
When Döngö and warrior Kochku
He had cut down and slaughtered together,
When Giant Zhoon and Giant Kaman
He had slaughtered on the pass,
When Manas struck the Kalmyks,
When Akbaltay found a plan,
God's sun clouded in the sky;
Sixty cares came upon him at once.
Altai was no cherished homeland,
The Black Kalmyks were no kindred people.
Akbaltay had pondered,
Riding a stallion along the water,
Thinking deeply of a better plan:
To scatter their countless cattle,
Akbaltay had thought,
And flee toward the Kyrgyz!
His son Manas, poison had arisen;
Uproar arose from broad Altai.
Manas, born in Altai,
Had thrown the world into commotion.
“He slaughtered those who came,”
The khan of the unbelievers—
Khan Esen—had heard it.
From those who went and those who came
He had heard and weighed his valor.
“He will bring strife to Beijing,”
Warrior Esenkan then
Stood out of his wits.
“He will torment the governors-general;
The governors will be ruined,”
Esenkan had understood.
“We are forty Kyrgyz households;
Let us not suffer needlessly,”
Wealthy Jakyp, that great schemer,
Had been deeply troubled.
When Akbaltay devised his plan,
He gelded stallions into riding horses,
To migrate away from Altai,
He swiftly settled on this plan.
He castrated rams into wethers,
He fattened all his sheep.
He gelded breeding camels into pack beasts;
The Black Kalmyks in Altai—
He raided their horses and left them afoot.
He castrated bulls into cattle,
Set the few households in commotion,
Extinguished the fires they had lit.
Wealthy Jakyp was a wise man;
His Kyrgyz of few households,
He moved them all together.
Their shitty bundles of belongings,
He would not keep, but scattered all.
Pasture after pasture, as in a game,
They fled, migrating as though at play.
Hurting every heart,
Galling every pack beast,
Stringing all the livestock out,
Making all the children cry,
Without rest throughout the day,
Without sleep throughout the night,
Thus they made their migration.
How could the valiant spare themselves?
They moved without looking back.
Manas had gone to Koshoy;
Some time had passed since he left.
For Zholoy to send his army,
To plunder them without restraint,
Only three days' road remained.
The fortunate one was Kutubiy;
Kutubiy was the name given him.
The people trusted his words.
He knew letters from opening the Quran,
He knew God's name by heart.
The bravest of the forty boys,
To the magnanimous one touched by Kydyr
His bearing seemed an equal.
He girded white cloth about his waist;
When uproar split the sky,
He entered alone and attacked.
Great in courage, fierce in wrath,
He laid his challengers flat.
When Manas left for Koshoy,
Clasping his hand tightly,
Akbaltay and Jakyp—
That bloodthirsty one had gone,
Entrusting them to Kutubiy.
At that time Kutubiy,
Alarmed, recited his prayers,
And saddled Telkyzyl.
To the winding mountain's spur
He rode to keep a lookout,
Upon the jutting headland.
When he climbed and kept his watch,
Wishing his people safe,
Gray haze, burning dust,
A host as dense as ants—
There was a great trench dug.
Zholoy, the Kalmyk hero—
As though slaughter had struck,
The swarming unbelievers
Had all swept forward.
Their mighty men in jackets
Had advanced with all their strength.
No mortal had beaten him face to face,
No one had bent him down and taken him,
No one who met him had survived;
His fame was precious, his name weighty.
Appearing when China faced ruin,
A trick rider, swift on foot,
His passage a clamorous upheaval,
Karazhoy of the treasury
Had raised a mighty tumult.
Mustered from the governors downward,
They were sweeping forward.
Look at the reeds, look at the flood,
Shouting “Emperor!” as their battle cry,
Look at the Chinese people sweeping on!
Look at the grass upon the earth;
Look at Chinese countless as the grass.
Look at the trench they have dug;
Look at the misery they have brewed!
A white standard comes,
A shouting uproar comes;
The sight weighs heavily upon the soul.
When he looked toward the front,
His arm guards spreading broad,
Mighty great Zholoy came.
When he looked to this side,
Ezkara of the Manchus—
They had all swept forward.
The drums made for battle
Were beaten hard and thundered.
Wearing dark Kalmyk fur coats,
The many seething lords
From a spur of broad Altai
Came driving onward.
Indistinguishable from insects,
They came swarming forth.
From the ridge at Tash-Moynok
Their multitudes came forth;
From the rugged, hilly headland
Half of them came forth.
Black summit, black cliff—
They came boiling out.
One could not tell them apart;
The clamoring unbelievers
Came swarming forth.
“When Akbaltay has grown old,
When wealthy Jakyp has aged,
Will this army overwhelm them?
Valiant Manas is absent too.
Will they make sport of our small people
And slaughter them all?!
Upon this unwary people
Will they descend from above?
Will they scatter their four kinds of livestock?
Seen just now, now gone—
Such is this treacherous world.
Renowned Manas is absent too!
How can I stay and watch?
To my forty Kyrgyz households
Let me tell this grievous news!
Forty boys from forty homes,
Let me gather them as my support;
Let me fight without delay.
‘Flee, scatter!’ to my people
Let me go and give the warning;
Then turning back to fight,
Let me return and launch an attack!”
So Kutubiy said then.
Telkyzyl beneath him,
A true courser and battle-steed,
Had appeared from the unseen.
From Kulan-Talaa's sandy desert—
There was valiant, hapless Manas;
He and Kutubiy stood together,
And while they stood embracing,
Telkyzyl had come trotting up.
At full-grown age, that noble creature,
As a Buraq-like horse for Kutu,
His swift steed had thus arrived.
Beneath him, the courser Telkyzyl,
Had appeared from the unseen,
Had been found on the open plain.
Racing with wild asses,
Whatever else, that noble creature
Was kin to the wondrous coursers,
Telkyzyl, a courser true.
Kutubiy had seen the enemy.
Whipping this way and that,
Spurring right and left,
To his elder, old Baltay,
To Akbaltay, Jakyp, all of them,
To the forty households themselves—
Whoever heard cried out
At Kutubiy's words.
“Your father's grave, forty households!
To your old Jakyp, blessed by Kydyr,
I have come to bring the news,
To all you forty Kyrgyz households:
We have encountered inescapable
Wrath from the one God!
There is no forest for shelter,
No Kyrgyz to flee among;
Grief within me, fire without!
To grapple and wage battle,
To stand as our support,
He was a lion born a leopard,
Truly created by God,
A warrior wolf of battle!
From the people of Kakanchyn,
From the mighty-armed unbelievers,
From the great warrior Zholoy,
To rescue us,
To grapple and give battle,
Our leopard, wolf-man Manas is absent.
Would God strike us down like this?!
Before Manas returns,
Will the Black Chinese drive us out?!
Have you seen what I have seen?
Have you learned what I have learned?
Look at the dust of flying earth,
Look at the mass of Chinese souls!
Damn this buried world!
Never had so many swept upon us;
If the Manchu host attacks,
If they fall upon us, they will spare no soul!
A deep pit none can escape—
The dense host has swept upon us,
Swarming like ants,
Buzzing as they move to surround us.
Their army divided into armies;
Their oppression looked unlike any other.
If we trade blows in battle,
Blood has poured out like water!
Khan Zholoy of the Kalmyks,
Their khan has swept upon us;
Ezkara of the Manchus
Has swept upon us too.
Karazhoy of the treasury
Has swept upon us with them.
Great Döödür, who lifts a camel,
He too has swept upon us!
Earth flies into the sky;
The army crowds upon us.
Our means are gone; we are dead, ah!
Your father's grave—our people are far away!
If we flee and hide upon the pass,
There is no dark forest thicket;
If we grapple and fight,
The one we counted on for support,
Our leopard, lion Manas, is absent too!
Forty households into one hollow—
We have migrated to gather here;
To be wiped out, every one of us,
We have migrated to be slaughtered!
The line that God has drawn,
We have migrated to behold it.
At the hands of clamoring Kalmyks,
All of us in sorrow,
We have migrated here to die!”
Thus Kutubiy spoke.
Despairing of his hapless life,
He began with sorrowful words.
Wealthy Jakyp, of livestock and of life,
Gave up all hope.
“Oh, my child, did I not tell you before?
You heard it then!
Can we have the strength to fight?
Can mortal men defeat them in combat?!
That swine beyond the reach of the Flood—
Can the world find strength enough?
Alas, Baltay, you are doomed!
You spoke a thousand idle words,
You will not stop calling for Manas!
Mad Baltay, wait and see!
Have you taken gold as plunder?
Take heed, Baltay, of my words:
Have you now fallen into misery?!
Hell-bound Baltay, may you perish!
Nine wolves and a single sheep—
Would they leave its carcass if they tore it?!
The surging Chinese, upon us—
If they struck, would they leave our carcasses?!
You have no empty land in Zheti-Özön;
You rave, crafty Baltay.
Dwelling in Zheti-Özön,
You have no mighty Kyrgyz people!
Did you not see, Baltay,
The sport the Chinese made of us,
When my father Karakan died,
The havoc wrought by the Kalmyks!
They shattered our upright people;
Without a crime or feud,
The Chinese slaughtered them.
Much gold coin and treasure
They took away as booty!
Karakan's eight sons,
They drove us to destruction.
One of us went to Eren;
One of us went then
Into depths from which none return!
One of us went to Kangay;
We came to Altai!
Altai was our homeland;
These Black Kalmyks, these Manchus,
Though unbelievers, were our people!
Urging Manas on, you, Baltay—
Have you seen this dreadful calamity?!
We have trodden
Upon a dragon's tail!
Steel doors and gates—
His lineage is of mighty khans,
If you look upon their sovereign!
There are Kakanchyn's people;
Never freeing those who challenge them,
They have the custom of drinking their blood!
Could those who challenged them survive?
Could they return alive to their people?
To Manas who has wandered off,
How shall we send the news?
Having let the Chinese slaughter us,
How can we tell him without shame?
To Manas who has gone wandering,
How can we reach him if we spring forth?
By those bristling Black Kalmyks
We shall all be slaughtered!
To Manas who has gone alone,
How can our cries reach him?
Under the Chinese decree,
We shall go to our deaths!
Yesterday, while we lay in broad Altai,
‘My son has become unruly,’ I said;
Your wishes have burned us!
Into your mare's-milk churn,
Daughter-damned Akbaltay—
We have wonderfully brought about my ruin!
Against the deeply rooted Manchus,
You will not heed ‘Do not destroy yourself!’
Doomed Baltay, you will never prosper!
You turned aside for booty;
You will not leave your mischief!”
Jerking and shaking himself,
Akbaltay sprang up in fury:
“At the most, I shall die!
From the swarming Chinese
I shall see the Day of Judgment!
In this world, living without dying,
Is there anyone with an iron core?
Alas, Jakyp, what are you saying?
Can one who fears death prosper?!
We were driven here from Altai;
Our hands were bound to toil!
From the support you trusted,
We have come severed, Jakyp;
From your broad Ala-Too land,
We have come parted like this,
From your vast Kyrgyz people!
Do not grieve, Jakyp, do not grieve!
Manas is no ordinary boy,
By the blustering Kalmyks,
He is no soul to be trampled.
If the Chinese thunder upon us,
How can I surrender my days?
Esenkan has grown mighty—
Let me clash with him!
While my life is still whole,
Let all the Chinese sweep upon us;
How can I lie down and yield?
With the swarming Chinese unbelievers,
Let me join in the struggle!
If bloodthirsty Manas lives,
Kakanchyn is the one I shall grapple with,
Bakburchun the one I shall bind!
While I am still whole,
I shall fight to the finish.

How could I look upon the world!
From the support I trusted,
Binding my hands securely,
They drove me from my people.
In Lungta, Tungsha and Beijing,
So much has my migration suffered;
When I see it fully, my heart burns.
I have seven forefathers to avenge!
Their battle-axes, swords and clubs,
How could I fail to face them?
Before this dear head enters the earth,
Against Black Chinese and Manchus,
How could I fail to fight to death!
Grasping a spear, I shall break their ranks;
If leopard Manas appears,
Akbaltay, though grown old,
Will fall to a shot if fate decrees.
To the bellowing Kalmyks,
How can I yield my authority?
Turning back without death or loss,
How could I look upon his face?
To those arrogant unbelievers,
How can I yield my equal standing?
Now they have surrounded us,
Let me spur forward against them!
Does not a mortal servant die?
What God lays upon him,
Does not the suffering servant bear it!
Once God has given the command,
Must he not return the life entrusted him?
Does not one whose day is done depart?
When his flower falls, does he not fade!
I have my flower, I have my strength;
Let the Manchus thunder upon us—
I have the spirit to fight to death!
The governors' warrior khan,
I shall face him in combat.
If mighty God wills it,
Let me meet my death!
If Manas's shape appears,
Let me make him my support,
If mighty God wills,
Let me grapple with them!
If my appointed end comes, I shall die!
Their people swarm like black insects;
Our poor forty Kyrgyz households,
Those daughter-damned Chinese
Have the strength to shatter.
They have lords and they have khans;
To crush and plunder us,
They have firmly pledged themselves.
To descend upon our heads,
To seize and crush us like dogs,
Their khan has lost all justice;
The Black Chinese and many Manchus
Have swollen fiercely with wrath.
We were forty Kyrgyz households;
We had come as exiles.
Attacking an exiled people—
Have you seen their wickedness?
Their Torguts gathered, every one,
Demanding, ‘Give us your son!’—
Have you seen how they will not relent?
Mighty God's decree
Has come before our brows; let us face it.
To the seething Chinese,
Our support, that dear boy Manas,
How can we seize and hand him over?
The many Manchus have grown mighty;
Let us thunder against them!
Standing on either side,
Fighting harder than ever,
Filling the valleys with corpses,
Making slaughter beyond imagining,
Facing them and drawing bows,
Sparing not our precious lives,
Striking with our spears,
Finding measureless means,
Without rest by day or night,
Fighting without mercy—
While this wise bald head remains,
Valiant Manas, the lone only son,
How can we hand him over now!?
The clamorous Chinese people,
Let us meet them face to face!
That unbeliever, Keder's son,
Grim-faced warrior Zholoy,
Will impose a heavy tribute;
How can we submit to tribute?
We shall keep on smashing them!
Manas, bestowed upon our fortune,
Unless our people die in slaughter,
How can we give him to the mighty?!
Those shambling Chinese,
How can we endure their oppression?
Against the Kalmyks who come to humiliate us—
Alas, what a wretched man you are—
Let us trade blows!
Kakanchyn has grown mighty;
Let us also muster our strength.
Against wrathful champion Zholoy,
Let us grapple and spill blood.
Manas, whom we count on for support,
Because their khan has come to demand him,
How can we hand him to the Kalmyks!
Standing face to face,
We were among those who died long ago;
Let us go out to meet them in battle,
Let us take delight in hell.
Parted from our countless people,
With the surging Black Kalmyks
Let us clash!
The Chinese are multitudes, we are alone;
We forty Kyrgyz households are dead.
From these Black Kalmyks
We suffered oppression long ago.
Driven away as a spectacle,
We came to Altai long ago.
At that time, we forty households
Were slaughtered and died!
Against the looming Kalmyks,
Let me stand and fight.
My support, wolf-man Manas,
How can I give him to the Kalmyks!
Dear Baltay, wealthy Jakyp—
Spending all my countless cattle,
With Black Chinese and Manchus
Let me measure my strength!
They have their lord Esenkan,
Who has sent so vast a host.
If we look from end to end,
He means to crush us utterly.
He means to block my road;
If I fall into his net, this unbeliever
Means to bind my hands,
Means to show my eyes
Wonders no man can bear to see,
He has meant to dig my pit,
He has meant to brew my misery;
He has meant to bind, indeed,
The cherished jewel upon my brow,
That wondrous boy Manas.
While Akbaltay still lives,
How can I give up my Manas?
This boar has grown mighty—
Let me clash with him!”
Saying this, Baltay could not stay still:
“If I die, there will be great regret;
Ripping lungs and cutting heads,
Let me wage a mighty battle!
My lion Manas, blessed by Kydyr,
Having seized and given him to the Chinese,
How could I face the Kyrgyz?
These Kalmyks are eager for it;
Let me fight them to the death!
While my beard is white as a swan,
While my departure is near,
With the countless Kalmyk people
Let me measure my strength!
For fortunate son Manas,
Until we are utterly slaughtered,
Let me keep on smashing them!
Manas is no ordinary son,
Putting us out of his mind,
He is no slave to wander at play!
Manas is no worthless son,
Putting us out of his heart,
He is no slave to wander without care!
Born greater than other mortals,
This Manas is no worthless one!
While that destroyer lives,
He is no son to forget us!
If mighty Manas lives,
He will make my noble head a khan's;
Of those roaring Kalmyks,
He will lay twelve thousand low!
Those haughty Chinese and Manchus,
How can I submit to their words?
To those swaggering Chinese and Manchus,
Without fighting them myself,
How can I stand and yield?
While I have strength and power,
Let me die fighting them!
Grasping a spear, I shall break the ranks;
If death comes, I shall fall to a shot!
What the Maker has written,
How can I leave without seeing it?
Against the swarming Black Kalmyks,
How can I leave without dying!”
Saying this, Akbaltay,
Having heard Kutubiy's words,
The Black Chinese and Manchus—
Having heard they would surround them,
Readied spear and sword,
Fastened his club crosswise,
Alarmed by the many Chinese,
He saddled Kulager:
“His father Jakyp is senseless,
An ill-intentioned soul,
A creature the One has made!
Much livestock, but no sense!
Your countless cattle that you number—
Let them be sacrificed for Manas alone!”
Thus Akbaltay spoke,
Raging and gathering his force,
Addressing Khan Jakyp:
“When your father Karakan died,
You came an exile to Altai.
Can countless cattle become your child?
All forty households that move and breathe,
Let them be sacrificed for Manas alone!
If you fear the Black Kalmyks,
Go before them and offer your livestock.
If pleading makes them spare your life,
Bind your head and offer your blood!
I shall die rather than remain alive;
While this dear head is mine,
I will not give up Khan Manas!”
Wrathful and making ready,
Fastening on his weapons,
Akbaltay sprang forth in fury,
Against the Black Kalmyks and Manchus
Preparing to grapple.
“Living on without dying,
What legacy shall I attain?
For this boy Manas,
I shall give myself in sacrifice!”
Akbaltay said these words:
“Among the seething Black Kalmyks,
May I die raising havoc!”
Thus Akbaltay,
Without looking around him,
Without sparing his life at all,
