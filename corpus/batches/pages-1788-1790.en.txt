Showing her skill,
The dark spring water flowing above
She had led through the interior.
She had a golden couch built,
And covered it with fine white cloth.
“Lest the underside rot,” she thought,
She had juniper ashes spread.
Without letting one person know,
Without letting a single soul suspect,
Without showing the six villains,
Without making it known to the people,
She tended Manas.
If anyone knew a hint of it,
Your sister-in-law deceived and killed them.
For Manas lying ill,
She carefully prepared his belongings;
The dead warrior had not the smallest regret left.
Then fierce Kanykei
Joined what had been broken,
Gathered what had been scattered,
And did not cease her tears.
Precious coins of yellow gold
She gathered for his funeral.
The Muslims of the Islamic faith
Wept in sorrow.
The reason for their weeping:
The leopard was the pillar of the faith.
When they girded themselves, that dear lion—
Was not Manas their backbone?
When they met a task in dire distress,
Without leaving them in ruin,
Was it not Manas who cared for them?
Leaving no part of the earth untouched,
Was it not Manas who ranged across it?
His wrath struck terror into souls;
At the head of the mighty people,
Was it not Manas who stood?
The Chinese upon the ridges—
Was it not Manas who slew them?
All that he did before Allah—
Was it not Manas who won His favor?
The Argyn and the Kyrgyz,
Letting no wind break through from any side,
Was it not Manas who sheltered them?
His fortune was complete;
His heart was a boundless river and lake.
He was a lion upheld by his patron saint.
Yielding to none in all the world,
The lion Manas was a hero.
A grievous sickness came upon him;
Your fierce wolf put them through such trials.
His whiskered mustache drooping,
Setting out toward Judgment Day,
Now his illness grew severe.
As Manas lay upon his pillow,
And fierce Kanykei bore the hardship alone,
She sent word to the khan.
A day of terrible trouble came
For all the people.
Word was sent
To Khan Karacha’s city.
Urging Kartkurong to his dancing stride,
Traversing many a road between,
Traveling far, sparing no horseflesh,
Taking no rest by day,
Taking no sleep at night,
Letting Kartkurong no time to graze,
Never lying on his side to sleep his fill,
Never resting upon his elbow,
Never taking even a brief nap,
To Karacha’s daughter Saikal
Khan Ajybai came.
Khan Ajybai’s arrival
The thousand men posted to watch for news
Saw from the mountainside.
To Karacha’s daughter Saikal
They sent urgent word.
When she heard this news,
Furiously from her palace
The khan of the Oigut came forth.
That scowling, dark Sart,
Ajybai, had arrived—
Your dear Saikal heard.
Hearing it, unable to hold still,
Her splendid hair as thick as a girth
She bound tight upon her crown.
Her iron shield, proof against bullets,
She pressed down upon her head.
The golden-cheeked battle-axe
That took men’s souls,
In the venom of a dragon
Tempered six separate times,
That glowed red when swung,
When it entered the fighting foe
Grew to the length of a rope—
That golden-cheeked battle-axe
She fastened snugly to her wrist.
Drawing it from its sheath with a shout,
She took her sword in hand.
Khan Saikal, Karacha’s daughter,
Struck once more by God’s affliction,
Flew into fury.
No soul could withstand her wrath;
Fire poured from her mouth.
Karacha’s daughter Saikal
Loomed like Mount Opol,
Troubling the hearts of those who saw her.
Karacha’s daughter Saikal—
Her right shoulder was broad.
Were it not for the fault of being female,
She was a warrior who could destroy the world.
Finding no mountain to hurl herself against,
Finding no enemy to fight,
Finding no stone to bite,
Finding no head to sever,
Finding no broad ridge for a camping ground,
Finding no warrior to joust with,
The standard fluttered in her hand;
The painted spear of dark steel
Flashed in her right hand.
Looming like Mount Opol,
Argynkhan’s son Ajybai,
When he saw Khan Saikal,
Was deeply troubled in his heart.
