Amid all the great festivity.
Hero Kyyaz, strutting like a cock,
Clasped his hands behind his back.
He would not leave the child alone,
Yet he was somewhat afraid
Of Aichürök’s wrath.
Uttering no words and saying nothing,
Hero Kyyaz stood thinking:
The child was his enemy,
Would take vengeance without fail,
And wage war on a vast scale.
If the orphan lived to grow,
He would take revenge and destroy him;
They would gird themselves for combat
And batter one another to pieces.
Hero Kyyaz knew this;
He knew the child would be his doom.
Burning and blazing like an ember,
He restrained himself only
For fear of frail Chürök.
That day hero Kyyaz
Simply brooded in anguish,
Then into Aichürök’s presence
Kyyaz came running.
To the moonlike woman Chürök,
Into her burden of deep torment,
To the fairy-born unfortunate woman,
Kyyaz rushed in.
Laughing with false words,
The wretch spoke with his lips alone,
Fawning falsely upon the child,
Intending to take the baby in his hands,
To have him named,
Then, while the child was still an infant,
To gouge out both his eyes,
Or, if he found the chance, slaughter him,
And remove the child
Forever from this world.
His words concealed another purpose:
Hero Kyyaz, son of Bagysh,
A bitter enemy appears
In Aichürök’s eyes.
Gathering his nobles and khans,
Hero Kyyaz stood there,
Demanding, “Give me the child.”
Hero Kyyaz continued to ask:
“My moonlike wife Chürök,
The warm heart within my breast,
This alone was my prayer
To Almighty God:
Fairy-born, unfortunate woman,
You have fulfilled my deepest hope;
My poor dear, you have found for me
The child who will brighten my brow.
The spring flowing through the hills,
The young hero who will remain after me—
Bring my son so I may see him,
Open my mouth, and kiss him!
I shall uncover his radiant brow
And press him to my breast.
Calling this child my support,
I shall banish all my sorrow.
To a white-bearded elder,
One who has lived to a great age,
I shall offer entertainment and discourse
And have him name the child!”
Saying this, hero Kyyaz
Raged in fury.
God had struck Kyyaz,
And still he demanded the child.
Before Kyyaz, greatest of the nation,
There was no way to refuse
The newborn infant;
Such is this wretched world.
The unfortunate fairy-born woman wondered,
“What will the infidel do to the child?
How will he bring about his end?”
If he presses the baby to his breast,
May God strike the infidel;
He means to slaughter the tender young one—
