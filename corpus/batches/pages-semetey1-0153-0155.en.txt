He was a khan whose conduct was evil.
Back when he brought misery upon me,
When I was left a neglected wife for six months,
Still living, still sobbing,
I passed those days in grief and tears.
That dark Sart with his curled beard,
I renounced him as my father then.
I wept beyond measure;
“I will not see his face as long as I live,”
I made that vow then.
I left his city weeping;
I renounced him as my father then.
Let me die when my time has come;
My father, who treated me so—
How could I look upon his face!
Rather than go to my father Temir Khan,
Let me wander among strangers and die.
Why should I go to Bukhara?
If I go to my father,
What wish would be fulfilled!
The looming mists part,
And a dark city comes into view.
My dearest uncle,
Let me enter royal Kokand.
Let me go and stay in royal Kokand,
And win a place there by my conduct.
Until he reaches twelve,
Let me hang an amulet about his neck;
The lonely child won through such suffering,
Let me raise him in this way.
If the people of Kokand mistreat me,
Where the kettles are gold and tea is cheap,
Where simply resting soothes the soul—
Among the cities of the Muslims,
There is no city like Samarkand.
Let me make my way toward Samarkand.
Sansyz is in Samarkand;
Let me heed his words,
And submit to what he says.
Suffering hardship and running on foot,
Semetey, whom I carry upon my back,
“The generous one’s only survivor,” I shall say,
And give him over as a spiritual disciple.
The decree that God has laid upon me—
Let me see that it is thus.
Alty-Shaar, Andijan,
And farther on, the town of Margilan—
Let me make my living in those three towns.
My father, evil-minded Temir Khan—
Why should I go to Temir Khan?
Great Bukhara, which has lost my affection—
If I go to that city,
What wish would be fulfilled?
The orphan and my mother,
Let me take them upon my shoulders.
Rather than suffer at my own people’s hands,
My beloved Khan Bakai,
Let me suffer at strangers’ hands.
Let me settle beside an enemy,
And become someone’s neighbor.”
When Kanykei had said this,
Your khan Bakai, the leopard,
Could not bear to stand and watch;
That saint of yours was overcome with grief.
“Manas, my prized goshawk, if only you had not died!
This Kanykei standing here
Will not yield and heed my words.
Humiliation such as this—
If only You would spare Your creatures!
While the leopard lived,
Unbelievers and Muslims
Knew that I was a khan.
Let alone a woman, my worth
Was known to Manas, the leopard himself.
Honored gray wolf, if only you had not died!
Your orphan, fleeing and wandering—
If only he had not come wandering to Bukhara!
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
You are bent on digging my grave.
In mourning, refusing to heed me,
Kanykei, you would set my bitter fate aboil.
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
You are ill-intentioned.
Keep acting so, and you will come to no good!
A father is a person’s sacred shrine.
If you keep dishonoring your father,
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
You will never come to good.
That precious bird, the wild duck,
Flies over its plain without turning back.
Who would abandon, Kanykei,
The child born from their own belly?
When her husband dies and she is widowed,
A woman fares better by bowing her head.
When a woman’s husband dies,
Her natal family becomes her refuge.
If you go to Bukhara, you will find a home;
Kanykei, you will be as you were before.
If you enter Kokand, they will treat you badly;
They will not leave your orphan unharmed.
If you enter Margilan, they will treat you badly;
They will not leave your only child unharmed.
While Manas, the leopard, lived,
From Alty-Shaar and Kokand,
Driving and beating the Sarts,
Six thousand otter pelts, a thousand silver ingots—
That was the tribute I took from them.
Hey, my child Kanykei,
If you enter Andijan, they will treat you badly;
They will not leave your orphan unharmed.
Back when Manas lived,
I made my home in Keng-Kol.
Take heed, Kanykei:
The Muslims ruled by twelve khans,
To Beijing, ruled by the khagan,
I drove them under escort.
That lion, Hazrat Ali,
Came through to Beijing;
When we fought and wrought slaughter,
Beijing, untouched by Chaliyar,
When we drove it off in a crashing rout,
The khagan’s people, untouched by Alexander,
When we drove them together and slaughtered them,
Until the tender stalks grew ripe,
Until the autumn gossamer flew,
Fighting hard for six months,
When we waged that fierce campaign,
Karykan, khan of the khagan’s realm,
When he stood weeping at his throne,
Fighting long without tiring,
When we made a spectacle of it,
When Akkula fell to a bullet,
When my dear Almambet died a martyr,
Entering Beijing and turning back,
The most precious of all people—
Parted from Khan Syrgak,
With our wings broken,
The one we had relied upon,
Parted from that Khan Chubak,
The khagan’s forty Chinese tribes,
When they encircled us forty-two times,
When they brought pressure down upon us,
Steeped in blood upon blood,
Then unbelievers and Muslims
Stood fighting without retreat.
Then the only hero of his nest died;
Manas’s companion in hardship, his equal, died.
May God never let us see such things!
Muzburchak and Khan Kokcho died;
All who met their fate at Beijing died;
Heroes among the spearmen died;
Many who had been driven to Beijing died.
Their teeth jutting out,
The dead horses rose in mountains;
All their women
Wore black and became widows;
All their males
Lived on as orphans.
I know Sary-Arka,
Umotoy, Kokcho’s son,
Who husked his millet, stones and all.
Umotoy, Kokcho’s son—
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
“He marched my khan father under guard,
And spilled his blood at Beijing,” he says;
That slave Umotoy has vengeance to take.
If you go to Umotoy, my little foal,
He will not let you settle anywhere in Sary-Arka.
Your only child, won through such suffering—
He will not let your Semetey prosper.
If you go to Sary-Arka,
He will not let you make Sary-Arka your home.
“This child’s father did such things,” he will say,
And treat your orphan badly.
God, You have made me howl in disgrace,
In the whiteness of my beard,
While I still have journeys to make.
Manas, take me with you, spare me this howling.
Broad trouser cuffs, an iron coat,
When I wore them,
Setting the face of the earth astir,
When I struck,
Reaching and beating down the world,
While I had all my powers,
When I held command—
Why did I not die then?!
The four corners of the round world,
Trampling them like coal, poor soul,
When I strode across them,
Punishing the face of the earth,
Those who defied me,
Seizing and smashing them,
When I crushed them—
Why did I not die then!
Now a woman will not heed my words;
Oh God, You have crumpled my days.
You have toppled my golden throne;
You have burned my garden by the river.
Manas, my mighty beast, if only you had not died!
You have broken my branch!”
King Bakai, the elder,
The saint who had seen everything,
On account of his orphan Semetey,
Because of ill-intentioned Kanykei,
His bellowing split stones;
Bakai’s shouting split heads.
He tore out his mustache and flung it away;
He tore out his beard and flung it away.
Fire poured from his eyes;
Flame came from his mouth:
“I shall take the road to the other world,” he said.
“If Kanykei will not heed me,
I, Bakai, shall die now,” he said.
“How can I remain alive?” he said.
“If you will not heed me, Kanykei,
I shall simply take my own life,” he said.
“Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
I was a morning star; let me set.
Beside the generous one gone to the other world,
Let me lie with our feet together.
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
When I speak, you will not heed my words.
Today, Kanykei,
You would knot up my life.
In this time of grief,
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
You are bent on rousing my fury!
