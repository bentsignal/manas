“Ah, Karakan, do not die!
Do not let such humiliation
Fall upon mortal people!”
All the people wept;
The aged old men wept.
“Tomorrow they will seize me,”
Wept the young girls at play.
The khan’s plundering was bitter torment.
The Kyrgyz left without their khan
Were divided by the measure of a whip.
Molto and Alöökö
Held them tightly between them,
Laid grievous suffering upon them.
From the helpless, powerless Kyrgyz
They levied a full-grown beast from every hearth.
That accursed swine
Collected everything they had.
The Argyn and the Kyrgyz—
Their burning hearths were quenched,
They were driven out together.
Karakan was dead.
There was no one to speak against them;
The Argyn and the Kyrgyz
Were divided by the measure of a whip.
The Argyn and the Kyrgyz
Were driven away, their foremost far off;
That people were worn down,
All struck down, none spared.
To give the raging unbeliever
There were no livestock left to seize,
Nor a soul left to answer back.
Scattered in turmoil and ruin,
The poor people fled without rest,
Grieving and sorrowing,
The man who found a way for his people—
They had lost Karakan;
They scattered, finding no way out.
When the khan died, the two khans
Brought their full force to bear.
The multitude, paying no heed,
Wandered far and wide.
Unable to endure the tribute,
Some wandered to Altai,
Some wandered to Khangai,
Some wandered to Urum,
Some wandered to Crimea.
The open land was left without its people;
The people wandered, left without a khan.
Karakan’s wife
Was left a widow, dressed in black.
Karakan himself left behind
Treasuries filled with precious goods.
Karakan’s eight sons
Were all alike still young.
When that Karakan died,
All were left young, still crawling,
All helpless, all bewildered,
Left young when their father died.
Those eight sons of Karakan,
Some born singly, some as twins,
Were alike in valor before the people.
All the people
“When the orphan boys grow up,
Perhaps they will be of help”—
That was their constant wish.
Their father Karakan was a khan;
Karakan had eight sons.
If they lived and grew to manhood,
They could deliver the Kyrgyz;
Such were those children.
The valiant boys were Jakyp and Ulak Khan.
Swooping down from the uplands,
They plundered the treasury
And the wealth of the multitude.
And so those children
Were left crying in the open country.
The boys matured and became men,
Equals of other heroes’ sons.
When the boys grew tall and mounted horses,
Your elder Baltay came to them.
If you ask about Akbaltay,
He was the mighty khan of the Noygut.
He had lost his Noygut people and their royal camp
To Alöökö Khan;
He too had fallen into destitution.
Horses in countless herds on the hills—
Alöökö, master of the white yurts,
A khan such as your Akbaltay—
He had trampled him down,
Had scattered all his wealth.
Finding no grave to crawl into,
Finding no place to shelter,
He was utterly at his wits’ end.
“He is a ruler’s son,” he thought,
And fled toward Jakyp.
“With Karakan’s eight sons
I will try to join forces;
If we die, I will die with them.
I will scout with them,
Take horses from Champan.
Rather than stand idle and pay tribute,
I would die fighting tooth and nail;
Against the Black Kalmyks and the Manchus,
I would die fighting.”
What else could poor Baltay do?
He took Jakyp for his support.
Their sheep shared the same fold;
Your elder Baltay, the gray-maned wolf,
Shared his meals in fellowship.
“When we scout, let us share the dust,
Jakyp, my little foal;
Let death be shared, our lives as one.
Living, let us gather our wealth together;
Dying, let us share the prayers of faith.
My son Jakyp, hear my words:
Alöökö drove my people
Out of my own land.
He chilled the breath in me,
He broke my back,
He raided my horses,
He robbed me of sleep,
He broke my back,
He slaughtered my people.
His Torguts gathered, every one;
The Black Kalmyks brought trouble on my people,
Brought them to utter ruin while they lived.
My wretched Noygut people
They slaughtered, sparing none.
They bound their champions;
Every one of their warriors
They slew, leaving none alive.
They beat all the old men,
They plundered all the girls,
They dug my pit deep.
Alöökö of the Chinese
Made my bitter fate boil over.
He took the spoils by force;
Upon my old Noygut people
He brought great devastation.
He poured out his wrath and hemmed us in.
Unable to bear his wrath,
Your elder, Khan Baltay, was worn out.
Untamed beasts no catching-pole could touch—
Alöökö, may your house burn,
