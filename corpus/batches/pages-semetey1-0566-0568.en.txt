He had given the command,
Upon Almambet's head
He had brought this horror:
“Surround the dismounted Burut
This very moment;
To Karykan of Beijing
Take him bound and alive.
Of half the realm of Beijing,
Ask for the khan's rule.
Into a dungeon forty rope-lengths deep,
Binding both his hands,
Gouging out both his eyes,
Into that pit they have dug,
Take him and cast him alive.
On the fool who fled to the Buruts,
Take your revenge this way!”
His warriors heard the order;
Swarming thick as ants,
Teeming like mosquitoes,
The Chinese closed upon him.
Their lasso-men cast their lassos,
Their spearmen thrust their spears;
Mail-clad men with iron ropes
Surrounded my Almam,
All those dagger-handed men.
When the Chinese pressed him hard,
He had no cliff where geese wheeled,
To stand as his shelter,
To break him free—
No mountain, Manas the leopard;
No Chubak, Akbaltai's son;
No Ajybai, no Bakai khan;
None of the forty heroes.
Your lion Almam lamented,
Standing there among the Chinese!
“Their horses, raided and driven off,
It seems I came to divide;
My death has come from the infidels;
With my blood spilled here,
It seems I came to die,
Humiliation no human ever sees—
It seems I came to see it.”
Giving his last wishes, reciting the creed,
Despairing of life, saying, “I am lost,”
He fought without yielding;
My leopard Almam, that dear lion,
Struggled to save his life,
Not letting the lasso-men catch him,
By mail-clad men with iron ropes
Not letting himself be ringed.
While your Alake stood thus,
Syrgak the leopard, the renowned hero,
Whose presence was unlike any other's,
His fly-like soul shriveling,
Dark tears streaming from his eyes
And rolling down his collarbone:
“The massed Chinese have engulfed him;
Let me reach my Alake,
Rather than remain alive, before him
Let me fall in sacrifice.
Let me arrive before Almam;
Parted from my Alake,
My twin lamb, my companion,
Having devoured my twin,
How could I leave without longing?
Against the red-naveled Chinese,
The infidels of the dark faith,
The head-severing warriors,
Let me come with my waist girded,
Charge alone and attack,
Till the yielding stalks ripen,
Till autumn's gossamer flies,
Let me fight hard and wreak slaughter,
And my fierce Almam the leopard—
Let me break him free!”
Kokchebich beneath him,
He whipped now right, now left,
And spurred on either side,
Roaring in fury,
Calling, “Alake, leopard!”
Into the Chinese ahead, the many warriors,
Your Syrgak charged without wavering and attacked.
Those he met were killed;
Those he met shed their blood.
Those within reach of his spear
He felled with the spear;
Those who came nearer
He struck down with his sword.
He smashed seven hundred,
Thrusting wherever he reached,
Crying, “Where is my accomplished Almam?”
His head all bloody,
His two flailing arms
Unable to rise, hanging limp.
Shouting, “Islam!”
The seething Chinese
He routed, drove and scattered.
My Almam, left on foot,
The warrior barely standing—
He reached him faster than an arrow.
Where he reached my Almam,
Showing his lion's courage,
Seizing him by the shoulder blade,
Young Syrgak, the fierce hero,
Hoisted him onto his right knee.
In a frantic haste,
Splitting the countless Chinese apart,
Dear Syrgak carried your Alake away.
He reached there faster than an arrow;
At the place he reached was Sarala.
He mounted him on Sarala,
Rekindled his extinguished fire,
Revived his dead soul,
And havoc upon Kakan
He wrought, standing without retreat.
Joining with your Alake,
How they slaughtered the Chinese—
He made him see his very life before his eyes.
My dear mother Kanykei
Had drawn it in a picture:
“Let the orphan see it when he comes;
May their service
Never be forgotten,” she said,
And drew it clearly in a picture.
On this side of it,
Their steeds with piebald heads,
Manas khan's forty nobles—
My dear mother Kanykei
Had drawn them all together.
Then the boy Semetey,
Wailing, weeping, sobbing,
As though they might come back to life,
The boy cried out in tears:
When dawn splits and breaks,
“I never became his guiding mark;
When he wreaked havoc on Kakan,
Joining with my father,
I never became his companion.”
Speaking thus, Semetey,
For the lions gone to the other world,
Recited the Quran for those warriors.
Unable to bear gazing at them,
Urging them on as though alive,
The boy made a lake of his tears.
He went farther on,
To Bek-Jailoo at the head of Besh-Tash;
There is a story to be told.
When Kanykei fled to Bukhara,
With Abyke and the slave Kobosh
Bakai fought fiercely,
Won the name “Bloody Neck,”
Bakai the blood-drinker battled on.
Bakai khan herded camels
In that time of bitter suffering.
For twelve years he fought,
In those days he shot sixty of Abyke's warriors.
Yesterday, when King Manas died,
These were the words of his last wishes:
“When the khan dies, to the old man
His camels will remain close.
Herd the camels, my uncle;
Do not camp at ease.
Now, my uncle Bakai,
Do not live as you did before.
If you go to an assembly, you will see:
If they call you 'White-beard Bakai,'
Uncle Bakai, that day you will die!
Worse than death, my khan uncle,
You will see the face of Judgment Day!
If you disobey, disgrace awaits;
Beginning with this Jelmaian,
Khan uncle, the orphan's camels are entrusted to you!”
The camels Bakai was herding
Had then reached seven hundred.
When he arrived that year,
There were seven hundred camels.
At Jar-Kyia of Jangyryk,
On the path where beasts pass singly,
That the orphan would surely come, hey,
The seven hundred camels knew, hey;
They had filled Jangyryk, hey.
All the camels bellowed, hey,
Bellowing toward Semetey, hey.
They wished to speak, hey, but had no speech;
All together they cried out, hey.
Asy taylak, Thai taylak,
They all ran, ah, swaying,
Their shaggy neck-hair parting, hey,
Their tears spilling, hey.
They surrounded him, hey, and bellowed,
As though gathered at a salt flat, hey,
All sniffing the orphan, hey, and bellowing.
While the camels stood thus, hey,
The prince's heart was torn, hey.
“My father, Prince Manas, hey, do not die!” he said,
The orphan's tears spilling, hey.
“Alas, when even his beasts are like this, hey,
How is my human life worth sparing, hey?!”
Semetey sobbed with the camels;
The prince's son, hey, your dear boy,
Stood wailing then, hey, and crying out:
“To Temir khan's city, hey,
Oh God, I wandered in disgrace;
Hey, my ocean of a father, take me with you!”
Then Semetey, weeping and wailing, hey,
Saying, “Let me die,” hey, yet unable to die,
Unable to take his own life!
Then from those dear quail-like eyes
Tears fell drop by drop, hey.
Those dear currant-like eyes, hey—
The orphan's tears streamed a whip-length down.
The boy wept, hey, and stopped;
The boy's two eyes, hey, had become embers.
Alas, while the orphan stood thus, hey,
Then the camel mother, the gray she-camel in her fifth year,
Came to the boy, hey, bellowing with him.
The camel father, hey, the gray bull,
Came forth with them then, hey, crying out.
Behind those two, hey,
Manas's broad-humped, hey, black hybrid camel,
Jelmaian, left from Manas himself,
Its front lip flapping, hey,
Its rear hump, hey, swaying,
Had a golden halter, hey, on its head,
