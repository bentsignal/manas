Its collar was gold and its sleeves copper,
With paired mail-rings and lark-eye links,
A keleме coat of broad mail,
Impenetrable by bullets when worn in battle.
Its lining was worked in silk;
The formidable Lady Kanykei
Had bitten and worked it for twelve years.
She strengthened its weave exceedingly
And made bullets glance aside from it.
Enemies who came out shooting
Collapsed when they found no way through.
While making Akolpok,
She depleted a great part
Of his father’s treasury.
Through her toil, Kanykei
Gave Akolpok as a gift:
“My only son will wear it;
He will attack the enemy without tiring.
He will say, ‘My mother was incomparable,’
And understand my worth.”
Unable to gather his senses and put on
His mother’s Akolpok,
The hunched, rash hero
Could not mount Arbuurul.
At times his mind wandered;
A red haze appeared
Before the poor youth’s clouded eyes.
His gaze wandered distractedly,
And his entire body
Hung utterly slack and would not gather itself.
The splendid hero, son of Manas,
Stood unable to catch his breath.
Rushing forward upon Algara,
God had struck the slave.
When he saw Semetey,
Karagul worked himself into a frenzy,
Thundered in fury,
And addressed Semetey:
“Kürpüldök, from bank to bank—
This is the road I myself reconnoitered;
This is the host that deceived
Your rash father Manas six times.
Know this, gray wolf Karagul:
You have come to behold it;
You have come from the Kakan
To die, without remaining alive.
I am the valiant man who drew bows
Like pouring rain against
Your rash father Manas;
I am the valiant man who inflicted
Humiliation no person had ever seen!
You have come to behold
The wrath of Karagul.
Great rash son of the Bloodthirsty One,
You have come here to die!”
Saying this, Karagul
Now gathered his courage.
A forty-fathom lasso with a black strap,
A catch-rope made of birch—
He spat upon his hand and grasped it,
Held the narrow end and swung it,
And with its thick end struck the hero,
Sending it crashing against his crown.
What else could a slave devour?
Semetey stood grieving;
A full span of scalp
Had been torn away.
Then Semetey—
Have you heard of him?—
Offspring of the blood-drinker,
Offspring of the accursed one
Who set fire to grassy land,
Manas the leopard’s own son,
Who poured blood into black water,
Who set the tussocked land ablaze,
Who cut off a head when told, “Take his cap,”
Who spilled a life when told, “Spill blood,”
Who seized the grief-stricken—
