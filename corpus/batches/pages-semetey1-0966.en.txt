Not wishing to see the shrew,
Gray-wolf-born Semetey
Wearied his horse and surveyed the land,
Let his horse run and flew his bird.
“The season is nearing spring,” he said,
“The lowland grass has grown full;
Along the shore of Ala-Köl,
All the geese and ducks
Have gathered as thickly as a beating swarm.
The caravaner has taken to the road;
The season of migrating birds has come,
And my needed journey has arrived.
The White Falcon upon my hand,
With twelve tail feathers and ten flight feathers,
Preens every one without exception
And keeps looking toward the sky.
He flutters and strains against the perch,
Rears back and flies in reverse.
The season of migrating birds has come;
He cries out and will not stay upon his perch.
Day and night he never stops,
Giving out his piercing voice,
It resounds over him with a clatter.
If I do not fly my bird at the swans today,
If I do not have him take a swan from the lake
And drink my fill of joy—
They say a swan's gall is precious,
They say that precious thing is medicine.
If I do not return after taking a swan,
If I do not see the delight
Of the falcon upon my hand,
If, in her old age, I do not give swan meat
To Kanykey,
And to aged Chyyrdy,
If I do not see the prowess
Of the falcon left to me as a gift
By my heroic father Manas...”
“Who poured blood into black water,
Born beyond counting from
The gray wolf, the male leopard—Semetey,
My morning star beneath the crescent brow,
Lord in the midst
Of the Argyn and the Kyrgyz.
This ruinous gray wolf of a son
Grew eager for the falcon.
“Let him wear these when flying the falcon,”
Said my extraordinary mother Kanykey,
“And let him know my esteem.”
She lined the inside with silk,
Had it sewn with close stitches,
Folded it like a snake's belly,
And had silk wound around its cuff.
“Let him wear it with a wide sleeve,” she said.
Kanykey made the white glove,
A glove fitted to young Semetey;
He took it and put it on his hand.
The falcon upon the perch
He promptly took upon his hand.
His personal retinue numbered twenty-five,
All of them sons of khans:
Külchoro, son of Almambet,
Was a blessed guide when sent ahead;
Kanchoro, son of Khan Chubak,
Was like a countless host when leading them.
Kalyk, son of Sarykhan—
If an enemy came out to grapple,
His light brought advantage.
Baytaylak, son of Khan Bakay,
Had not yet ridden into battle,
And did not yet know an enemy's worth.
Oh, his form in this world!
Oh, this fine son of his father!
Shayymbet, son of Jamgyrchy,
Was another spirited young man.
Külchoro, son of Almambet,
Hurriedly saddled
The weary Buurul,
Hurriedly saddled the horse,
