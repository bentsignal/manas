Straining harder as they heaved,
The fourteen tigers roared;
From their terror,
The men's beards trembled.
The fourteen tigers growled;
Azhybay's and Tümönbay's
Beards quivered strand by strand.
Chürök moved toward the palace,
Pacing at an amble.
With all recourse gone then,
Azhybay and Tümönbay
Cowered close to Aychürök.
The two had lost their senses,
Their wits gone and faces distorted.
Here and there the two men
Went along vomiting.
When they drew near
Aychürök's palace,
Her guards were beyond counting,
And her watchmen more numerous still.
Around the foot of that palace
Stood a grove of plane trees.
When they entered that palace,
Appearing like Opol Mountain,
As stately as a prince's riding camel,
Azhybay and Tümönbay
At that moment now saw
The hero Semetey.
Akun's daughter Chürök
Had an inexhaustible tale;
Without showing him to any person,
Aychürök had brought
The tiger, brave Semetey, by night
And hidden him away.
When they went in and looked,
Semetey, son of Manas,
Had a face like oiled wheat,
And eyes like twilight mist.
He was as large as half a mountain,
As though he would devour any attacker;
His right shoulder was broad,
A lion who could shatter the Oirats.
His brow was like rugged foothills;
If he caught Chynkozho and Toltoy,
He looked certain to swallow them.
His brow was high, his menace strong;
Like Khan Manas himself,
He would flatten any attacker!
Semetey, left behind
By Manas who had gone to the next world,
Was himself an orphaned bridegroom;
He had now come of age
And was seeking to take his wife.
Azhybay recognized him;
Running forward with folded hands,
He offered his greeting:
"Peace upon peace be with you,
Our splendid bridegroom!
We have kept hearing the news,
'Surely the blood-taking bridegroom lives.'
These are our words of greeting:
Do they live safe and well,
The inheritance your father left,
Your people of the six Kyrgyz tribes?
Argyn and Kyrgyz mingled together,
Are all of you safe?
And your aged Bakay,
Shield of the peoples of the world?"
