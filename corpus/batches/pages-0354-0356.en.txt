Esenkan gave his command,
Against the fierce hero Manas
He gathered calamities in abundance.
Of Beijing with its nested fortifications,
Esenkan was the ruler.
A khan of mighty wrath:
“If Manas grows to manhood,
He will bring havoc,” he thought,
And feared him greatly.
A keen judge, a commanding lion,
Esenkan, master of Beijing,
Was a formidable man, craftier than others.
His sovereignty was extraordinary,
His knowledge was considerable.
He was both ruler and khan;
This fierce Esenkan
Possessed the gift of second sight.
A judge of heroes, deeply knowing,
He was a man beyond reckoning.
When that great Esenkan
Exerted his stern resolve,
His words were never idle;
From those days to these,
Once Esenkan gave an order,
He did not have to speak twice.
Know his sovereignty by this:
Beijing's forty Chinese tribes
He held and gathered all together.
When Esenkan gave command,
The great drum was beaten hard.
He was a mighty Manchu khan;
Ezkara's forces massed together.
There were khans wearing arm guards,
All their champions were there,
Strong men dressed in jackets,
Renowned masters of the spear,
Seasoned men who knew the enemy's devices.
There were giants as great as mountains,
Titans no horse could carry;
A mortal could want for nothing—
He had people of every kind.
Much is false, and much is true;
No one has returned from seeing it.
Unless God knows it plainly,
Half is false and half is true;
Unless the Creator himself knows,
No one has come back from beside him.
He dressed him in a coat of mail,
Mounted him on Chong-Küröng,
Gave him a thousand Manchu troops,
Each worth a thousand men,
Gave him many mighty fighters,
And sent Ezkara away.
Bells rang thick and fast,
Troops surged and massed together;
The eye could not take them in.
May God spare us the sight—
Their full extent could not be known.
Swaying, stretching out along the edge,
Seven thousand mighty fighters swept forward.
The earth shook and split apart.
Those who would not flinch at a sword-stroke—
He set those hardened men apart.
Those who would not cry “Ah!” under an axe—
He set those heroes apart.
Those who aimed their shots at the foe—
He set his marksmen apart.
Skilled and cunning with the spear—
He set those seasoned men apart.
He divided them into separate groups;
Their opponents were forty households.
Displaying their hostile purpose,
Riding long-eared mules,
Wearing swords without scabbards,
Like clouds about to rain again,
Their faces turned to wrath—
He set those seasoned men apart.
Dun-colored, with dark rings on their legs—
He set the riders of gray horses apart.
So fat their rumps were hollowed, unable to trot—
He set the riders of idle horses apart.
Drawing the eye when seen,
Looking like white tents—
He set the riders of pale horses apart.
Driving troops forward in successive waves,
Driving all the troops together,
The earth split and heaved;
All his titans and giants were there.
A force of some seven thousand,
With trumpets and shawms sounding,
With loads also strapped to rhinoceroses,
Their broad arm guards jutting out,
His governors formed separate ranks.
Only God could know it all:
When seven wolves tear at one sheep,
Will they leave its carcass whole?
The multitude of Chinese covering the earth—
If they struck, would they leave a carcass whole?
Some mighty fighters held
Iron nets for catching men.
Earth flew, dust blazed;
Now misfortune was boiling over!
Long ago, in the prophet Noah's time,
When the Flood swept over this place,
When it spread across the earth,
The eighteen thousand worlds
It overran and overwhelmed,
Many sorrows dwell within the heart—
Beyond the Flood's reach,
Eight hundred households had remained.
Later, when the holy prophet Mustafa
Ascended to the Heavenly Throne,
Upon Beijing light was poured;
Though they tried to shut its gate,
It opened toward the north.
“They have much restraint, but no faith;
The eighteen thousand worlds
They would lay waste if they stirred”—
Upon this very Beijing
God shed the light of abundance.
No one journeyed there,
No one bowed it down for questioning.
From upon his golden throne,
Esenkan gave his command.
Its gate had doors of steel.
His sovereignty was extraordinary—
Such was Esenkan, if you looked.
A deep moat had been dug;
The khagan's people rose,
Leaving none they met alive,
Slaying those who opposed them.
Zholoy, khan of the unbelievers,
Strong of arm, a hero himself,
Had a head like a great cauldron,
Eyebrows like a dog lying down,
A broad, thick body, short in height,
Heavy as a mountain of black rock.
Could anyone who fought him live?
If you see Zholoy, flee far away!
Broad of face and wide of shoulder,
His strength was extraordinary,
His valor was considerable.
Given him by brave Esenkan,
He wrapped himself in the white padded armor.
