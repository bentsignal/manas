That was his horse;
When she looked this way,
From Andijan had come
A Karabair swift steed.
From the broad lower Alai had come
One with sieve-like lungs and copper forelegs.
From broad Crimea had come
One with a round neck like a wild ram’s.
From Zheti-Suu had come
One with a boar’s rump and owl-like feet.
From Chambyl-Bel had come
A splendid, fleet-footed thoroughbred.
From broad Kashgar had come
Short-necked Tuuchunak.
Other swift steeds had come,
Black-necked coursers.
Other swift steeds had come,
With sieve-like lungs and copper forelegs.
Bits of black steel—
Oh God, horses able to champ them had come.
Six days without rest, without drinking water,
Horses able to contend had come.
Broad-chested, short-maned,
Renowned steeds had come.
Handsome, great coursers
Had come, each different from the next.
When she saw those horses,
Praying to the true God,
My poor one wept aloud.
Your sister-in-law wept in distress,
Praying to the one God,
“Grant it to me,” she pleaded.
When she looked at Taitoru:
Laying his right ear back,
The crust of the dark earth,
He flung above his head—
Such was Taitoru.
His bit of black steel,
He knocked against his throat;
His tail like a strand of silk,
He lashed against his thighs—
Such was Taitoru.
Sweat parted his coat into furrows;
His eyes looked like a wild sheep’s—
Such was Taitoru.
The creature tossed his head,
Flung his forelock toward the sky,
And plunged his hooves into the earth—
Such was Taitoru.
“Alas, ill fortune was written on my brow;
When my king passed away,
A hard life lay ahead of me.
Unless my ill fortune stands in the way,
Today my Taitoru
Will lower his head to the ground;
Here in this Bukhara,
Oh God, he will take the first prize!
God will open my fortune;
The many griefs that fill my heart,
God will scatter them today!
Otherwise, if Taitoru falls behind,
Kanykei, exhausted,
The luckless woman will die of shame!
In sorrow, this woman
Will see the face of Judgment Day.
The one God is whom I pray to;
Or else my Taitoru will come out ahead!
Rivers and springs coursing through valleys,
The lonely child won through such suffering—
Today his luckless mother will see him!”
So many cares, her breast could not contain them;
As your mother spoke these words,
Her tears ran across ten plains.
“When the moon goes dark,
What will become of the night?” she said.
“If Taitoru falls behind,
Oh God, what will become of the day?” she said.
“Who would show love
To a widow like me?
Oh God, I entered in the race as an omen
My swift horse Taitoru.
If you come in front, my courser,
I shall see my orphan child.
If you fall behind, Taitoru,
I shall spill worthless blood.
My darling Taitoru,
I am a poor soul who has strayed from her village.
I have no renowned champion, no support;
To cheer you firmly onward,
I have none of my renowned Kyrgyz people.
I have no forest to flee into;
To stand beside me as support,
No Nogai of my khan’s is left.
My darling Taitoru,
Lengthen your stride!
Open your mouth like a devouring maw,
My darling courser,
Take sure steps upon the ground!
This wretched head of mine—
Do not let a bird of prey devour it,
By falling behind, Taitoru.
Here in this Bukhara,
Oh God, do not let them call me a vagrant woman!
Do not bring me shame;
Do not bind up my strength;
Do not burn my soul.
Six times, my courser,
I shall look through the spyglass.
If in all six you do not appear, my Bay,
I shall die before my appointed death.
If you fall behind, you wretched horse,
To Almighty God
I entrust you for safekeeping.”
Having spoken these words,
Lamenting, weeping, crying out,
Before the one God, the Creator,
Sobbing still more bitterly,
Her heart painfully torn,
She stood and grew quiet.
Among six hundred and forty-three steeds,
“Into your keeping,” she said of her Bay,
And stood there, having entered him.
Your sister-in-law had entered Taitoru;
To God she repented, again she repented.
Bukhara filled with dust,
And thronged with moving people.
Old Eshen of Bukhara,
With sixty wrestlers beside him,
Was the one driving the horses.
His commander of ten bobbed in the saddle;
Their poles jutted up;
Their wrestlers loomed and flailed;
Their warriors swaggered.
Raising dust along the lower road,
They came out on their way.
As though the horses would not walk,
Striking them once beyond the groin,
They drove them forward with blows.
When your sister-in-law saw old Eshen,
Her anger boiled:
“I feel like seizing this Eshen
And slaughtering him;
I feel like gouging out his eyes.
This Eshen whose way has gone well—
I feel like seizing him along the road,
Dragging him upon the road,
And tearing him apart for the dogs.
If he will not obey when I speak softly,
If he will not move when driven,
This old Eshen here,
Let me seize him on his way
And raise a mighty commotion.
If these men will not obey,
Let me seize them along the road,
Let me gouge out their eyes,
Their sixty or so wrestlers—
Let me slaughter them, sparing not one.”
Having spoken these words,
She shouted in fury.
Your sister-in-law, built like a warrior,
To the sixty wrestlers and Eshen
Called out, “Stand still!
Damn your father’s grave, old Eshen!
My good days are gone; obey me,
Listen to these words of mine.
Sixty wrestlers and old Eshen,
Come, all of you, leaving none behind;
Come, you white-bearded elder!
If you disregard my condition,
If you disobey these words of mine,
You shall see what I have in store for you;
Every last one of you is dead!
Sixty wrestlers and old Eshen,
If you drive the horses, drive them quickly;
Drive every horse hard.
When you drive on from here,
You will pass through Keng-Bulak;
The spur where the mountains end,
You will cross, Eshen, if you live.
Beyond it lies in haze
Broad Bukhara’s great Ak-Chii;
You will pass through great Ak-Chii.
When you pass through mighty Ak-Chii,
There is a ravine with thick-set flanks;
For a rider making the journey,
It is a six-day road.
When you hasten over it,
There is a ridge like a saddle’s back.
Driving onward without sparing the horses,
It is a six-day stretch.
Again, old Eshen, hear my words:
Be sure you make no mistake there.
When you reach that Ak-Chii,
There is no hill the size of a knucklebone,
No hollow the size of a saddlecloth,
No furrow touched by a plow,
No burrow dug by a marmot,
No water for even a tiny sip,
No grass to nibble at;
Gray haze and scorched sand—
Think of this, Eshen:
A wild duck flying there is dazzled;
When a black vulture flies there,
Its wings grow weary on the way.
It stretches to a point in haze,
Its corners shrouded in murk;
You will pass through it if you live.
When you go farther on,
At the spur that slopes inward,
At the overhanging headland,
When you climb onto its back,
My dear Eshen, hear my words:
That is the ridge of It-Torpu.
Across it and down the far side
Lies the desert of It-Olor.
My fine Eshen, hear my words:
Take care there.
Upon the face of the earth, not one twig
Shall I leave, Eshen.
When you go farther on,
Ridge after rugged ridge appears;
A rugged mountain pass appears.
When you go farther on,
Ridge after hilly ridge appears;
A hilly mountain pass appears.
There is no bulging mountain,
No hollow the size of a knucklebone.
Hazy as a saddle’s back,
That is the ridge of Keng-Kepe.
