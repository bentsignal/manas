Akkula led by a body length,
But when they reached the open plain,
Kartküröng led as he pleased.
Valiant Bakai and Khan Manas
Grew boundlessly excited;
Out on the wide yellow plain
The wolf-maned hero now arrived.
Such was the nature of that plain:
Not a blade of grass to nibble,
Not a drop of water anywhere.
The land was hidden, shrouded;
The face of the earth could not be seen,
As ground fog settled and swirled.
Nothing could be caught by the eye;
The earth's face lay in ground fog,
And none could tell what lay where.
The haze would not lift from the ground;
No mountain peak, even cap-sized, could be seen.
Hills and hollows lay beyond,
The plain of Ak-Özön on this side.
When they rode into it,
Shouting came from the farther side.
Listening to those shouts,
They heard the battle cry, “Manas!”
On the nearer side, more shouting;
Listening carefully,
They heard the battle cry, “Kakan!”
The twelve fearsome men who had gone ahead,
Whose valor was extraordinary,
Had outstripped the others.
Allowing no escape to either side,
They had the warrior Alööke—
Those twelve champions and valiant Shuutu—
Blocked before them.
For Kakan-Chyn's Beijing,
A gateway with doors of steel,
Alööke of the Chinese
Proved a formidable warrior.
He charged amid a great uproar,
Shattering the twelve,
And was about to overwhelm them.
Alööke, ablaze with deadly fury,
Was breaking free on every side,
Ready to make his escape.
At that very moment,
Striking Akkula once on the flank,
The lion prince
Raised the battle cry, “Manas!”
When bloodthirsty Manas arrived,
Shouts rose from the flank;
Valiant Bakai arrived as well,
Raising the battle cry, “Manas!”
Shouts rose from the sloping side;
Your wolf-maned champion Ajy,
Maneuvering upon Kartküröng,
Sent all he met crawling to the ground,
Raising the battle cry, “Manas!”
From the front, the companions
Raised their shout of “Manas!”
However great his valor,
Alööke looked on
And now had no recourse left.
They slaughtered without ceasing,
And caught him between them.
Alööke and his governor
Dismounted from their horses.
Their knees bent—
When the Chinese are defeated,
Such, it seems, is their custom—
Before Khan Manas
They bowed like brides.
Once Alööke had been captured,
That warrior Manas of yours
Began a dreadful torment.
The Manchus beside him,
His nobles and princes,
Those who did not rule the people,
He cut them all down.
He sharpened his black tongue:
“Khan, do not kill me!”
Alööke was quick-witted;
He poured out words like these:
“Well done, Manas, wolf-maned warrior!
You truly are a lion.
A peerless, blood-drinking champion,
A true warrior you are.
Upright Manas, my wolf-maned one,
Born in the broad Altai,
You were known to be a lion.
When they heard your fame,
The khans of Kakan-Chyn
Were distressed to their very core.
When they said that you were born,
My collarbone gave a jolt,
My dung-filled guts shook.
That swine, the warrior Esenkan—
Did you see his foolishness?
‘Manas was born; that wolf-maned one
I captured long ago,’ he said.
‘To a dungeon forty rope-lengths deep
I took him and threw him in,’ he said.
That ill-starred Manchu—
What can I do about the lies he told?
My bloodthirsty Manas, you have come;
I am a hare caught in your hand.
You have kept on slaughtering;
I heard no news, received no word,
Never heard, ‘Khan Manas is coming.’
For long I knew nothing of your fame.
When I held my white dwelling there,
I levied tribute on the Burut,
Taking and consuming without mercy.
From your women I took the skilled craftswomen;
As tribute I took
Your fairest, beautiful girls.
Hear this from me, Manas:
Long ago I had paid in full
The price of my life in this world.
I took aim at your champions;
I fought with serpents,
Destroyed every one of them,
And seized the city of Margalang.
My Burut, in your land
I was a khan with a white dwelling.
My strength and power then
Were like those of Solomon.
