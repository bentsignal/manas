Let us die as his sacrifice.
If we remain alive instead,
And stagger on to Bukhara,
What wish will we fulfill?
Losing the heir my khan left behind
Here along the way,
Mother, how can we go on alive?
O my beloved mother,
Give me my Aktinte dagger; I will take it,
For the orphan granted after such suffering,
Let wretched me become a sacrifice!
My beloved mother,
That precious bird, the crane,
Stretches its flight across the plain, they say.
With this great head still on my shoulders,
I walked away from Talas,
Saying, ‘He is the child my khan left behind.’
God has granted us no way onward;
My beloved mother,
Will he be left in some ravine?
It seems his strength, too, is departing,
This child the Magnanimous One left behind.
My beloved mother,
I have no strength to give him my breast;
Not a scrap of strength remains in me.
We frantic women, crying out,
Have turned aside into this valley.
Mother, was this the day we were to die?
The child that noble man left behind—
Bereft of my only one,
My beloved mother,
From going out in search of food,
Dark blood has streamed from the soles of my feet.
This narrow world has closed upon me;
Your bitch Kanykei has lost her strength.
Oh, mother, honored elder,
I have no strength when I try to speak;
Only my wasted frame remains, with no strength.
Will he reach the valley and settle there,
My beloved mother?
That dear lion gone to the other world—
Bereft of his heir,
Will it be like a fire put out?
Oh, my beloved mother,
When I cry ‘Oh,’ blood comes from my mouth;
How can I hold back my wailing?
I have no strength when I try to walk;
Exhaustion drains what little strength remains.
My beloved mother,
From the ridge where we girded ourselves,
We have come away in vain
From my blessed Kyrgyz people.
Cruel torment has fallen on this head,
Cut off from those people.
We have earned an evil name, alas,
For the only child the Magnanimous One left,
Whom we carried off and brought to death;
We have earned an evil name, alas.
In the dark valley among the gorges,
My beloved mother,
We seem to have fallen flat, alas.
Like a crumbling mountain's spur,
Mother, we seem to have collapsed, alas.
There is no sheltered bank to lie beneath,
No food to let your only one devour,
No course of action left,
My beloved mother,
No gain from this flight.
My beloved mother,
We took no force apart from the Kyrgyz,
Nor a plentiful store of travel food;
My beloved mother,
We took no force apart from the Argyn,
Nor loaded ourselves with plenty of gold.
Aikojo in his motley coat—
He was one of those we relied upon—
We took no written charm from Aikojo,
Having killed three of those scoundrels,
When we fled, we did not even
Take a single horse to ride,
Nor a coat to keep us warm.
From Bakai, who resembles a prophet,
Mother, we went away without seeking an answer.
Taken utterly by surprise,
The countless riches Manas left—
We have left them scattered.
A curse upon our womanhood!
Without thinking matters through beforehand,
Mother, we acted in such haste.
What we see today is gone tomorrow;
My beloved mother,
Such is this shitty world.
My beloved mother,
We poor souls who would not eat horse fat
Have come to long for plain water;
So this is how we are to die.”
As Kanykei stood weeping,
Chyiyrdy gave her courage:
“My beloved Kanykei,
Dawn knows what dawn will bring;
To a person still alive
God himself supplies the day's needs.
Do not weep, Kanykei;
Do not sob, my little foal.
Can grief bring compensation?
Can one prosper by calling those six scoundrels kin?
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
Manas was my noble goshawk;
My child was my hen goshawk.
Manas was my falcon;
My child was my hunting hawk.
When the falcon flies from the nest,
The hunting hawk remains in its place.
When the noble goshawk flies from the nest,
The hen goshawk remains in its place.
Do not weep, Kanykei;
Do not sob, my little foal!
Grief used to bring compensation;
Kanykei, my child, can a grieving person prosper?
Will gold come if you wail?
If you keep grieving, Kanykei,
Will one of those six scoundrels
Come and give you food?
What has snapped cannot be joined again,
My beloved Kanykei;
What has scattered cannot be gathered again.
Will a spilled bowl fill again?
Can grief bring compensation?
My beloved Kanykei,
Do not fill your belly with sorrow;
Do not burn my soul.
Do not dig my grave;
Do not bring to the boil, Kanykei,
My far-reaching bitter lot.
Do not grieve, Kanykei;
I think God will grant your wish.
The earth draws us toward a place of joy;
I think his uncle Bakai is coming.”
Walking unsteadily, Chyiyrdy
Climbed the gray hillock.
Having given the distraught woman courage,
When she climbed the gray hillock,
Her mind grew deeply troubled.
Her dear eyes, clouded with age,
She turned and wiped.
Your mother, who resembles a prophet,
Turned her head toward Talas
And gazed across the plain.
From the projecting promontory,
From the mountain's hanging spur,
From the face of the green foothill,
From the edge of Ak-Kyia,
Dust rose in surging clouds.
When she looked at the surging dust,
It was the dust of a gray steed;
The man upon its back
Was someone who looked like Bakai.
“For this grieving head,
There is a night like the Night of Power!
As we languish in flight,
There is a day when my Bakai comes!
Oh, Kanykei, get up;
What lay exposed is covered now;
Your husband's friend Bakai has appeared.
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
God is granting your wish;
Look—Bakai is coming!
Your extinguished fire is lit again;
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
Your dead soul has come to life.
Your scattered people are gathered again.
Your wasted horse has grown fat;
Oh, Kanykei, my little foal,
Your hungry belly has been filled.
The most precious of them all—
My king Bakai is coming.
The treasure of the forty companions,
My mighty one Bakai is coming.
The dark ridge where I girded myself,
My supporting ridge Bakai is coming.
The one I relied upon for strength,
My lion Bakai is coming.
A vast lake with sweeping shores,
My great river Bakai is coming.
Who pledged his hand to twelve spiritual guides,
My saint Bakai is coming.
Oh, my beloved Kanykei,
Let us make our fruitless lament;
Before his uncle,
The orphan whom we cradle—
Let us bring a smile to his face.
Let old Bakai, who sent us away,
See what he lacks and have his fill.
The banner fastened to the spear—
Let us carry out to meet him
The gift the Magnanimous One left behind.
The heir that mighty steed left—
Rise, Kanykei, lift him up!
Letting him know of my wandering,
Let us carry him out to meet him,
Making my orphan smile.”
After she had spoken thus,
Your dear mother Kanykei
Burst into piercing cries:
“Oh, my beloved mother,
With this great head still on my shoulders,
My bloody face is torn,
Swollen wounds cover my face.
My black hair, untouched by a comb,
Is spread loose, its strands grasped and pulled.
Having fled Talas and turned aside,
At thirty-two years of age,
Bereft of my mighty warrior!
Since my blood-drinking hero died,
Forty days have not yet passed;
He has not been dead for forty days.
With the Quran chanted in a flowing voice,
The poor man's fortieth-day observance is not yet past.
Disgraced, I have not yet faced the people,
With the crowd surrounding us and crying out;
At the very least,
We have not yet held the blood-drinker's funeral feast.
A year has not yet passed;
Six months have not yet elapsed.
For me to rise and bustle toward my elder,
The time to greet him has not yet come.
Mother, let me die if my hour has come;
After my blood-drinking hero passed away,
Who pledged his hand to twelve spiritual guides—
My saintly elder Bakai—
How can I look upon his face,
As though I could walk out to meet him,
