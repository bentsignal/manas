Your gray-maned wolf was then
Withstanding every calamity.
He fought without circling in thought,
Grappled without concern,
And began a violent struggle.
His precious, matchless life
He cast away, bound by a hair.
Until I save our heads
From the imperial host,
For honor and for the people
I will sacrifice my life!
How can I remain alive
While the inherited Kyrgyz people,
The treasure I held dear,
Are taken captive alive?
Thus Er Kenen spoke,
Spoke softly but firmly,
And began addressing Jeljetpes:
“Dear Jeljetpes,
Do not let the Chinese take the land;
Do not let the people be left without descendants;
Do not let them slaughter every living soul.
My first forefather had sons one by one;
After long yearning, I, Kenen,
Was blessed with twin boys.
I held a feast and slaughtered ninety beasts,
And gave them rhyming names.
They are medicine for my breast, my solace,
My two only sons, whom I begged
From God in the open steppe:
My Alymsaryk and Kulansaryk,
The light of my rising morning star.
Let them not die before a hidden foe;
Let them not see the place of no return.
I entrust my twins to you!
After fighting without rest for many days,
I will let them bind my hands.
With the twin heroes following you,
Find, Jeljetpes, a place
Where you can escape alive,
For the sake of the resolve you have made.”
Parted from them,
Thus Er Kenen spoke.
At Ak-Kaykan of At-Otpos,
In a ravine no man could tread,
Your gray-maned wolf had surpassed all
In courage and lion-heartedness.
He again held back
The infidels who surged upon him.
They stood face-to-face,
Firing guns and drawing bows,
Fighting in a bloody crush.
Arrows whirred from the bows,
And shattered wherever they struck;
Blood poured with a hiss.
Bullets flew like hail—
No trial had ever equaled this!
Through hollows, hills, and valleys,
Black blood flowed rumbling.
The battle heaved and swelled;
Dead men lay in heaps,
Dead horses rose like mountains.
Every hill was dust,
Every hollow filled with blood;
Now a vast battle raged.
Chyntengir received the news.
He mounted a dun horse huge as a mountain,
Put on a rippling deep-blue coat,
And advanced, pouring out his rage.
He hitched up his belted coat
And bore down upon Kenen,
Who rode Kertulpar.
He advanced fearsomely, raising dust,
Intent on reaching him now,
On carrying him off
And riding away unhurried.
When he drew near and faced him,
Kenen prayed, “May God aid the solitary man.
Darkening like the burning sun,
Let my noble soul depart;
Let me duel with this boastful warrior
And take my pillow beside me.”
Thus Er Kenen spoke
Against the infidel who poured out wrath,
The alien enemy among the people,
The hero Khan Chyntengir.
He turned his stallion's head
And at once leveled his spear.
Before he could reach Chyntengir,
Before he could strike him with the spear,
Before he could use his great skill
And cunning against the fearsome khan—
Do not ask what Chyntengir was like:
He had an armor-clad body,
And endless speech.
His stone-hearted people
Had strength where one equaled a thousand.
He had giants whose hunger no feast could sate.
