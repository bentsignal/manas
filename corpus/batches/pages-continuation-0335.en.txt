Speaking quietly but with weight,
He began a long and matchless speech;
Whoever heard it gave full attention,
For every precious word he spoke
Loomed like a towering mountain:
“Ebegei and Kogoi, two lions,
We are scouts who have come before you,
Envoys come to voice our grief,
Confidants who disclose secrets,
Men who have watched you from afar.
We have seen much slaughter by khans
And, heroes, have wandered without refuge.
When I lived in Kakan,
Among the forty khans of the Black Chinese,
I was no commoner, but a khan;
No open field, but a city;
No hollow, but a fortress;
No weakling, but a mighty man.
I was one of the sons who wore
A pearl-studded plume and coral stones,
One of the sons who understood
The Chinese affairs of judgment,
One of the heroes who rode the clouds,
Scattering frost as he went.
My ancestor four generations back was true Kyrgyz;
When his mother was six months pregnant,
The Chinese captured her as spoil.
With no blessing to make him a father,
With no marriage rite to make her a mother,
She was a palace maiden before the khan.
Among the palace maidens,
In the imperial garden,
In the city of Bakburchun,
My mother bore my forefather.
My forefather became a Kaldai khan;
My earlier ancestor passed away.
Thus four generations of my fathers
Lived among the Chinese as Kaldais,
And the fourth ancestor departed.
When I reached seven years of age,
My mother and father died.
I was raised by the treasury;
The diviners approved me,
Saying I would become lord of Beijing.
No living thing could match my racer.
A tribe called Kunde
Served its khan;
They were charged with raising me.
I was allotted the finest racer;
Because no wind could catch me when I galloped,
Because I proved consummately swift,
They named me Zhelzhetpes—‘Wind-Cannot-Catch.’
But lately in Chinese Kakan,
One mouth appeared beneath another,
One tongue beneath another;
Slander closed in upon my head,
My life was pressed so hard,
My strength was exhausted and spent,
And every means I had was gone.
Beaten with a cudgel by Chynteñir,
Finding neither protector nor refuge,
Heroes, I fled here in desperation
With eleven brave companions.
We are exiles, sundered from our people;
My two uncles, Ebegei and Kogoi,
We have fallen into your hands.”
When the two lions, Ebegei and Kogoi,
Heard this news,
They raised a gold-crescent banner,
And sent an uproar through the many Kyrgyz:
“God has sent us a lion!”
They pledged a horse for the ai-tuyak sacrifice
And swiftly pledged possessions of every kind.
To Kenen, rider of Kertulpar,
That lion-like champion
Who commanded the moonlit world,
They sent six fleet horses
And a letter written by Ebegei,
Bearing news to their khan
And now to all the many Kyrgyz
Whose ears could hear and eyes could see.
In the fruitful gardens of Talas,
Upon brave Kenen’s throne,
There was neither sorrow nor care.
Not even in his deepest dreams
Did he imagine the vast Chinese host would come;
There was no sign, no deed to suggest
That a furious enemy would engulf them.
The heroic steeds grazed on summer pasture,
The milking mares stood tethered;
Boots off and legs outstretched,
Kicking up their heels and tossing their caps,
The multitude lay resting.
While the people rested at ease,
Word and letter reached
The lion, brave Kenen;
Trouble swiftly struck, as you can see—
It was the letter of Ebegei and Kogoi.
