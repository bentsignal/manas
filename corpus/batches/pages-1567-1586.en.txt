Roared along like a flying bird,
His lacquered lance with its wolf-tongue blade
Flashed as though struck by moonlight.
He arrived ahead of an arrow;
As soon as brave Chubak reached him,
He struck his flank and swept past.
Kongurbay’s padded boots stretched out,
His neck craned like a rooster’s,
He twisted aside, bent low,
Both eyes staring wide,
His back bowed over.
Through six thousand Chinese, a thousand governors-general,
Kongurbay fled.
When Kalcha escaped,
Almambet saw it—
With brown eyes free of pale flecks,
A white face free of freckles,
A golden forelock, a tasseled sash,
Azizkan’s only son—
At the seething Chinese
He charged and attacked again.
When Almambet and Chubak, the paired heroes,
Fought their way through them,
Would noble Syrgak stand still?
From one side of the Chinese
He charged in, hunched low.
As they fought,
Looming dimly in jagged shapes,
Rising in murky ridges,
It was a region that yielded tea.
When their fury rose,
The seething host of Chinese
Was a flood that would engulf them.
How numerous the unbelievers were,
Almambet had seen before;
He himself knew it well.
He shouted at the top of his voice,
Calling Chubak and Syrgak:
“Come here!”
At his call the two lions,
Their whole bodies covered in blood,
Like wolves that had attacked sheep,
Licking their lips and faces,
Came up to Almambet.
“My champion Syrgak, brave Chubak,
We have met a deep-dug pit,
We have met seething misfortune.
Not one of us will return alive;
From this Beijing before us
We shall not return until we die!
The reason we cannot return—
The swelling Flood never entered here;
These Chinese fill the land.
For nine years I contested the khanship,
Slaughtering my people without cease;
Parted from my own band like this,
Having seen hell in Beijing,
I was the one who found you.
When their fury rises,
They are a flood that engulfs all;
The more they are slain, the stronger they grow—
These people of mighty Beijing.
If we match ourselves against the Chinese,
We shall never withdraw alive.
If we stay like this in Beijing,
Not one of us will return alive!
Think, you two lions:
Your heroes Muzburchak and Kökchö are absent;
To roar out and stand beside you,
Your lion, generous Manas, is absent.
If calamity falls on our heads,
There is no forest to flee into;
To stand beside you,
Your vast Muslim people are absent.
You have no means of matching the Chinese.
If we keep matching ourselves against them,
May these Chinese not meet us with slaughter,
May they not surround us!
In their sheer numbers, the Chinese people
Are a lake, a river, are they not?
May they not bind us alive,
And before Kakan Khan
Take us as a gift,
For forty girls kept ready in Beijing,
May they not take us there as breeding stallions,
May they not contrive to get our offspring!”
Dust covered all the earth;
Living bodies filled the world.
The massed Chinese rushed forward.
Seeing the state of the Chinese,
Your champion despaired of life:
“Your leopard khan Manas is absent;
Shall we be left unburied?
Think, fierce Syrgak;
Consider it, my champion Chubak!
Through heedlessness, by mischance,
Shall we be left unburied?
That towering black ridge,
That is a pass no man can cross;
Chalyar could not roam and scout there,
That is the place he left in haste.
Unable to match their strength in combat,
I was the one who fled.
Seething like ants,
Those are the Black Chinese people.
If not for his being Chinese,
Alöökö’s Kongurbay
Is more a lion than all of us.
Everyone who comes here dies—
Such is the sorrowful city of Beijing!
With the people of invincible Beijing,
How often we fought before!
Facing the Chinese without turning away,
How very often I fought!
Let us not fall short of our desire like this;
When God’s decree comes,
Without seeing the Muslim people,
Let us not die in regret!
Feel no shame, you two lions;
Let us turn our horses’ rumps to them.
If your generous Manas is there,
Let us take him for our shield.
With Manas standing beside us,
Against the host of Black Chinese
Let us fight without fleeing.
Then, against Kongurbay,
Far away, let us take our revenge!
Against the enraged Chinese,
Let us not match brute strength.
If we slip away, I know a place:
Zhangyryk’s Zhar-Kyya,
Where a single horse must pass in file,
A place that befriends a lone man.
Should we bar their way there?
Against the teeming Chinese
Perhaps we could stand and wreak slaughter.
Holding them off by cunning,
Against mountain-like Kongurbay
We could break his band and fight with lances.
If Kalcha fell into our hands,
Perhaps we could cut off his head.
Kongurbay, khan of the shaven ones—
Far away, perhaps we could take our revenge!
Fighting with cunning,
Lances in hand, breaking their ranks,
Falling to a bullet when death comes,
At the tangled mass of Chinese
Let us thrust our lances in earnest.
From hell, whose true name is precious,
Let us free our lives!
Against the swarming Manchus and Chinese,
Let us devise a special stratagem.
Their surpassing lion Kongurbay—
Let us capture him himself!
If the Muslims stand beside us,
Until the pliant stalks ripen,
Until the autumn gossamer flies,
Standing firm and fighting six months,
Let us take revenge this way!
A solitary road where no owl flies,
A great white plain just below,
Salt marsh deep to a horse’s flank—
Meeting them in battle,
That is the road where we can take revenge.
When they descend into it,
With brave Kalcha at their head,
That is the road where we can attack,
Fighting without tiring,
That is the force on which to take revenge.”
As Almambet spoke,
They judged his words sound.
As dusk fell and the sun set,
As the people tired and dew settled,
Where no plow had scored a furrow,
Where no marmot had dug a burrow,
They turned back and dashed away.
Almambet, Syrgak, brave Chubak
Urged their horses on and sped off.
The three lions raced together,
Their stirrups striking one another,
Their equally swift steeds—
They struggled against their mouths.
Every steed was outstanding,
Unlike other creatures—
Racing flying birds,
Their shortened, knotted white reins
Held tight in each man’s hands.
When dawn broke clear,
When the bright rays fell,
With cotton sash and broad boots,
Kongurbay, khan of the shaven ones,
With six thousand Chinese, a thousand governors,
His white beard turned scarlet,
Pursued them without sparing himself.
Clinging relentlessly to their tracks,
He pursued them to exhaustion.
“The Chinese, the people of forty khans,
I shall gather them all without delay;
I shall smash them in fine fashion,
I shall slaughter them as far as Mecca!
Those left trudging on foot,
I shall catch upon the road.
Every one I meet,
I shall cut off his head!”
So Kongurbay raged.
Ay Jangjung gathered his courage;
The Chinese surged, an engulfing flood,
An engulfing lake that uprooted mountains.
Almambet, Syrgak, brave Chubak,
The heroes, bathed in sweat,
To the spur where the mountain ended,
To the jutting promontory,
Reached it and took shelter against it.
They turned their steeds’ heads;
The three falcons looked back,
Standing to watch the Chinese.
Their ribs seemed to come apart;
As they stood watching the Chinese,
When they saw their vast numbers,
Their hearts were sorely troubled.
Then Almambet spoke:
“Chubak, my champion Syrgak,
Turn your horses’ heads a moment;
Noble falcons, you two lions,
Stand a moment at the place ahead!
Have you seen what I have seen?
Have you understood what I know?
Turn aside toward the spring;
Let us put our hands in the water,
Let us wash our hands for prayer;
Let us put our feet in the water,
Let us wash our feet for prayer.
Let the spirited horses graze,
And quench their thirst.
Then let us mount our horses,
Let us put on our battle dress.
If we flee to Talas,
Making people say, ‘They fled here,’
How could we go on living?
With this vast Chinese enemy
Let us fight here!
If suffering comes, let us endure it;
If death comes, let us die!
When the Flood swelled and overflowed,
When it covered the face of the earth,
Light poured down on Beijing—
These are that vast Chinese people.
Not one of their people has stayed behind;
They are pursuing us fiercely.
We shall not withdraw to Talas alive;
Not one of us will return alive.
If death comes at Chinese hands,
If we die here,
Take me by the waist and fold me over;
Do not leave my bones to the Chinese,
Load me onto Sarala!
If a peaceful day comes for you,
Hollow out a bowl-shaped patch of earth,
Lay me there with your own hands.
Recite the Quran and dedicate it to me,
Slaughter Sarala!
If my wedded wife Aruuke
Bears a girl instead of a boy,
Say, ‘Almambet, gone to the next world,
Has gone without a male heir.’
If by chance it is a boy,
Tell them to slaughter ninety beasts for his feast;
Tell them to name him brave Külchoro!
The horse he rides will be gray;
He will be a lion supported by saints;
He will be a hero five times greater than I.
He will be a burning ember;
The enemy he charges will be halted.
When he plunges among them,
His thousands upon myriads of enemies
Will perish, unable to face him.
My horses in the mountains number nine thousand,
Great warhorses and wondrous steeds together.
If by chance I die at Chinese hands,
The child who must bear my hardships is young.
Gather together the livestock I amassed;
Aruuke, Agynay’s daughter—
The one who went there together with me—
Wed her to Mazhik!
Ahead of us is a dug pit;
Behind us, drawing near,
A mighty host comes sweeping on.
Imposing Kongurbay
Has ridden out to single combat.
His exceptional hero Ay Jangjung,
His shout splitting the sky,
Has raised a war cry behind him.
The countless Chinese, not one left behind,
Leaving no part of the earth free,
Have overrun it all.
The earth cannot bear it and splits;
Their great numbers have raised such dust
That a fog has fallen!”
At the gently flowing spring,
The provisions Kanykey had given
They mixed and drank again.
They sat and conferred a little,
Their noble swift steeds—
They filled their bellies with grass.
They girded their swords at a slant;
Their threadlike lives were hard pressed.
They made ready in coats of iron,
Rejoicing at the sight of the enemy,
Clad in blue iron.
“We trust in God!” they said,
And sprang onto their horses.
The drums began to beat.
They no longer drooped as before;
They had gathered strength.
“I shall slaughter the Burut,”
Said Alöökö’s Kongurbay.
“Manas in Talas—
I shall raid his horses!
I shall seize their women and girls as booty,
And set them on horses’ rumps!
Every one of their heroes
I shall split open on the road!
Every one of their weaklings
I shall make a servant forever!
I shall trample on their heads!
I shall scatter their treasure!”
He came rumbling on,
Thundering like a storm.
When he saw Kongurbay,
Like a man seeing his in-laws,
Ulak Khan’s Syrgak,
Without knitting his brow,
Stood forth openly, delighted:
“If my death has come, let me die.
On that moon-bright yellow plain,
Let me try meeting him in combat!”
So spoke your Syrgak then.
He wheeled and surged forward,
Straining like a lion;
He would not yield to Almambet.
Holding his lance steady,
He charged with a rush.
Seeing this, the many Chinese,
To surround him,
Drew close to Syrgak.
Kalcha charged fiercely
And struck Syrgak hard.
Though he struck him, brave Syrgak
Did not stir or flinch.
Ezkara, who had survived,
Made his great brown horse prance,
Charged from the flank,
And he too came and struck once.
Ay Jangjung came thundering:
“Damn your father’s grave, Syrgak!
You wretch who has wrecked the world!
Where did you spring from?
You have broken my back;
How many of my lions you have destroyed!
You plundered the valuables, leaving none!
Do not spare your flylike lives;
Swarm over him in earnest, Chinese!”
Ay Jangjung came shouting.
Through six thousand shaven ones, a san of governors,
Ay Jangjung made his way
And thrust his lance in earnest.
The many Chinese closed around him;
Then the young lad Syrgak
Was left in their midst.
No man had strength to wrestle him;
No lion’s teeth could pierce him—
Akbaltay’s Chubak,
For the young leopard Syrgak,
To stand beside him,
Charged into the fray.
Sarala shone as though moonlit;
Making him dart like an arrow-snake,
Almambet rushed in shouting.
Without sparing his precious life,
He plunged among the countless troops.
With golden forelock and tasseled sash,
Azizkan’s fierce son
Spurred on either side,
Struck with his whip right and left,
And shouted, “Islam!”
The surging Black Chinese,
Without sparing his flylike life,
He scattered and firmly halted.
Brave Chubak braced himself;
Shouting from the flank,
Chubak charged through.
Ay Jangjung and Ezkara—
He reached the two together.
On reaching them, brave Chubak
Brought both down at once,
Knocked them flat and swept past.
After both fell from their horses,
Without sparing his flylike life,
Taking Arkesken in hand,
As he was about to reach them faster than an arrow,
To reach them and cut off their heads,
A champion named Zheldet
Arrived ahead of an arrow.
Reaching them, he set them on horses,
And both alike, safe and sound,
He led away.
As he stood covered in blood with Kögala,
Your fierce Chubak,
Without looking around him,
Alöökö’s Kongurbay,
Without sparing his life at all,
At the ocean-like champion Chubak
Charged now, that slave Kalcha.
Without sparing his own life,
Fiercely at Chubak,
As he was about to thrust his lance,
Shouting from the flank,
Almambet came charging.
Before Kalcha could flee back,
The skilled Almambet
Reached him in the blink of an eye.
Kalcha, big as a camel,
Almambet made small as a knucklebone;
From atop Algara
He knocked him flat and swept past.
Kalcha fell!
When Kongurbay fell,
The swift runners on foot made ready;
All the leopards swarmed together.
The champions whose lances could reach
Battered with their lances,
Pressed on with their battle-axes.
At that moment brave Syrgak
Spurred toward Kalcha.
His eyes strained with eagerness:
“Let me seize him by the very hem!”
Before the falcon Syrgak could reach him,
Before he could cut off his head,
Forty shaven ones behind him,
Their shouts splitting stone,
Set Kalcha on his horse,
Straightened him up and took him away.
Then the young Syrgak’s
Heart was sorely troubled:
“The Chinese are countless; we are alone.
We have no support to rely on.
Shall we die unwarned?
Our renowned lion Manas is absent!”
So spoke dear Syrgak,
And over the blood-flowing battle
He stood grieving.
After plunging through them for nine days,
Almambet emerged then,
Wounded in nine places.
After fighting for seven days,
Akbaltay’s Chubak
Was wounded in seven places.
See the blood flowing in winding streams!
See the lives fallen in heaps!
Without regard for those who died,
Without thinking, “I shall die,”
The massed Manchus and the many Chinese
Swarmed over them together.
“Capture them alive!”
Kongurbay went about shouting.
When the Chinese pressed upon them,
Hardship fell upon their heads;
They fled again.
When a horse came racing up ahead,
They fought a running skirmish.
Even then the many Chinese
Swarmed over them.
See the reeds, see the flood!
See the people sweeping over them!
When Chubak rode on ahead,
The leopard turned back—
Syrgak kept fighting.
When Syrgak sped away,
Almambet stayed to struggle.
When Almambet and Syrgak rode off,
Brave Chubak stayed behind,
Fighting without sparing his life.
Pursuing day and night,
Chubak came battering them.
The three kept forcing their way onward;
They had no support to rely on.
Bearing countless wounds,
As they fought their running skirmish,
Their champion lion Manas absent,
They fled without turning back.
Crossing pass after pass,
Traveling road after road,
The many Chinese came pursuing,
Strung out one by one,
Bellowing and clamoring.
Those left alive said,
“We shall reach Talas.”
Letting their horses have their heads,
The three champions sped on.
To the great host ahead
They came then.
For so many days, so many nights,
They had battled the Chinese;
Blood drained from their faces,
Bearing so many wounds,
Drenched in dark blood,
Their faces had grown gaunt.
Their lives were hard pressed;
Against the whole vast Chinese host
The three had fought alone.
Until the pliant stalks ripened,
Until the autumn gossamer flew,
Taking no rest by day,
Lying down to no sleep at night,
Without sparing their flylike lives,
Without thinking, “We shall die,”
The three falcons, all alone,
Had fought the Chinese.
Shouting in fury,
Gathering his courage at last,
Not letting the Muslims leave,
Your fierce champion Manas
Was standing to bar their way.
“Having ridden out from Talas
And come expressly to the Chinese,
How can I flee?” he said.
With his sword he halted the host
And stood herding them back together.
The poppies had ripened and turned to grass;
The horses had grown fat and firm.
The heroes lay idle,
Frustrated because there was no battle.
For spirited young men and swift horses
The time to be of use had come.
Almambet, Syrgak, brave Chubak,
Safe and sound, with Manas
Met and exchanged greetings.
They dismounted,
Tethered their horses crosswise,
And stood offering greetings.
Then Manas said this:
“My pennon on the red lance,
My eagle, champion Almambet,
My gift that came from China!
My twin Chubak, my splendid one,
You will never leave my mind,
A bringer of good fortune when I send you ahead,
My precious Syrgak,
My falcon when grappling with the enemy,
My countless army in a shooting fight,
All three of you my champions,
My complete good fortune,
My morning star above my head,
Have you come safe and sound,
All three of you, my lions and tigers?!
Clash with Beijing in earnest,
And throw it into turmoil!
The Chinese who cannot match you—
Make their red blood flow;
Drive them toward Deep Beijing!
Their imposing khans,
Split them open along the road!
The spirited Burut Muslims,
From the uncircumcised Chinese,
Rescue them!
Bring the end of the world
Down upon the Chinese!
If we flee without fighting,
Will the Chinese not come sweeping after us?
Saying, ‘Manas has fled,’
Pursuing the Burut,
Will the Manchus not destroy them utterly?”
He spoke his words to the heroes.
The three champions standing before him
Ate and rested,
Washed and performed their ablutions,
Bathed in clean water,
Put on clean clothes,
Laid their heads toward the direction of prayer,
Pillowed them on their white-framed saddles,
And entered sleep then.
Dawn broke clear;
Light fell on the earth.
Stars parted from the heavens,
Spilling toward the west;
Dawn faintly opened.
When the light grew clear,
Making ready for their ablutions,
They dipped their hands in the water,
Washed their hands for prayer,
Dipped their feet in the water,
Washed their feet for prayer,
And loudly called the call to prayer.
The heroes began their prayers.
Two customary, two obligatory—
The four cycles of the dawn prayer
They duly recited.
They ate their food,
Made ready each at his horse,
Pulled the rear girths tight,
Shortened their cruppers,
Adorned their lances,
Girded on their battle-axes,
And turned toward the Chinese.
The whole host could hardly hold still;
With Khan Manas at its head,
It stood watching, prepared.
They stood facing the enemy, eyes fixed,
All the Burut gathered together.
As they watched, the many Chinese
All came sweeping on,
Rolling forward from their furthest depths.
The face of the earth could not be seen;
The sky darkened with dust.
They marveled at their numbers.
Shouting, they charged in—
Kökchö and Muzburchak.
Holding his lance steady,
Brave Chubak entered with them.
All forty companions entered;
Riding out in front were
Almambet and brave Syrgak.
All the Muslims except Manas
Entered shouting,
Calling the battle cry, “Manas!”
Without looking up or down,
All the Muslims entered,
Without thinking, “I shall die.”
Imposing Kongurbay,
From among the Chinese officials with rank beads,
He separated out the best;
Of the Chinese in crimson jackets,
He separated out the strongest
And took his place before them.
Driving the Chinese from behind,
Not leaving one to lag,
Ay Jangjung moved along.
On one side, upon his great brown horse,
Having separated out the Manchus,
Ezkara came shouting.
With a single eye in their foreheads,
Knowing nothing of death,
From the Chinese called One-Eyed,
A mighty host had arrived.
What appeared dimly in the distance,
Separate behind them,
Was no haze: it was people.
Looking at their numbers,
They were no people, but a rushing flood.
The mass of Chinese
Was a river, a sea, a lake.
When the Burut swarmed forward,
Seeing this, Kongurbay,
With Ay Jangjung behind him—
The two lions joined stirrups
And rode apart for single combat.
Seeing this, Almambet
And renowned eagle, brave Syrgak,
Stared steadily at them.
Where no plow had scored a furrow,
Where no marmot had dug a burrow,
On the yellow plain smooth as a blade,
They went rushing on.
Clods from his horse’s hooves shrilled in the sky;
Kongurbay came rushing,
Heading straight toward Syrgak.
Ay Jangjung came shouting,
Heading straight toward Almambet.
With golden forelock and tasseled sash,
Azizkan’s only son,
Without sparing his flylike life,
Without thinking, “I shall die”—
But the Chinese Ay Jangjung
Was a hero born adept.
Giving the only son no time to prepare,
He charged and thrust his lance.
“The edge of his jacket,” Almambet thought,
“At the face of his golden belt!”
That rumbling Chinese warrior—
Outstripping Ay Jangjung,
Almambet thrust and swept past.
Both lances struck together,
Shattering into fragments.
Their splinters shrilled in the sky;
Clumps of white fiber, horse-head-sized,
Scattered here and there.
The massed Chinese, the many Manchus,
With Ezkara at their head,
Swarmed forward screaming.
When Syrgak drew near,
Slipping aside with Algara,
The skilled Kongurbay
Suddenly changed his course,
Wheeled toward the countless troops,
And entered without being struck.
When Syrgak pursued him in,
Directly before him the one-eyed men,
Every one of them a giant,
Foot soldiers skilled in war,
Swarmed out to bar his way.
Left alone in their midst,
He struggled for his life,
Ulak Khan’s Syrgak.
Making their center boil up,
Brave Syrgak drove them;
Scattering those around him,
Brave Syrgak pressed them back.
Sweeping away everyone he met,
Brave Syrgak slaughtered them.
Like a falcon striking jackdaws,
Whatever he pleased to the Chinese,
Brave Syrgak did.
As though it were play instead of war,
Brave Syrgak laughed!
Unable to bear it, Kongurbay
Called the Manchus and Chinese,
Shouted at the top of his voice,
And told the mighty host:
“All of you swarm together!
Seize him by the very hem!
Set upon him in earnest!”
When they heard Kalcha’s words,
All the Chinese swarmed together,
Feverishly making ready.
The one-eyed men with battle-axes
Also swarmed into the fray.
Those skilled at striking with swords
Made ready and entered from the sides.
As he stood alone in their midst,
Syrgak once again,
Seeing this from afar,
Akbaltay’s Chubak,
Argynkan’s Azhybay,
Shuutu the slave, his hat like a yurt roof-felt,
Yraman’s Yrchy Uul,
Old Kyrgyl, leader of the forty,
Boz Uul and Baychoro—
All forty companions swarmed forward.
“Against the pouring host of one-eyed men,
I shall fight in earnest;
Syrgak, left alone,
I shall rescue!” they said.
The forty companions charged in.
Chubak reached the Chinese
And threw them into uproar.
All forty companions
With the countless Chinese and Manchu troops
Became locked in the fray.
Drenched in red blood,
Fighting without turning away,
Their heroes grew weary.
The strong and the valiant,
Neither drawing back nor flinching,
Stabbed one another straight in the heart.
Many a steed was left riderless;
Many heroes lay there,
Bodies gone, heads left behind.
No one could tell it was day;
No one could tell it was night.
A black fog of dust had fallen;
The eagles went on fighting.
Without sparing their threadlike lives,
Without regard for those who died,
Wading knee-deep in blood,
As they pressed against one another,
As the Burut wreaked slaughter,
Fighting intermingled,
As they battered one another,
Seeing them from afar,
Kökchö and Muzburchak,
At the teeming Chinese,
To thrust their lances without turning away,
Taking the companions as their support,
To drive the Chinese back
And reach Beijing,
With a host of countless troops
Swept into the battle.
Muzburchak on Telkyzyl,
Brave Kökchö on Kögala,
Taking the lead of the great host,
Came making their steeds roar.
Seeing them from afar,
Kongurbay and Ezkara
Now took counsel:
“Even if their mighty host numbers thousands,
We shall prowl like lions.
Almambet, Chubak, brave Syrgak—
If it were not for those three,
The remaining mass of Burut
We would not leave alive an eye-blink!
Muzburchak and Kökchö,
Before those three mighty ones just named
Can rescue them,
Let us reach and bind them;
Let us bring torment upon their heads!”
Spurring Algara on,
Riding far out ahead,
Looming like a huge mountain,
Behind him came the champions,
All swarming together, not one left out.
Driving them forward from the rear,
Ezkara came prepared.
Before another moment passed,
He arrived ahead of an arrow.
Alöökö’s Kongurbay,
As soon as he reached Kökchö,
From atop Kögala
Knocked him flat and swept past.
When Kökchö fell from his horse,
Kongurbay’s forty nobles
Took him into their midst
And surrounded him.
From one side, Muzburchak,
His shout splitting stone,
Came charging.
The forty nobles faced him;
All forty thrust their lances together,
Swarmed over Muzburchak,
And took him into their midst.
See the bullets falling like rain!
Charging without fail,
See the troop upon troop!
Seething like sand,
See the mighty Chinese multitude!
Even then Muzburchak
Did not stir from his horse.
That Kökchö had been captured,
Muzburchak saw.
There was no way to rescue him.
Biting his finger until it split,
His two eyes blazing,
Muzburchak stood dying of anguish.
Kökchö’s Kögala
One noble had taken in tow.
All the Chinese rejoiced:
“Now we shall finish them!”
They beat their booming drums.
A thousand of the swift foot-runners
Seized Aydarkan’s Kökchö
And pulled him about like a goat in the carcass game,
