He gave Ayçürök,
And his people witnessed the giving.
He gathered exactly ninety
Khojas and mullahs.
They held the feast for twelve days,
The peoples gathering at the celebration,
And had the marriage contract solemnized as proof.
He gathered in the treasury
The many goods to be given to Chürök.
Driving sixty red pack-camels,
“Let my child Chürök live on these
Until Judgment Day,” he said.
Loading ornaments of red gold,
Driving seventy red camels,
Loading seven bales of cloth,
And in Ajybay's charge,
Exactly twenty-one red camels
He had kneel by the treasury.
For pitching at encampments,
He loaded a Kyrgyz felt yurt.
The wonder of that dwelling was this:
Its crown was gold, its ropes were copper;
The roof-cover of that dwelling
Was silk satin outside.
He let Chürök, Akun's daughter,
Make merry for thirty days,
And sing plaintively for forty-one days.
For Akun's daughter, his only girl,
With a waist like the moon in a red dress,
From among the dazzling beauties
He gave maiden attendants.
With broad rumps and wide hair ornaments,
Graceful and camel-eyed,
He gave married-woman attendants.
Extinguishing the fire she had kindled,
They moved fortunate Chürök
Toward Talas.
Upon Chürök, like the full moon,
They put garments flowered with silk,
And mounted her on a bay piebald pacer.
Mounted on a pale bay pacer, swaying,
She migrated out of Afghanistan,
Shining in a long robe.
Mounted on a gray pacer, swaying,
She migrated out of Afghanistan,
Shining in a sumptuous robe.
Like flowers growing in a mirror,
She migrated out of Afghanistan,
Like demons departing at dusk.
Arrayed like a noblewoman,
Swaying as though struck by a peri,
She left Afghanistan and traveled on.
They rode far without sparing the horses;
Whenever she looked to the joyful side,
There was entertainment all along the road.
“It is the land left by my father,” she said,
And came to settle in Talas,
In the land won by her father,
Among the people left by her father.
Ayçürök, daughter of Akun,
Whom their fathers had betrothed,
Found a homeland like broad Talas
And found a husband like Semetey.
After blood-red slaughter and empty clamor,
The time came when Semetey
Took Ayçürök,
And his soul found peace.
Semetey, son of Manas,
Could die without regret.
Like a dark, rugged mountain,
On that day no one remained
Who could cast a lasso upon the prince.
His fortune came fully into balance,
A lion supported by the spirit emerged,
And from his father, blue-maned Manas,
An even greater hero emerged.
