The forty monks standing before him
Have their chief at the watchpost.
Before Great Malgun
Lies a sacred text in Chinese.
For the unbeliever Malgun to ride,
They mount him on a gray mule.
That great donkey, swifter than other beasts—
No animal can match its running;
And Malgun upon its back,
None can overcome, however they wrestle him.
If you ask what Malgun is like,
His speech is unlike a human's.
Looking at him from the front,
He has one eye as large as a milk pail.
That is the stronghold of Outer Beijing;
The immeasurably huge Malgun
Is its one-eyed khan.
What times, what nights await us,
Leopard Syrgak, my champion?
For the two of us upon this road,
What days shall there be?
What frightens me, what I fear,
Is that unbeliever Great Malgun.
He spares none who grapple with him;
The meat of a single pig
Will never fill him at one meal.
Consider my words carefully;
Such is what your Alake says.
Beijing of Kakanchyn—
One-eyed Great Malgun
Stands guard over it there.
There, by the mottled-gray saddle and rocky ridge,
His cauldron is boiling.
There on the slope, to the great plane tree,
His mule is tethered.
When I cry the battle name ‘Almambet!’
Get to his mule!
If you reach it, my Syrgak,
Cut off the mule's head.
Listen well to what I say:
‘What has become of Alake?’
Keep your eyes upon me.
If you flee from the Chinese,
I shall know your childishness.
If what is told is true,
On the coming Day of Judgment,
I shall make you a black donkey and ride you!
If I do not tell you, whom shall I tell?
My banner fastened to my spear,
From our little handful of Kyrgyz,
Asked for expressly from Manas,
You are the gift I brought with me.
Do not hold back, Syrgak!
I shall fight Great Malgun.
Do not rush away in fright;
Do not shrink at the sight of his size
And fail to strike.
Without fear, my Syrgak,
Get there before an arrow could!”
Having heard these words,
The companion dear to his very life,
The leopard lad Syrgak
Readied himself, battle-axe in hand,
Calling upon his courage.
He rode forward, alert.
Your young tiger Syrgak,
Holding his battle-axe ready,
Licked his lips like a hungry wolf.
Never taking his eyes from Malgun,
He stared intently;
His eyes became red fire.
Almam's roaring voice
Seemed about to shatter everything.
Poised to spring toward the mule,
He waited with desperate eagerness.
Fortunate when he entered battle,
Like a whole army when he fought,
With golden forelock and fiery belt,
Azizkhan's only son
Loomed like a mountain.
He went on ahead, apart from the others.
If you looked closely at his form,
With cotton sash and broad boots,
Kongurbay, khan of the Kalmyks—
He looked like that great fierce warrior.
Jerking the soft-leather lead rope,
Surging forward with his racer,
His great boots stretched out;
He drew himself up, stiff and stately,
His neck arched like a rooster's,
His sword clanging at his waist.
As though he were the khan of the Chinese,
He rode on, stiff and proud.
When he drew near the monks,
Your Almam's appearance
Was just like Kongurbay's.
A great pipe, its bowl like a hearth fire,
He pulled from his belt.
Tobacco from his pouch
He put in, five handfuls.
Your champion's pipe
Still lacked five fingerbreadths of being full.
With Chinese fire-steel and striking stone,
He struck and set an ember alight.
Kongurbay's lacquered pipe
Gurgled when he drew upon it.
When he let out its smoke,
It twisted like a sleeve
And billowed above his head.
When they saw this,
‘It is the fierce Kongurbay!’ they said.
The forty monks at the watchpost
Stood looking, calling out.
When he came close,
He took the lacquered spear in hand,
Like the fierce Kongurbay,
His very image, stiff and stately.
A coral stone the size of a leather flask
Glittered upon his crown.
Flame poured from his mouth.
The watching monks,
Seeing him, had their attention drawn.
Among the countless Chinese people,
A khan named Kongurbay had arisen,
Whose voice could take one's life:
He roared in fury,
