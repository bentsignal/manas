He had him hang the sword beneath his clothes,
To go as envoy to Esenkan,
To obtain Burulcha,
He sent Ajybay on his way.
“To carry word to Beijing,” he said,
He who summered in the Two Kemins,
Who tethered twin black horses,
Who made a dark cave his house,
Who made the dark forest his wealth,
Who trained a harrier into a hunting hawk,
Who gathered scattered folk into a people,
Who began alone and grew in numbers,
Who began poor and grew rich,
Like sand gathered into stone,
Who became chief of the assembled people—
Hero Ürbü, Eybit Khan’s son,
Was an outstanding orator.
“Let him go with Ajybay,” they said,
And he became his close companion.
Ajybay and Ürbü together,
To go upon an embassy,
Before Esenkan,
To receive whatever answer he gave,
These two set out.
While the two were on their way,
Word reached their khan,
Before Khan Esenkan;
Confusion had descended
Upon Esenkan’s city.
The Chinese peoples of forty khans
He had pressed to gather;
Half of brick and half of stone,
In Esenkan’s city
He had such a tower built.
Into Esenkan’s heart
Fear had fallen:
“Khan Manas and Almambet,
The two have arrived;
The Kyrgyz people have slaughtered,
Destroyed and shattered the Chinese,
And come to besiege Beijing,”
He had heard the news.
After hearing that news,
Ay Jangjung and Kün Jangjung,
Esenkan and Kongurbay,
Onto that standing tower
The four had climbed.
Ajybay and Ürbü, the two envoys,
Had seen it.
Alöökö’s grim-faced son
Addressed these words to them:
“The Burut who have come as envoys,
Let me slaughter both alike;
Let me cut off their apple-like heads,
Let me settle the matter thus.
To the great khan of Kakan-Chin
Let me send word again;
The countless Chinese, the many people,
Let me gather them all.
When all my people have come,
The idol made of bronze
Let me fasten up high,
And march the people before it;
Upon these two Burut
Then let me inflict torment.”
When Kongurbay spoke thus,
Esenkan stood thundering,
Growing excited and agitated:
“When I call you grim-faced, Kongurbay,
You begin to bluster!
You ought to think, Kongurbay:
A fire blazing from the valley is terrible;
He fled from you once—
Your blazing dog Alma is terrible!
If you kill these envoys—
‘There is no death for an envoy,’ they say—
Will the turbaned Muslims be finished?
Khan Manas, who has come besieging,
Will he flee from you?
In Beijing of Kakan-Chin,
When Almambet brought turmoil
And raided the horses,
We gave you a vast host of monks.
When Almambet brought calamity,
When he raided your horses,
Barring the ford and fighting,
Unable to match Almambet,
You turned back, Kongurbay!
When you return to the gate,
You begin to thunder;
As though you had destroyed the world,
You begin to bluster!
They are a people rooted together;
That he would stir the Burut to action,
That Manas would come here,
I was the one who knew.
If we kill these envoys,
Manas will never let it pass;
He will not leave us two unharmed.
If we kill the two here,
The Burut behind him,
Will he not gather them again?
In Beijing of Kakan-Chin,
The multitude of Manchu and Chinese,
Will he not slaughter them again!”
When Esenkan said this,
Raging and flailing,
Kongurbay took offense,
Bridling before Esenkan:
“Why should I remain in Outer Beijing?
Let me go to Inner Beijing!
Karykan, who knows my worth—
Let me reach Karykan!
From Khan-Too of the jinn and spirits,
All the Chinese on this side
Let me gather utterly;
Let me reach their very roots,
And the Burut, from their roots up,
Let me slaughter every one!”
Raging, Kongurbay,
Renouncing Outer Beijing,
Went beyond all bounds, did you see?
Upon the great khan, greatest of forty khans,
Pressure fell, and the grim-faced wretch
Fled away, did you see!
To Esenkan in Outer Beijing,
To Esenkan’s gate—
The two envoys then,
They arrived, did you see?
Esenkan’s gate—
Its doorway was exceedingly narrow.
To keep strangers from entering,
It had a sorcerer.
Looking to one side—
A dragon stood ready to suck them in;
Looking toward one edge—
A marksman stood ready to shoot.
Looking to one side—
A boar stood ready to strike;
Looking to one side—
A tiger stood ready to tear them apart.
Such sorcery as this
Ajybay and Ürbü beheld.
Ajybay’s Kartküröng,
Though he lacked a tongue to speak,
Was a beast wiser than other creatures.
Counting his years of maturity,
His count of mature years was sixty.
Coming forward at an amble,
Hero Ürbü lost his wits,
Cowering toward Ajybay.
That Ürbü had grown afraid,
Hero Ajybay now understood.
“To stand here frightened like this—
Our home, Keng-Kol and Talas, is not here;
Majestic Manas is not here!
You are a witless man.
When the command comes from Allah,
When Gabriel, the angel of death,
Stretches out his five hands,
Must not a poor mortal die?
Whatever deed is laid upon him,
Must not a poor mortal endure it!
Strange is this treacherous world;
We have seen its wonders.
Our multitude of Burut,
When our people chose us,
Receiving Khan Koshoy’s blessing,
We have come as envoys.
When we mounted and set out this way—
Consider what I am telling you—
We died already then!
To shelter those who cower,
Khans Muzburchak and Kökchö are not here;
If a day of torment comes from the Chinese,
To rescue us,
Almambet and Chubak are not here either!
We are dead, not alive;
We are among the dead.
Let us take the sword Zhoybolot;
If their conduct looks evil,
Rather than simply die,
Let us take an enemy down as our pillow!”
Though Ajybay spoke this way,
Ürbü did not cease to fear.
When courage left Ürbü,
Making his eyes blaze like a lion’s,
Kartküröng, his mount,
He had Ürbü hold firmly.
The renowned companion Ajybay,
Broad of jaw and broad of tongue,
Was a man who made no mistake,
A surpassingly eloquent soul,
Who knew seventy kinds of language,
A hero who cultivated wisdom.
Girding on his Zhoybolot,
Before Esenkan
He was the one who entered alone.
As was the custom of envoys,
When he entered the door,
Bowing like a young bride,
Bending his knees to the ground,
He bowed his head to Esenkan.
Before Esenkan,
A golden throne gleaming with gold;
Around his throne,
Dark trees planted in a surrounding ring.
Before his doorway,
Many wrestlers stood ready;
Without shrinking from the wrestlers,
Ajybay walked past.
Beside Esenkan,
Ay Jangjung and Kün Jangjung
Were sitting together then.
Before them, these were
The words Ajybay spoke:
“Renowned hero Manas has come,
Who broke the Oirat and took spoils,
Who broke through their midst and made a road;
Champion Manas, the lion, has come.
Before hero Manas,
Told to drink water, they drank poison;
Told to take a cap, they cut off a head;
Told to spill blood, they spilled guts;
At talk of turmoil, they grew eager;
At talk of strife, they rejoiced;
Who never turned back from the enemy,
Who never fled in fear of death,
Who never drew back an outstretched spear,
Who never returned downcast from battle—
The earth split and trembled—
Khan Manas’s forty companions,
Before hero Manas,
All forty have come together.
Of whoever said, ‘I have only one horse,’
They slaughtered and ate the horse as they came.
With Beijing, which other mighty men cannot reach,
‘I shall do battle,’ he has come saying.
Among horses, the steed is piebald-headed;
If you ask of his outstanding champions,
Almambet and Chubak, their names rhyme.
Understand his reckoning, Esenkan;
Sending me as envoy,
‘What shall I do?’ he has come asking.
Ay Jangjung’s daughter Birmyskal,
Esenkan’s daughter Burulcha—
‘If my strength suffices, the two
I shall take as tribute,’ he has come saying.
I have more words to speak:
The mighty one among the forty companions,
Their fierce Khan Bakay is there,
Who never rests upon an elbow,
Who never takes even a moment’s sleep;
His strength can match the world.
He and Manas, the two,
Are sons of brothers.
Renowned Syrgak is there too.
Behind that Syrgak,
Wearing coats no bullet can pierce,
Riding extraordinary steeds,
All twelve khans are there.
Among the twelve khans,
Muzburchak and Kökchö are there,
Koshoy and Töshtük are there too.
There are peoples of twelve lineages.
To serve as prayer leader,
Aykozho has come from Mecca;
Among the mighty army,
That man is there too.
Left fatherless,
Ruling a settled people,
Tethering Maaniker,
Summoning the whole world,
Giving his father a memorial feast,
Making his ancestral spirit renowned as well,
Kökötöy’s Bokmurun—
He too is among the army.
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshtek
Is not a man to sit idle;
Kozubek, khan of Kokand,
And fork-bearded Synchybek of Andijan
Are no khans who hold back;
He too is among the army.”
When he heard Ajybay’s words,
Esenkan grew angry,
Thinking so many thoughts:
“This envoy who has come,
Shall I cut off his head,
And against the enemy who has swarmed here
Fight to destruction?” he thought.
“Those Burut—may their daughters be divorced!
Shall I submit to their coercion?
Burulcha, my shining mirror,
How can I give her as tribute?”
Thinking this, Esenkan,
While he stood raging,
Ajybay, who had come as envoy,
The steel girded beneath his clothes,
Without Esenkan knowing,
Gripped properly and made ready.
“Taking this khan’s head,
I shall take my pillow down with me,” he thought,
Preparing without revealing himself.
While he stood thus,
From before Ezkara,
The forty lords who had lain bound
Came back released.
Then one lord,
Bending his knees to the ground,
Before Esenkan,
Bowing in the Chinese manner,
Shuykuchu stood watching;
His eloquent tongue, to the utmost,
Shuykuchu stood sharpening.
Before the khan, rattling away,
Shuykuchu stood pouring out words:
“Among the Burut, renowned Khan Manas has arisen;
At his voice, souls fled their bodies.
His fortune is complete;
His right shoulder is broad.
Not merely us, the Chinese people,
But the eighteen thousand worlds,
He is a lion who could slaughter them all.
Shedding blood upon the campaign,
A hero like Khan Zholoy died;
Why should I speak of others?
Hero Madykan died too;
So many mighty khans died;
All whom he encountered died;
So many kinds of great men died.
When the great battle was fought,
With Ezkara at their head,
His companion Chubak alone
Bound them, O world—
All forty of the lords!”
Bellowing these words,
Shouting between repeated sobs,
Shuykuchu stood pouring out words.
While Shuykuchu stood thus,
Ajybay began to speak:
“Name the ridge you rely upon;
Name the place where you are fortified;
If you will give, speak truly!
If you say, ‘I will not give,’
Until the swaying stalks ripen,
Until the autumn gossamer flies,
I shall fight six months without rest!
Take off your robe and give it;
Step down and give your throne!
If you survive in Beijing,
For six months of rule,
Give your city of Outer Beijing!”
While your Ajy spoke breathlessly,
Esenkan’s resources failed him.
Ay Jangjung and Kün Jangjung,
Esenkan, the three together,
Now devised this plan:
“What is this talk of the Burut gathering
And making such a spectacle?
The forty lords of the Chinese,
That Chubak alone, the wretch,
Bound and took them—what does this mean?
That the Chinese into this slaughter
Were cast—what does this mean?
Let us make ready for the tower
And see the Burut’s condition.”
While these three stood thus,
Having seen Beijing before,
Almambet, who knew it well,
The renowned sultan Manas
Had mount Akkula;
Its collar gold, its sleeves copper,
Double mail with lark-eye rings,
A netted outer garment, a broad jacket—
He clothed him in white padded armor.
When slung upon him, that blessed thing,
The mirror of his broad shoulder blade,
When used against an enemy in combat,
The fine object Akkelte,
He had hung upon his shoulder blade.
Then gray-maned Manas,
His eyes made to blaze like a lion’s,
Made to rage and jerk,
Held by the forty companions,
Made to lunge toward Beijing,
Given his red banner to hold,
He placed them together in one spot.
Khan Bakay and Muzburchak,
Having them gird their swords in their sheaths,
He placed in another spot.
On this side of them,
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshtek,
And hero Töshtük, Eleman’s son,
Wearing deep-blue coats of iron,
All mounted on mighty steeds,
As though about to destroy them now,
If let loose, Beijing
They seemed ready to shatter at once.
There he lunged in one place;
All the Burut made ready,
As though about to trample everything,
Surrounding and swarming in.
When he saw them swarm,
Unable to collect his wits,
Esenkan was distraught.
Thinking this in his mind,
Esenkan resolved to submit.
With pressure upon him,
A thousand girls together with his daughter
Esenkan resolved to present as tribute.
Whatever words he said,
Esenkan resolved to accept;
The khanship of Outer Beijing
Esenkan resolved to give.
To a lineage of the Chinese people,
Word without holding back
Esenkan resolved to send.
A group of the guard khans
Esenkan resolved to gather.
Fair-faced young wives and beautiful girls
Driving forth to give as tribute,
Raising Manas as khan,
Begging for his life, from death
Esenkan resolved to escape.
Esenkan’s daughter Burulcha,
And Ay Jangjung’s Birmyskal,
Putting these two girls at the head,
All the multitude of girls
He clothed in loosely draped silk,
And mounted on smooth amblers.
Clothing them in pure red,
Behind the two girls—
When snow falls upon snowy ground,
Look at the snow and see their flesh;
When blood drips upon the snow,
Look at the blood and see their cheeks!
From such surpassing beauties,
Waists a handgrip wide, faces like the moon,
Rooster-like necks and long, thick hair,
He separated those who stood proudly.
The languishing beauties,
Behind Burulcha,
He sent onward in groups.
Thimble mouths and languid eyes,
Pendant-strung braids and reed-like waists,
The stately girls he set apart.
Ayzhang’s daughter Birmyskal,
With Birmyskal at their head,
Of beauties such as these
He separated a company.
With the two girls at the head,
Taking a thousand dark-eyed ones,
With so many camels leading,
Loading elephants and taking gold,
With Khan Esenkan at their head,
They came bearing tribute to Manas.
Then Khan Manas,
To Koshoy and Bakay,
Spoke these words:
“Beauties among women are coming;
Before you, as tribute, so many
Surpassingly beautiful girls are coming;
There is something to be said.
Of gold, gold coins and silk,
So much cloth is coming as tribute.
Almambet, Azizkan’s son,
Was formerly khan in Beijing.
Esenkan’s Burulcha—
That he would take her, from of old
He had a promise.
What I say to you:
A beauty like Burulcha,
Do not separate her from Almambet.
They say few souls surpass her;
Esenkan’s Burulcha
Is said to be a truly beautiful girl.
Ay Jangjung’s daughter Birmyskal,
Called a khan of outstanding renown,
Her love for Chubak,
They say, has been true from of old.
They call her a beauty among women,
A girl whose wisdom is full.
If you heed my words, people,
When the two girls come,
Let us gather the mighty army;
Almambet and Chubak, the two heroes,
Let us wed them to those two!
Of Beijing, which we longed to reach,
Let us see the wonders, my people;
The gold they have brought,
Let us divide it by the handful!”
When they heard Manas’s words,
All of them, all the army,
Made their promise firm,
Swore an oath unto death:
“Whoever becomes divided from us,
May his oath strike him down at once!”
Chewing white wheat flour,
Grasping hands firmly,
“Let this be the rule,” they said,
Gathering their wise men,
Having all twelve khans
Hold a twig and cut it together,
The pure ones bound by their promise,
Thus spoke the great men;
When Manas conquered the Chinese,
These wonders came to pass.
Burulcha and Birmyskal,
The two beauties at their head,
Many girls came as tribute.
Taking pale, mottled pennants and banners,
Seeing Manas’s bearing,
Beijing of Kakan-Chin rejoiced.
Esenkan and Ay Jangjung,
“Do not slaughter the people needlessly,” they said,
Before hero Manas,
Presenting tribute and pleading:
“Of Beijing in Kakan-Chin,
I have given you the khanship—know it!
The golden throne beneath me,
I have given you—take your seat!
Do not massacre my people;
Do not drive away my good fortune.
Let me offer you as tribute
My Chinese peoples of forty khans!
By killing my Khan Ezkara,
Do not break my back!
As you wish, be khan as long as you will;
I have given my land of Beijing!
Be raised as khan over my people;
Let me serve you,
Let me submit to your words.
Your men who have been slaughtered—
Let me pay their blood-price now!
Do not bring down all the houses;
Do not cut down and destroy the willows.
The Chinese were an established people;
Do not crush and torment them!
Manas, if you are truly a khan,
Do not dig my pit;
Do not make my misery boil!
I too was a khan like you.
Do not bind my hands;
Do not shatter my feet;
Do not send wandering into the wilderness
My multitude of Chinese people!
The stubborn who would not heed words
Heard this and grieved:
‘Manas will enter Beijing,’
Forty years before this,
Karykan in Beijing
Had it written in his register.
Charging all around,
You have become the white tiger;
Unable to find a way to flee,
I have fallen into your hands.
Charging as soon as you see,
You have become the gray-maned lion;
Offering girls because of your wrath,
I have fallen into your hands!”
When Esenkan said this,
All the guard khans,
Showing reverence to Manas,
Bowed with bent knees,
Bent low in submission.
Behind them, the multitude of girls,
Showing reverence to Manas—
“Birmyskal and Burulcha,
A thousand girls with those two at the head”—
All came with pleading eyes.
Rubies, gold and precious stones,
Loaded upon gelded camels,
White silver and yellow gold,
Borne upon elephants’ backs—
All of them, all the army,
Gathered and stood together.
The girls who had come as tribute,
And the great quantity of gold and gold coins,
They divided as spoils.
The gold and gold coins,
The many troops come on campaign
Could no longer load them all.
The beauties white as the moon,
The purest of humankind,
The densely gathered troops
Could no longer manage them all.
The established people, the multitude,
Koshoy, true to his promise,
Chose as their impartial arbiter.
The army that went had no regrets.
With hero Koshoy judging fairly,
When he came to divide the spoils,
Not a single man remained empty-handed.
Their elders Koshoy and hero Bakay
Now took counsel together.
Ay Jangjung’s daughter Birmyskal,
Having a golden tent erected,
They now married to Chubak.
Muzburchak and Kökchö as arbiters,
They gathered them all.
Esenkan’s daughter Burulcha,
To the golden-forelocked, tasseled-belted
Almambet, Azizkan’s son,
They now married her.
Chubak, Akbaltay’s son,
To the city of Tash-Köpürö,
Placing a crown upon his head,
Seating him on a golden throne,
To the people of the half-khan,
They brought and made him khan.
Golden-forelocked, tasseled-belted
Almambet, Azizkan’s son,
Over the Chinese called Torgut,
They made Almambet khan.
Dividing them up in various ways,
(From every side, from them all),
The forces of the twelve khans,
On every side they made them khans.
For Manas and the forty companions,
The Kyrgyz and Chinese together—
Even soaked in water forty years,
Its patterns would not fade in the least;
Even swept in floods for forty years,
Not an edge would budge—
The carpet with golden patterns
They brought and spread.
Magnanimous Sultan Manas,
Upon that carpet,
They now seated him.
As though he would divide an inheritance,
The Chinese multitude cried out,
And gathered around Manas.
With Khan Esenkan at their head,
They truly raised Manas aloft.
Esenkan’s golden throne—
Seating him upon that golden throne,
Over Beijing of Kakan-Chin,
They proclaimed him khan.
Taking tribute from Beijing,
Seated upon a golden throne as khan,
The mighty army of the twelve khans
All had their minds at ease.
The elders Koshoy and Bakay were there;
All the champions were there.
This time in Beijing,
Having many remarkable deeds performed,
The Kyrgyz had their freedom.
With hero Manas at their head,
Having girls taken as tribute,
Upon the Chinese peoples of forty khans
They levied one horse per household,
Had them gathered, leaving none out,
“For the Kyrgyz army to eat,”
Had fat oxen slaughtered,
Had the guard firmly posted.
All who went became lords;
The many troops, without division,
All became blue as iron.
Over Beijing, which Solomon could not reach,
They now exercised rule;
Upon Beijing, which Alexander could not reach,
They now levied taxes.
They made mares their slaughter meat;
Taking great levies and taxes,
They left many Chinese on foot.
When the Muslim became khan,
Of Beijing in Kakan-Chin,
He plundered its riches.
Bearing great levies and taxes,
The Chinese were left in torment.
“One cauldron for every two households—
Keep yourselves alive,” he commanded.
In gold, silver and precious stones,
He demanded and took tribute.
From Esenkan’s people,
He gathered a countless multitude.
Holding a feast for sixty days,
The Chinese man called Shuykuchu
He made a true Muslim.
A Chinese man like Shuykuchu,
Having him cut with a sword,
Having him pressed down with an axe,
According to the Burut’s will,
Shuykuchu went along.
Falsely a Muslim,
In truth remaining Chinese,
Shuykuchu went on living.
Becoming a cook for Manas,
Shuykuchu kept entering his presence.
How he conducted his affairs,
The weaknesses of Khan Manas,
Shuykuchu kept learning.
His conduct and his deeds,
Shuykuchu kept writing down.
In Beijing, where Solomon had not gone,
Having become a splendid khan,
Let us leave Manas thus;
Now let us begin the tale
Of him who fled to Beijing,
Let us begin with Kongurbay.
To Inner Beijing itself,
Before Karykan,
Kongurbay had gone;
Throughout the world,
Kongurbay had sent word.
“We have let the Muslims overcome us;
Esenkan’s Burulcha,
We have lost her to the Burut.
In the fighting, khans died;
All the common soldiery died.
The great wrestler with iron ears,
Kutan the Giant, went and was slain.
With his forty lords at their head,
Ezkara, khan of the Manchu,
Was captured and bound.
Of your Black Kalmyk people,
Dear Khan Zholoy died;
Among the khans of the Lower Sibe,
The wrestler Ylama died too.
She was khan of the Torgut;
Though a woman, Kanyshay
Was a soul we relied upon.
When the Burut swarmed in,
She died among the Torgut.
Of your people called Solon,
Remaining where they had not died,
Fighting six months without rest,
Esenkan’s Börüköz—
A hero like him died.
I had fierce battle fought;
Mighty, fierce heroes
I lost to the Burut.
Now I am afraid:
‘He will plunder my riches;
If we do not pay his tribute in full,
If we do not obey his words,
Manas will bring torment,’ I fear.
Their Manas is a mighty hero,
A lion guarded by the unseen patron.
Set the others aside:
This Beijing where we stand,
He is a hero who could slaughter it alone.
Chinese and Burut intermingled,
When the great battle took place,
Had it not been for Khan Madykan,
He would have resolved to strike;
Upon your heads, Karykan,
He would have done wonders no other man could do!
Dust rose from the intervening ground;
The fugitive pig, knower of secrets,
Almambet too came out to battle.
Syrgak, Ulak Khan’s son—
I have never seen such a hero.
He shook me fully awake;
Syrgak slaughtered
Your mighty Chinese people.
Tall of body and broad of shoulder,
He is a hero equal to Manas,
Chubak, Akbaltay’s son.
Finding words in a sudden crisis,
Finding eyes in darkness,
Finding a fortified ridge,
He is a hero who girds himself with resolve,
The one called champion Bakay.
A lion among humankind,
All of those men,
Their Manas, who keeps them together,
Has the might of a mountain.
At one glance, he is one man;
Approach him, and he is a thousand men.
If I charge him from behind,
He has a dragon’s appearance.
When he roars and extends his spear,
He has the terror of six thousand heroes.
Throughout the face of the earth,
Respect for Manas has spread.
His companions who came from everywhere,
Just like Manas himself,
Had become Khan Manas.
Not just anyone could torment
This Manas who has come.
Among the people of the whole wide world,
No champion remains who can resist him.
When they face and enter the enemy,
If you ask of the champions,
Almambet and Chubak are there too;
To enter battle shouting,
His khans Ajybay and Bakay are there.
Girls, gold and precious stones,
If we do not give them without hesitation,
He can bring a terrible spectacle upon us.
When his Syrgak exerts himself,
He is like hardened red iron;
When his Manas truly roars,
His bulk is equal to a mountain.”
So many thoughts would not fit in his breast;
When he went to his great khan,
Kongurbay of the Chinese,
His tears streamed across ten fields.
To Kongurbay himself,
Karykan stood and said:
“Of Beijing’s forty gates,
Have you seen one doorway?
Opening toward the setting sun,
‘Until the age of Judgment Day,
The city of Beijing shall not be taken,’
Is written upon its gate.
‘Beijing shall not die of hunger,’
Upon this standing Beijing
God’s radiance has been scattered.
This is the land upon which light poured down;
Thirty men, from ancient times,
Mounted golden thrones in this place.
Solomon came to Beijing;
He was a hero who turned back.
Untouched by the assault of a hostile army,
It is a people that remained unharmed.
Knowing Manas’s valor,
Though I pressed the Burut many times,
I am the one who could not take them.
Against this city of Beijing,
So many champions rode;
‘An enemy of discord shall arise within,
And over this standing Beijing
He shall rule for six months’—
Khan Manas, six years before,
Was recorded in our register.
That standing Manas—
Those who heard the news grieved:
‘Almambet and Manas shall unite
And take the rule of Beijing,’
Was written in its book.
Manas shall be khan for six months;
After that, there shall be peace.
If he takes tribute without sparing,
Do not cling to wealth.”
Speaking thus, Karykan
Now showed terrible wrath,
