The infidel who devastated the people,
Whose wrathful power spells the end of the age—
Look upon him here in Beijing!
Will he remain without picking a quarrel,
Will he stop without trading insults?
If he rules the people and commands a nation,
Will he not seek revenge upon
The Chinese nation of forty khans
For his khan-father, blood-shedding Manas?!
The great lords of the Chinese realm,
Finding Chyngteñir’s judgment fitting,
The strong men clad in armor,
The heroes wearing mail,
The champions dressed in stone-hard armor,
The boundless men in quilted coats,
The warriors who know their own worth,
Despairing of their lives like flies,
The many seething dignitaries,
Saying that an edict had come from the khan,
Ran up and down,
Surged right and left in turmoil,
Young and old together—
All of Kakan and Beijing—
The whole people now heaved like a flood.
He made ready in wrath;
Chyngteñir was a mighty lord.
Taking the aspect of a blue-maned lion,
His voice thundered like the sky,
He loomed like a gray mountain range;
Any man who tried to judge him
Would lose the thread of his reason.
His fierce mustache appeared
Like the scree of Mount Kap;
His eyes were like blazing flame,
His jewel button was as large as a fist—
Such was his splendid appearance.
The countless Chinese facing him
Could not bear to look him in the face;
Whoever saw him despaired of life,
And knew him for a blue-maned lion.
Such was Chyngteñir, khan of the Chinese realm;
Such was the command he gave.
From before that formidable hero-lord
The envoys came forth,
The imposing appraisers:
Critics who could judge from afar,
Spies who ranged across foreign lands,
Men who worked through cunning,
Men who knew how to gauge a people,
Men who loaded merchandise on camels,
Who traded gold and silver,
Traveling as merchants,
Who among the Kyrgyz of Ala-Too
Drew out their hidden secrets,
Who kindled fire between peoples,
Who watched from outside to learn
The extent and strength of a nation.
The keenest judge among these observers,
The critic Zhelzhetpes, arrived.
He gauged the strength of the Kyrgyz
And observed their gathered people.
To the khan and lord Chyngteñir
He reported everything without omission:
“I observed Kenen carefully,
That lion and tiger of a warrior;
I studied the hero closely.
He is dark without and resolute within.
He will not fold his hands and submit,
Saying, ‘We are your slaves.’
He is no boy who will yield to fortune,
Nor some witless nobody.
They are not a people who will submit to being driven,
Like the Shibe and Torgout;
Nor are they a multitude like the Chinese.
The Kyrgyz nation is no grass to be mown.
Hero-lord, heed my words.
Let us send between us as envoy
An unerring, broad-jawed orator,
A man whose speech is sweet as sugar,
Whose skill surpasses the wisdom of an age.
Let us send an envoy between us,
And test their strength through words.
With reason and speech,
With strategy and skill,
Let us learn the measure of their strength.
Without quarreling or raising strife,
Let us mount the throne in triumph.
Let us cast a chilling lasso
Over the swift horse they would never surrender,
And take their graceful daughters
As plunder by such means.”
Thus spoke Zhelzhetpes,
Whose wisdom none could fathom,
The finest cultivator of reason,
A clear-sighted sage,
One who, among Chinese and Kyrgyz alike,
Would come first in any contest.
The wise man sharpened his words further;
