In old age Bakay’s eyes
Flashed within their deep sockets;
Unable to endure it, brave Bakay
Cursed Kanchoro:
“Oh Kanchoro, my foal,
My black-steel sword
That never recoiled from blood!
My luckless servant-foal,
Curse this conduct of yours!
When you were still an infant,
I hung an amulet around your neck;
Pressing you to my breast,
I raised you with lullabies.
Fearing you would die of hunger,
I cut fat from the rump and fed you;
So you would become a brother beyond suspicion,
I had you suck
Both sacred breasts of Kanykey.
Among the great Argyn and the many Kyrgyz,
Kanchoro, die an outcast—
When did I ever let them call you ‘so-and-so’?
To become a wing for your hero,
I led you with me and made you a warrior;
I made you equal to a hero’s son.
I reared you when you were an orphan,
Raised you to full maturity.
When you were severed, I joined you;
When you were scattered, I gathered you;
I kindled your extinguished fire
And brought your dead soul back to life.
I made your spindly body a hero;
My son, I made you an immense lion,
And increased your fame for courage among the people.
Now, when your uncle has stooped with age,
You have had the nation drag him as kökbörü.
In old age, was your uncle Bakay Khan
So cheap in the people’s eyes?
Kanchoro, you dragged me as kökbörü—
Did your mother have a vengeance to settle?
When great Bakay stooped with age,
Did I reap your ready harvest?
Did I slaughter
Both your forefather Baltay and Chubak?
Did I steal away your motley livestock?
Did I scatter your white-gold wealth?
Did I flee in pursuit of your kulan?
As though intent on ruining you,
Did I scatter your goods from the saddlebag?
My son, camp valley after valley,
Settling beside each little stream;
Parted from the one who loves you,
May you become as I am!
My son, live long without dying,
My son, inherit and wear my coat;
My son, bereft of your radiance,
May you become as I am!
My son, may a protector like me
Become your enemy;
May resentment remain within you!
Like me, may your beard be
Torn from your face;
May your ribs be stripped away.
My son, camp along the barren slopes;
Parted from your only one,
With suffering fallen upon your head,
May you become as I am!”
Dust whirled in utter confusion;
Your wretched uncle Bakay—
Behold how great was this calamity.
While the hero, his gray wolf, lived,
Great Bakay was khan over the peoples of the world;
He who would not drink kumis
Now thirsted for muddy water.
He had been a man of highest honor;
Great Bakay, who would not drink liquor,
Now thirsted for sour water.
A grievous affliction befell him;
