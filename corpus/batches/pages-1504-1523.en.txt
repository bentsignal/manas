That place was harsh indeed,
Without grass for a single bite,
Without water for a single sip;
This desert was all sand.
Fighting in this desert,
The wide world narrowed about them.
Springheads, springs and fescue—
Every man in that army
Longed to see them even once.
The coursers shook their heads, refusing to go;
All the noble hawk-like warriors
Stared wide-eyed with thirst.
Having plunged unsparingly into battle,
Their heads were buckets of blood;
Dust filled their chests,
And they longed to draw a breath.
The horses barely stirred;
The brave men's eyes
Barely showed a glimmer.
Drenched in bloody gore,
The leopards Chubak and Almambet,
And the dear mighty wolf, hero Syrgak,
Fought on without fleeing.
That they endured so much
Was because Kanykey had known
From the start what would befall them.
“When your very lives are strained,
My darling sons,
Take these provisions with you,
This restoring herb to feed the horses;
When a dark day comes upon you,
These will prove useful,” she had said.
From Jerusalem and Egypt
She had made them seek it without fail;
She had sent warrior Shuutu,
With a letter to five holy men,
And had it brought as a gift.
If a man chewed a thumb-sized piece,
It satisfied his longing;
If five stalks were taken and chewed,
They quenched a courser's thirst.
Sealing it in a hide bag,
Had she not folded it and given it?
Upon the swift camel,
When they set out from broad Talas,
Had wondrous Kanykey
Not loaded it for them?
“The lions will suffer hardship,”
Had she not thought and provided it?
“If the day of the world's end comes,
If the night is dark and moonless,
The noble ones will need this.”
Entrusting it to them, Kanykey said,
“May you be safe, my wolves!”
Had she not wept as she gave it?
When the warrior tasted the provisions,
He grew eager, bursting with strength;
When the horse tasted the provisions,
It grew eager and ready.
Every one of the lions,
When they tasted the provisions,
Opened their eyes wide;
Their aching places ceased to hurt.
“Where are the Chinese? Where?” they cried,
Striking their horses with their whips.
Vigorous young men and swift horses
Regained their strength;
Fire flashed from their black eyes,
And they went back into battle.
Without resting in the day,
Without stopping in the night,
Without leaning on an elbow to rest,
Without even a brief doze,
Chinese beneath them, flowing blood,
So many people in distress.
Dark blood flowed and clotted;
They fought on without fleeing.
Many a noble lion died;
Many bodies lay shattered.
The multitude of dead
Loomed like a mountain.
Gray-bearded elders died;
All the coursers died;
The people's ranks were scattered.
As Chinese and Muslims
Clashed fiercely together,
So many heads were cut off;
So many proud people died.
Graceful as young shoots,
Children not yet of age died.
Those left alive among the dead
Were covered in red blood;
Still more lay wounded.
Barely leaning on their horses,
Their cruppers torn,
Many could not stitch them up.
Their saddle-girths broken,
Their two eyes starting out,
Many could not put things right.
Of many swift coursers,
With saddles slipped beneath their bellies,
Still more men led them along.
“My precious, dear kinsman
Died in such a place,” they said;
Still more went about wailing.
Struck in the eye with a lance,
Still more had lost an eye;
Their arms torn away by arrows,
Still more were maimed.
Struck on the head with a battle-axe,
Still more went fleeing.
Unable to find their wits,
“If only I could escape back,”
Still more hurried in alarm.
Reining in their horses,
“This world is false,” they said,
Entrusting things to one another,
Speaking their final wishes in earnest.
All the brave men rejoiced;
The foolish, wretched cowards
Lost their senses in distress.
All the mighty men,
Without drawing back,
Fought with blows that thudded;
All the seasoned fighters
Seized one another by the lower hems.
Unbelievers and Muslims
Faced each other without turning,
Fighting a thrilling battle.
Warriors skilled with spears,
Champions who were true lions,
Made their horses race like arrows;
With a single thrust of the lance,
They toppled men from horseback,
Bringing them down like mountains,
And fought without a pause.
Almambet and Chubak were heroes;
Behind them Syrgak was a lion.
Argynkan's son Azhybay
Was their true support.
The forty companions led by old Kyrgyl
Were fighting to the death;
All the warriors were fierce,
Not turning back to flee,
Steadfast in their pledge.
In the clamorous great campaign,
Against the Chinese they fought,
In front of the Muslims,
Almambet, Chubak and hero Syrgak
Were an iron fortress and rampart.
To defend the Muslims
Was the oath of these three.
“If we abandon the Muslims and leave,
Will they not know us for dogs?
‘To Beijing the Holy One never touched,
We drove and took them,
We brought torment and slaughter,’ we would say.
If what is told is true,
On the day of the afterlife,
Will the Muslims who died
Not make black donkeys of us and ride us?
No such battle can be found;
No such excitement will come again.
From the swarming unbelievers,
Can one who retreats prosper?
Leopard Manas is not here;
My dear Muslims,
Can fleeing turn out well?
Grip your lance firmly,
Truly use your wits.
Moving from morning until evening,
Fighting until night,
If death comes, let us perish,
Not one of us left alive,
Fighting against them.
Let us show courage, my people;
Against the many Chinese and Manchu
Let us charge and thrust our lances.
The Chinese who stand so stiffly,
Let us spill their blood and slaughter them.
At the dark mass of Chinese
Let us level our swords;
The dreadful one-eyed ones,
Let us slaughter, leaving none alive!”
Shouting these words,
Sarala with his stake-like neck
Almambet struck with his whip;
Sarala with his matted tail
Almambet roused to a quiver.
Across the misty plain,
Without turning, he attacked
Kongurbay's army.
Cutting through six thousand,
“Where is the vile Kalcha?” he cried,
Spearing all he reached.
“Where is the ogre Kalcha?”
Without fearing death,
So the wolf-maned lion went,
Never saying, “I shall die in battle.”
The lion rang out, unconcerned;
Apart from Kongurbay,
He reckoned none worth his trouser hem.
Through the seething multitude of Chinese
Almambet burst, splitting them in two.
With corners looming in the haze,
With points rising dim and dark,
If you looked closely,
It was Kongurbay's fortress.
Around the outside of that fortress,
Sparing none who grappled with them,
Killing those who fought them,
Were the renowned people of the monks.
Finding a plan for the Chinese,
Sending them surging to bring distress,
The Kalcha was a wise warrior,
For all the people of Beijing
A fortress firmly planted.
Who threw the whole world into turmoil,
Alöökö's Kongurbay,
Among round-headed humankind
Was born an outstanding hero.
Not yielding to his prowess,
Azizkan's Almambet
Was sweeping his way inside.
The many monks and Manchu,
Shattering all he met,
He was cutting his way through.
Manas was born in Altai;
When he reached twelve,
He destroyed courts and took spoils.
The leopard shattered the Oyrat,
Cutting a path through their midst,
Making Kokand cry in distress,
Making Talas his court,
Destroying every person,
Manas went cutting them down;
Shattering right and left,
Manas went slaughtering them.
Pouring blood into dark water,
Receiving the holy khan's blessing,
He was named leopard Manas.
While he acted thus,
When he ruled the people,
Found from the unseen world,
The Tashkeser Manas received
Was the mightiest of swords.
Khan Bakay's Oykuma,
The Achalbars Azhybay received,
Almambet's Zhoybolot,
The Kolbolot Chubak received,
The Arkesken Syrgak received—
Those were the six keen steel blades.
They brought joy to the precious soul;
They never shrank from a sweep.
If swung in wrath,
They never returned without drinking blood.
Six thousand Chinese, countless governors,
Kongurbay among them—
Zhoybolot in his hand,
Drawing it from the sheath and shouting,
Roaring like a dragon,
Among the Chinese before him
Almambet plunged.
Those fleeing to the right,
Shattering them toward the hollow,
Almambet cut them down.
Those fleeing to the left,
Striking left-handed, shooting right,
Almambet slaughtered them.
Khan Baltay's Chubak
Charged in from one side.
Heroes Almambet, Chubak and Syrgak,
Plunging among the Chinese,
Those daredevils brought them torment.
Almambet drove a thousand
Toward the foothills;
About six thousand governors
Syrgak pursued.
Among the champions in their very midst,
Chubak wrought slaughter.
Among six thousand governors,
The foremost was Kongurbay.
The black earth was drenched in blood;
Blood running down his flanks,
Hero Syrgak kept slaughtering.
“What stratagem can I use?”
Watching from outside,
That damned Kalcha Kongurbay wondered.
“As long as Syrgak lives,
For these Chinese here
The wide world is narrow.
Among the Muslims,
He is worth their Manas.
Multitude of Chinese, many Manchu,
Hear the words I say!
If we capture Syrgak,
Heaven has granted him to us!
If we do not capture Syrgak,
The Chinese will all die alike!
Syrgak's thrusting lance
I have watched three times.
His skill is beyond the rest;
Even now it has not left
My deepest thoughts, that lance of his.
His right shoulder is broad;
Outstanding Syrgak, that champion wretch,
Is a hero born among the Burut.
His eyes are dimmed and misted;
He is swollen with pride now.
Without feeling shame before Syrgak,
Let us pretend to flee;
Let us turn our horses' rumps to him!
Deceiving and coaxing Syrgak,
With a great army from the rear,
Let us hem him in with Chinese
And throw him into the dug pit!”
Before he had closed his mouth,
Your Syrgak wrought havoc.
On the sloping ground he speared men;
He pierced seven together with one thrust.
Marvels no other man could perform,
He performed as he came through.
Behind him, Baychoro,
The Chinese fallen from their horses,
Leaving not a single one alive,
Came through slaughtering them.
Seeing this, the countless governors
Lost the shafts from their hands;
The coverings slipped from their hearts.
In their fear, the countless governors
Struck their battle drums once;
All of them fled together,
Charging toward Beijing.
The Manchu stayed without fleeing.
Amid the many Manchu,
Kongurbay lay concealed, watching Syrgak,
Keeping watch in ambush.
When Syrgak came near,
Kongurbay, khan of Beijing,
Charged out with a roar.
The Kalcha reached him from the side;
As soon as he reached Syrgak,
He struck hard and rode past.
The lance flew into splinters,
Cracking into separate pieces.
The Kalcha's thrust as he passed,
Your leopard warrior Syrgak
Met with a ringing, heedless ease;
The Kalcha wretch Kongurbay
He reckoned not worth his shin.
When Kongurbay had thrust and passed,
All the Manchu reached him;
Circling round in many layers,
They surrounded him.
As they stood surrounding him,
As he was left alone in their midst,
Syrgak thought this:
“When will my dear Almambet see me
And, while the Manchu surround me,
Come to give me support?”
His insides burned like embers;
Unable to put up even a dog's fight,
For as long as meat takes to cook,
Hero Syrgak stood dazed and frantic,
Unable to gather his senses.
“For me to stand here dazed,
This is no tamarisk-covered plain,
No banks of ancestral Keng-Kol!
There is no Talas where we dwell;
Leaping along on Akkula,
Coming with a roar to strike,
My wolf-maned lion Manas is not here!”
So saying, hero Syrgak
Summoned courage, gritting his teeth;
He struck Kökchebich once,
And among the Manchu enclosing him
He plunged in attack.
His eyes blazing like a lion's,
Giving them more of the same,
Hacking all he met to pieces,
Hero Syrgak went shouting,
“Where is that Kalcha? Where?”
Mountain-like Kongurbay
Stepped apart from his ranks;
He stood waiting for Syrgak,
Appearing straight before him.
“Come to single combat, Syrgak wretch!
Let me spear you and split your gall!
That thing of yours marked by ritual,
Let me cut it off again!
Death-seeking Burut, Syrgak wretch,
Let me thrust my wolf-tongued lance;
Joining you with Manas,
Let me make you like an extinguished fire!”
Hearing this, hero Syrgak,
Smoke coming from his mouth,
Flame coming from his eyes,
Rumbled like a falling stone.
As he gritted his white teeth,
They crumbled like millet.
He charged at the Kalcha,
Roaring like a lion.
Before hero Syrgak
Was a path for just one horse;
Prepared beforehand,
There was a deep pit dug.
When Syrgak began his charge,
Almambet saw him from afar.
“Now Syrgak is dead!” he cried,
Spurring right and left,
Lashing with his whip every which way.
From afar he shouted, “Go!”
“O Syrgak!” he yelled.
His many worries would not fit his chest;
Tears streamed in ten directions.
He arrived before an arrow could;
Reaching Syrgak,
He caught hold of the reins,
Keeping him from reaching Kongurbay,
Making him rage and struggle,
Like a woman whose husband had died,
Making the leopard struggle,
He led him out and away.
Then Almambet said this:
“That dark ridge shaped like a saddle
Is no ridge to cross without counsel;
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
Is no warrior to slaughter without caution!
Whatever place it may be,
The land of Beijing is no place
To enter blindly!
To reach him now,
A rope's length in front of him
There is a pit he has dug;
Beyond the edge of the pit
Is a salt marsh where a horse will sink.
Either when you fall into the pit
Or when your horse is bogged down,
To cut off your head,
There stands that mighty Kalcha.”
When your Almambet reached and rescued him,
Leading him out beside him,
Upon the Chinese people of forty khans
Pressure had fallen.
Riding and prancing a rhinoceros,
Destroying all he met,
Tiken the giant had arrived.
Joining Tiken the giant,
The true marksman Agayar,
Who had studied with Almambet,
He too had arrived.
For the many Chinese driven back,
A day of rejoicing had dawned;
They had begun to beat their drums.
A lion whose size was a mountain,
The arriving Tiken the giant
Was a lion from Beijing.
His marksmen led by Agayar,
Tiken the giant arrayed them and charged.
Angrily he said to the Kalcha,
“How have you ruled the people,
Mounted the golden throne,
And until now the Burut—
Why have you left them alive?”
Putting the Kalcha before him,
Tiken the giant drove him forward.
Joined when they were small as lice,
Together when they were thumb-sized,
Azizkan's Almambet
Knew the ways of the Chinese.
That they were Agayar and Tiken,
That they had now arrived,
Almambet knew.
Then your Almambet said this:
“In the kakan's Beijing,
There was a champion kept and nourished.
Karykan, khan of Beijing,
Torment and death
Could know six months beforehand;
He was rich in wisdom.
‘In Beijing untouched by the Flood,
When a hellish battle comes,
When the Chinese rise,
He will come out without delay,’ he said,
And kept that man nourished.
His champion has arrived;
Named Tiken the giant,
His fortress has arrived.
In my childhood,
Before the dragon,
When, saying, ‘We shall study,’
Six thousand children went,
And six thousand of them perished,
When six children remained,
One of them was Agayar;
He too has now arrived.
A great marksman in the treasury's service,
Who comes out when the Chinese rise;
Be ready to defend your life,
My darling Syrgak!
I shall not let them take me;
The reason they shall not take me—
I have saddled Sarala.
When I was a headstrong child,
Than these two who have arrived,
I studied about six months earlier.”
Before he had closed his mouth,
Agayar, who lay in wait,
Bent his knees to the ground.
Agayar did not move straight ahead;
He ran sideways.
Changing his cap,
He had dressed himself in green,
And had gone into their midst.
The plane tree of the mountain passes,
Kökötöy's shining lamp,
The star above his head,
Kökötöy's only son,
The spring that feeds the river—
When they saw Agayar,
The Chinese parted and dashed away.
As he sat proudly on Maaniker,
Holding him crosswise,
That bastard marksman Agayar
Shot Bokmurun once.
Kökötöy's only son,
His appointed death come, fell to the bullet;
He flew from his horse and fell with a thud.
His half-open eyes barely showing,
His fine moustache lying smooth,
He set out for Judgment.
Seeing this, Kongurbay
Stood roaring and shouting.
Of ninety thousand Chinese and Manchu,
He scattered the tumult to dust,
Breaking up their ranks.
“Their Syrgak, no bigger than a knucklebone—
Shoot him!” he roared.
Hearing this, Agayar
Dashed toward Syrgak.
At a lone ledge, a rocky cliff,
Just as he went to lie down,
At Syrgak who was nearby,
Just as he took aim to shoot,
The skilled Almambet
Took his lacquered musket in hand,
And aimed at the sorcerer.
The expert marksman's
Hammer stood ready.
“Agayar's head,” he said,
“His moon-like brow,” he said,
And pulled it once.
His bullet reached him with a roar;
Of the sorcerer lying on the rock,
It tore off his head as it passed.
From the farther side, Tiken the giant,
Whipping his rhinoceros,
Thundering like a storm,
Came toward your Almambet.
When he drew near Almambet,
With his black hat's brim,
From where did he swoop like a falcon,
Khan Manas's Syrgak?
Before he could reach Almambet,
Syrgak reached him from the side;
As soon as he reached Tiken,
He struck him in the flank and rode past.
When Syrgak had thrust and passed,
The mighty wolf born Almambet
Set himself aright;
To spear Tiken,
He moved in close.
If you looked, Tiken was a hero,
A lion born of Beijing.
He was thirty-five years old,
In the fullness of his prime.
Though two lances struck at once,
Though both lions thrust together,
Even then Tiken did not stir.
All the warriors grew excited;
Leaving the poor ones behind,
Only the foremost faced each other;
They joined together in battle.
Muzburchak and Kökchö, two giants—
Before a word could be spoken, these two
Tiken the giant reached as well.
As soon as he reached them,
He knocked both down and passed.
Before the champions behind them
Could reach these two,
Azizkan's Almambet
Reached Muzburchak.
As soon as he reached him, Almambet
Slung him behind like a feedbag,
And carried him swiftly away.
Keeping him from capture, he put him on a horse
And took him away alive.
To Aydarkan's Kökchö,
The lion Chubak came.

He leaned down and scooped him up;

Watching at that moment,
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
Toward Akbaltay's Chubak
He too came with a roar.
From the back of Kögala,
Chubak, vast as a mountain,
He knocked down and rode past.
Before the Kalcha turned back into position,
Khan Ulak's Syrgak,
Whipping Kökchebich,
Arrived before an arrow could.
The imposing Kalcha,
As soon as hero Syrgak reached him,

He knocked him down and rode past.

The daredevil fallen from his horse.

Argynkan's Azhybay
