Their slender tails quivered,
Their red heads bobbed,
And the geese rose in a boiling flight;
Wild fowl called noisily
Across the marshes where they lay.
There were marshes in separate patches,
And geese flying in scattered flocks.
Dark meadow grass and feather grass
Had grown into thick-headed herbage,
Sprang up again and filled the ground:
The season had turned to spring.
The ridges stood apart in folds;
From every side there roared
A cold, yellow wind.
It was a place where the cherished cap
With its blue-lynx trim shook in the gusts,
Where every heart opened freely,
And many fine men
Could live in delight.
At that time, there before them,
The young prince Semetey
Urged Buurul beneath him,
Made him plunge as though his master had died,
And cast the Akshunkar from his hand,
Setting it upon prey here,
Then setting it upon prey there.
He filled every saddle strap
With geese and ducks,
Hunted along this shore,
Then hunted along that one,
And loaded all his saddle straps
With geese and ducks;
What would not fit upon the straps
He hung from the crupper.
Your Akshunkar of Tengiz
Had talons of steel and a copper beak,
Its wingspan measured two fathoms;
Black-eyed, with blue-gray legs,
It let nothing it saw escape.
It was not sated by meat in bucketfuls,
And never ceased its striving;
It was not sated by meat in platterfuls.
It did not rustle when it spread its wings,
Nor startle the geese from the lake;
It did not rustle when it spread its tail,
Nor startle the swan from the lake.
He sent the falcon after it,
And Semetey drank his fill of joy.
Looming like Opol-Too,
Your mighty prince and sultan
Stumbled about, unable to find the swan.
At the edge of Ala-Köl,
Along the face of the reed beds,
From a great, noisy point,
At the tip of Kuu-Jeken,
There on Ala-Köl’s shore,
He saw a wondrous sight.
It moved with a resonant call,
Its voice unlike any swan’s;
It paced among the gray reeds,
Beating sand with its tail,
And beating the earth with its wings.
Its billowing feathers came apart;
When it called aloud,
Its deep voice could be heard.
Its neck was a fathom, its breast was straight;
It emerged from those reeds,
Appearing as large as a felt tent,
Grazing sedge and pulling grass,
Raising its head and pawing the earth.
Semetey, the brave son of Manas,
Looked closely and saw the swan.
On that dreadful, swaggering day,
Would your prince stand still?
He fell silent at the sight of the swan—
Had God struck the prince senseless?
To set the bird upon the swan,
And have it bring the swan down,
