The child who came from my womb,
You made him be called the Sarts' son.
That dear bird called the little bustard
Flies twittering for its young.
I bore such toil, poor soul,
For the child left by the warrior.
That dear bird called the wild duck
Wheels in flight in search of food.
How much I have suffered, poor soul,
For you, who came from my womb.
Ah, my plump little mottled gray goshawk hen,
My gray goshawk, my morning star.
When I was a young bride, my foal,
You whom I bore so late.
Your father Khan Manas's treasury
Upon the poor and the destitute
I spent and lavished.
When I reached thirty-one,
I asked God for you.
My wide world grew narrow;
I was so long delayed, poor soul,
Yearning for a single child.
At Khan Kokotoi's memorial feast,
Before the great gathering,
A company of holy sages came together;
You were born of their prayers.
All the holy sages joined together;
My orphan, whom I cherish,
You were born of their blessing!
From there we traveled onward;
For six days we traveled far without rest.
There lay the broad plain,
Chong-Ak-Chiy of spacious Bukhara,
A journey of six days away.
The white saddle ridge, like a child's saddle—
I, your mother Kanykei,
Used to keep watch there.
My father Temir Khan's court girls,
Thirty young wives and thirty maidens,
Exceedingly beautiful, all skilled in craft.
She herself was fourteen then.
Beyond Ak-Kaiky
Lay Kanykei's Keng-Kolchuk,
The place she went for pleasure,
Setting up swings and playing much;
The place your unfortunate mother stayed,
Playing in joy and merriment;
The place your mother delighted in amusements,
Pitching tents and taking the cool air,
The place she played upon the swings.
When your mother was a girl,
Keng-Kolchuk was where she took her pleasure.
Since your wolf-maned father died,
My little lake had dried away.
Since your mother suffered hell,
My plane tree had withered.
Let us turn aside to Keng-Kolchuk
And look for an omen;
Let us stay there a night as a rite.
If the little lake stirs and fills with water,
It means God has favored us.
If the plane tree greens and puts forth buds,
Your khan father's spirit
Has come to you, my only child.
If the nightingale sings in the plane tree,
It means the sun truly shines upon us.”
Saying this then,
She headed toward the plane tree.
Kanykei's Keng-Kolchuk—
Its reeds had rotted into mounds;
Thus Keng-Kolchuk had become.
Its apples ripened and became compost;
Its walnuts fell and filled the ravine.
Its willow tops bent down;
The surface of its sand was scored.
Its bistort grass swayed;
Whoever saw it rejoiced.
Thus Keng-Kolchuk had become.
Its leaves were like dressed hides.
My dear Keng-Kolchuk,
Its black widow spiders were thumb-sized.
In its middle lay a broad expanse of lake;
Your dear mother Kanykei
Had played there.
That little lake of Kanykei's
Was ringed with plane-tree boughs,
With birch and poplar mingled.
Its twigs were as great as plane trees;
Its small birds were like snowcocks.
It had snakes
Like ropes six fathoms long.
It had wild asses like colts in their third year.
God Almighty had created
Creatures of every kind.
The nightingale in the plane tree—
How that poor creature sang!
When the nightingale's voice grew sorrowful,
Ah, one who grieved could not bear to hear it.
The sorrowful soul would sob and weep,
Unable to move a handspan out of there.
In that plane tree
Were all manner of mournful birds.
Knowing human speech,
The famed parrot was there too.
If you looked at that plane tree,
Its leaves pressed close together.
To the delights of the plane tree
The boy now truly came.
Eighty red hybrid camels in number
They made kneel in a row for the night's halt.
Eighty people serving them
Boiled the food.
Buurul, as great as a hybrid camel,
They unsaddled, leaving his back bare,
And hobbled him above the hooves.
They let all the horses graze.
From his father, that peerless lord,
The copper cauldron left as a gift—
They boiled the meat of a barren beast in it.
Sweet food, a world of dishes,
Your mother Kanykei spread out.
They sliced the horse belly fat and ate the neck fat,
Breaking off heaps of sugar like snow,
Heaping up the clarified butter.
When they came to Keng-Kolchuk,
They delighted in amusements
And stayed as guests.
When dawn broke bright,
When light fell on the earth,
Starting awake, he performed his prayers.
That fine warrior, Manas's son,
Saddled Narbuurul;
He put out the fire they had lit,
Made them all break camp together,
And went again toward Talas.
The young lord Semetey
Over the rough, knobby hillocks
Came riding onward.
The hilly, uneven land
He crossed and rode on.
Your orphan, Manas's son,
“When will I reach Talas?” he said,
And hurried onward.
His mother Kanykei,
Along the road she knew,
Along the valleys, springs and streams,
Came hurrying onward.
Crossing the rough ridges,
How could the warriors spare themselves?
His fierce mother led the way;
They approached a land like paradise.
There were valleys, woods and riverbanks.
Let me tell you of that forest:
“They call it Kuleshen's Kul-Tokoi.
Within that forest
Are plane trees as large as houses.
Upon those plane trees
Are delights in abundance.
Long ago, when our wealth was scattered,
When we fled Talas on foot,
When we suffered grievous humiliation,
As we wandered toward Bukhara,
We were worn and wasted, our strength gone,
Our vigor fled, our blood drained away.
Finding no refuge, I cried out,
“Your child has gone hungry!”
And called upon the ancestral spirits.
My bones throbbed with pain;
The soles of my feet were raw and blistered.
“My orphan has gone hungry!” I cried.
To the one God Himself
I raised my voice in supplication.
I asked for the drink we needed.
“I come in flight; give me milk!” I said.
“Be a mother and protect us!” I said,
And embraced the poplar.
When we fled there helplessly,
The great poplar became our mother.
From its north-facing side,
From the edge of this branch,
Milk flowed forth like a spring.
We poor wanderers
Caught it in a leather pail and drank our fill.
We laid aside much sorrow.
My mother and I,
Relaxed and happy, drank our fill.
You, still in your swaddling clothes,
We laid between us.
It was a day created by Allah;
It was Friday's Night of Power.
Sweating in our deep, relaxed sleep,
We were steeped in delight.
At the hour of midnight,
I woke startled from a dream.
What God had done,
I had no choice but to endure.
When I looked beneath my arm,
My child was not beside me.
Feeling about and searching, I could not find him.
My ribs seemed to split apart;
Tears poured from my eyes.
From my numerous Kyrgyz people,
I had fled my own land,
My honored land of Talas.
For the one child Semetey,
I wandered away from my people.
Parted from this only child,
What wish could I attain?
Rather than this, poor soul,
Let me die now!
For sixty sheep and forty yearling colts,
The Aktinte knife had been bought.
As I drew it from its sheath,
Upon myself then,
As I was about to strike beneath my ribs,
My venerable mother, blessed by the spirits,
My holy sage, whom I cherish,
Caught hold of the knife.”
She said, telling of that moment:
“Oh, Kanykei, my foal!
Do not end your life in death.
Your mother and the orphan—
Do not leave them like an extinguished fire!
When the white goshawk flies away,
They say the goshawk hen remains.
Was not Manas my white goshawk?
Is not the orphan my goshawk hen?
Your swaddled orphan
May have wriggled free of his swaddling
And crawled away, may he not?
Look where he has crawled,” she said.
