My own cousin Ümötöy,
Is my lord safe and well?
At Kara-Kyr, where mares are tethered,
Whether this be diplomacy or enmity,
Consider it, my valiant cousin.
Indeed, cousin, if it is you,
Sing out what you have to say,
Speak your truth as plainly as your creed!”
So spoke Külchoro,
Ready even to sacrifice his life
And endure affliction;
The warrior, formidable in speech,
Was also mighty in strength.
When that noble man grew wrathful,
He also had steadfast courage.
The peerless one who knew the enemy’s schemes
Also possessed a lion’s valor.
Ümötöy, son of Köksö,
Was asking after Sary-Arka,
His ancestral homeland;
He was asking after the Kazakh people,
The nation of the Three Hordes.
There among them, Ümötöy
Was asking after their lord;
He was asking after the people
In heeled boots and leather trousers.
Ümötöy, son of Köksö,
Stiffened in anger,
As though he would reap and sack them,
Bent on their destruction.
As though to store them for provisions,
As though he had reaped their barley unripe,
As though gray-maned Manas
Had seized and slaughtered his father Köksö,
His anger changed his countenance;
Ümötöy flew into a rage,
Like one about to enter battle.
Mustering his courage and fury,
He said this to Külchoro:
“If you speak of lords, I am a lord’s son,
One of the highborn princes.
I am the dark eye of a surging spring,
One who rejoices at the call to combat—
I am Khan Ümötöy himself.
Not merely Ümötöy, but Köksö’s
Deep-dark, watchful eye.
Finding no mountain to charge,
Finding no foe to fight,
I have worn out horses ranging the land,
And worn out coats while taking heroes.
Do not draw out your words;
Having devoured my father, brave Köksö,
Do not speak evasively,
Thinking you have a sorcerous slave.
You skulking slave whose life is spent,
We have come to call you to account.
Meeting upon the open plain,
We have heard these words from you.
You battered madman,
Today we have seen your face.
Meat tastes sweet when shot and eaten—
They say the argali’s flesh is finest;
In every way I shall exact blood-price
For Köksö, son of Aydarkan.
Meat tastes sweet when caught and eaten—
They say the gazelle’s flesh is finest;
I shall pursue and demand an accounting
For Köksö, son of Aydarkan.
When the teeming peoples gathered
At six-gated Beijing,
At the place from which none would retreat,
They settled the land and guarded Talas.
Driving on the man who said, ‘My horse is my only one,’
They slaughtered and ate his horse,
Driving him toward the Six Khans’ Beijing,
Driving him on, saying they would do battle.
When they reached the road midway,
And the great war drum was struck hard,
