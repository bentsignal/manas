His brows were like reclining dogs.
If you looked at his mustache,
It was like nettles grown on old dung.
Look at the grime on his smoking pipe:
Like ground grain smeared about by children eating.
The tuft of hair down his back
Was like a stallion's mane.
The champion Kochku was a warrior;
From the presence of Esenkan,
Vowing, “I will bring Manas bound,”
He had set out with menace.
He, too, would never give up wrestling;
The flesh of a whole pig
Could not fill him at one sitting.
A trick rider, a swift runner on foot,
An honored, mighty champion,
His eyes were like the mouth of a grave,
As though they could suck in all they saw.
The nostrils of his nose
Were like winding gullies and ravines.
Whoever Kochku met,
He seemed ready to swallow, horse and all.
Do not ask about this Kochku:
He had the look of a thousand champions.
Could any mortal bring him low?
Could anyone meet him and survive?
His father was a raging Chinese brute;
Unable to understand human speech,
He was a beast-like swine.
Angry with Manas,
He loomed in his fury,
Huge and towering as a mountain:
“You have reached the age of twelve;
Even if you were eighty Manases,
Today you would die by my hand.
I will finish all of you together,
Beginning with your father, wealthy Jakyp.
I will bind you all and take you away.
Root and branch, you wretched Buruts,
I will finish all of you together.
When we merely played, you Burut,
You began this furious turmoil.
I had seven hundred champions;
You slaughtered every one.
My anger has risen; I stand unyielding.
Now I will put an end to you.
I will drive your caravan out of Altai,
You fucking, wretched Burut!
Now I will take my revenge.
Even if you had forty-five lives,
You fucker, I would spare none.”
Raging and thundering,
The infidel gathered his strength;
The ground split with the pounding.
Warrior Kochku came out for single combat,
The hair on his crown bristling.
Summoning his strength, straining forward,
To lay hands on Manas,
Kochku stood ready.
At that moment Khan Manas,
Born to surpassing good fortune,
Struck Toruchaar once on the flank.
“Let fate decide!” your warrior cried,
Taking his lacquered spear in hand.
His back hunched behind him,
His ribs stood out one by one;
The sight of him seized the heart.
That raging blood-shedder
Seemed ready to drink blood.
Your accomplished, gray-maned Manas
Arrived faster than an arrow.
The instant he reached Kochku,
He drove his spear deep and passed on.
A foot slipped from the stirrup;
As though a mountain had collapsed,
Kochku fell from his horse.
Akbaltay and wealthy Jakyp
Now stepped between them.
His father Jakyp said:
“My only son, Khan Manas,
You have unleashed fierce wrath.
Bringing dark night and grievous turmoil,
The Kalmyks' champion Kochku—
You have slaughtered him utterly.
You have dug your pit deep;
You are bringing misery to a boil.
You are going to show us, my foal,
A marvel no one would wish to see.
I came to Altai driven into exile,
Wandering, unable to find a way,
Helpless and roaming astray;
We came here straying from that land.
Separated from the great Kyrgyz people,
We fell into the depths.
We were eight sons of one father;
One of us went to Eyren.
Altai, too, was a land for us;
Manchus and Kalmyks mingled here,
And these people were our community.
By attacking a people with a royal court,
You will have the coat stripped from my back.
You are going to bring upon my head,
Through your unruliness, my foal,
An ordeal no one else would devise.
When they hear of this, my foal,
The Black Kalmyks and Manchu people
Will dig my pit.
Saying, ‘Your son has turned unruly,’
They will bring my misery to a boil.
You will have my possessions plundered;
Turning unruly, you wild stallion,
You will strip me of everything.
Now the Chinese will take my sheep;
These Chinese will put an end
To all my far-reaching hopes.”
As Jakyp stood lamenting,
Akbaltay strode in with vigor,
Addressing wealthy Jakyp:
“Do not grieve without cause, Jakyp.
Does a grieving man prosper?
Can this grieving of yours do any good?
Does not a man die when his time comes?
What Allah has inscribed upon one's head—
Must not this poor head undergo it?
Foolish Jakyp, what are you saying?
Let your son be spirited!
If that boy has no spirit,
Better that he had never been born.
Let your son be a warrior!
If that boy is no warrior,
Better unbegotten, part of the black earth.
There is a camp set on the plain;
Captured from Kochku,
There are seven hundred steeds as spoils.
Let us steep ourselves in spoils today;
Those seven hundred steeds, exactly,
Let us divide them as our plunder.”
Akbaltay spoke these various words;
Before he had closed his mouth,
Manas the leopard sprang forward,
Unleashing havoc.
The champion Kochku lying below him—
He cut off his head.
