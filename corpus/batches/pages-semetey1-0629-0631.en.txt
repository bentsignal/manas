They raided horses, and men died.
The two khans bore a feud;
They took up spears from the ground,
Fought wherever they met,
Rode stallions swift as wind,
Fought crying, “O my spiritual patron!”
A dreadful battle took place.
Young Koenaaly, the dear boy,
Had not fought with gunfire,
Had not settled far away;
That boy, to his father,
Had given no aid by striking with his whip.
The young topknotted boy lay naked;
The Turkmens had come swarming in,
With pale mottled pennants and banners,
With an uproar of “Boy-boy!”
When the unforeseen battle broke out,
That boy came out naked,
Gripped a spear and broke the ranks.
When the angel of death came for him,
He died, struck down by a shot.
Kokboru, son of Urumkan,
Came grieving to Manas,
Dear Koenaaly—
That was just how he lost him.
I heard the tale from a caravan traveler;
Tearing at his own beard,
Kokboru, son of Urumkan,
Returned to his homeland, I heard.
His wish must have been granted;
The young boy at his head—
Koenaaly must have come.
With your father, Khan Manas,
With your heroic father, the lion,
He must be lying as a companion!”
Making all this known,
Learned Bakay, the holy elder,
From the boy's father, generous Manas,
The old man left behind,
All those many lands—
The holy elder who knew and had seen them.
Your fortunate elder Bakay, the lion,
In every sort of task
An expert, a mighty lion.
A black stone as big as a small yurt
Khan Bakay took up
And firmly sealed the opening with it.
From that day to this,
No eye can find it;
Where it is remains unknown.
The cauldron for the sacrificial offering
Bakay now hung above the fire.
He cut the gray male camel at the joints
And put it into that cauldron.
That capable, dear warrior,
Who had seen the work of many ages,
That seasoned, dear lion!
Dry firewood that gave no smoke
He gathered without a moment's pause.
He struck it and made a fire;
Capable Bakay, the dear lion,
Began to boil the meat.
He sent smoke flying,
And busied himself, Khan Bakay,
Tossing off his goatskin coat,
Walking about broad-bodied in his shirt,
Running about in plain sight, lumbering,
He kept moving with a stately sway.
Your orphan left behind by Manas,
Pillowing his head on Ak-Kangky,
His head turned toward the qibla—
The noble boy, the lonely orphan,
Fell into a mighty sleep.
Khan Bakay boils the meat;
Semetey lies asleep.
Let us leave this here,
And of generous Manas, the warrior-lion's
Friend for the next world, Bolokbay,
Let us begin the tale.
Bolokbay, the lame smith,
With the boy's father, warrior Manas,
Shared a sheepfold for their flocks,
Shared food as boon companions;
Making precious things by his craft,
He had come to settle in Talas;
He too became a companion
To Sultan Manas, the gray-maned hero.
When Bolokbay came to Talas,
He raised the lame man to be khan,
Made him a giant among the Kyrgyz people.
Every treasure, the yellow gold—
To keep watch over it,
He put him in charge of the treasury.
The cave where Manas stored his treasure—
Forty guards led by Bolokbay,
His unfortunate men who guarded Manas's cave.
Yesterday, when the great wealth was scattered,
Taking up the orphan,
When Kanykey fled toward Bukhara,
The treasury cave
Was plundered by the six scoundrels;
Those unfortunate treasury guards—
When their goods were pillaged,
When havoc was firmly set loose,
The treasury, by the six scoundrels,
Was scattered as booty;
Unable to find his bearings,
Taking the forty guards with him,
When Bolokbay fled toward the cliffs,
Taking a short way over mountain tops,
Saying, “If Semetey comes, I will sacrifice it to Baabedin,”
Leading a gray two-year-old wether,
Traveling over the summit,
When his lord Manas died,
And his four kinds of livestock were scattered,
They remained for seven days;
Bolokbay, the lame smith,
Then went in alone and exchanged shots,
They survived without dying,
Fighting a terrible fight.
Abyke and Kobosh, the two scoundrels—
From them he suffered humiliation;
Chyybyt and Kochkor, making four scoundrels—
From these he suffered violence;
Azhibay and Kolbay, making six scoundrels—
From them he suffered tyranny.
Khan Manas's forty companions
Drew their bows, making arrows rain,
Sent shots flying like hail,
Thrust out spears and roared,
All shouting “Kobosh!” as their battle cry.
Taking battle-axes, leaning back,
The treasury of Manas
They rushed into and wrecked.
Whole otter pelts
They tore apart and divided;
The forty companions left by Manas
Used them to trim their trouser legs.
Bustling, their broad bodies looming,
Their wineskin-like noses bobbing,
Looting gold, hurrying about,
Their strange pig snouts twitching.
They dealt him a cruel reckoning;
Bolokbay's flank
Was shot by the six scoundrels.
From the treasury, Bolokbay
Took no fox pelt to make a cap,
Took no valuables for his needs,
Took no yearling colt to ride;
Howling, weeping, grieving,
Having lost his great lord, Khan Manas,
Suffering violence, hardship, oppression,
Suffering from the six scoundrels what no mortal should see.
The many treasures, the abundant gold,
When they passed beyond his keeping,
Without valuing the wealth a hair's breadth,
When warrior Bolokbay let it scatter,
Unable to find his bearings,
Kayypchy's dark cave—
When he fled toward the dark cave,
The six scoundrels wrecked his settlement.
Your poor man left behind by Manas,
Skirting Kara-Too,
Saying, “When will my dear Semetey come?”
The wretched man passed his days,
Placing his hopes in Semetey.
That was after Khan Manas had died,
When Bolokbay fled and wandered,
Making his living among the great cliffs.
Roaming through Kangshaak-Tash,
Sheltering in the dark cave,
Not dead, not in a grave,
Bolokbay at that time,
With his forty guards foremost,
Was not counted among the living.
That night Bolokbay
Woke with a start from sleep,
His only Semetey had entered his dream.
Crying, “Baabedin!”
He called his guards.
Bolokbay, himself a hunter,
Went scouting through the foothills;
Six wild ewes and four wild rams
Bolokbay shot and took,
And on top of the dark cliff
Offered them to Baabedin.
Then Bolokbay, the lame smith—
Mist stirred above;
Dootu and Bolokbay
Were friends for the next world.
He was that peerless smith, that warrior;
As he lay sleeping,
Of the boy Semetey
He had seen a sign just then.
“My dear forty guards,
Who fled and wandered in the mountains,
My wretched guards, come here!
Deprived of the treasury,
My emaciated guards, come here!
My dear forty guards,
Is there a night like the Night of Power?
Will the day come when Semetey returns?
With my honor restored thus,
Your exposed places covered,
All grief scattered,
All pent-up anguish released,
Will the day come when Semetey returns?
We fled to the mountains for our lives,
Praying hard to the true God;
Your staying alive and well
Is for the boy Semetey alone.
If my orphan is still alive,
He must have reached twelve years;
Kanykey, reckoning the time,
Must have chosen this year to send him.
My dear forty guards,
This wretch's eyelid
Keeps fluttering, twitching hard.
What can this mean?
Interpret this sign of mine!
My dear forty guards,
May generous Manas never die,
May this poor soul never suffer!
Skirting the mountains, seeing no people,
Seeing no land of famous Talas,
Seeing no white-kalpak Kyrgyz people,
