When he examined that tent,
Its poles were gold and its ropes were brass;
Its like had never appeared in the world.
Unless it had come from the peris,
No mortal could have made such a thing.
Such was the breadth of that tent:
It was called a tent, but was a white palace—
The tale of the splendor
With which it was adorned is long indeed.
It was a single tent with one entrance,
Yet from the far side it seemed different;
Seen within, it was a thousand tents.
In one corner of the tent,
Grey-maned Seytek and Akjoltoy
Seemed to be playing ordo.
In another part of the tent,
Brave Seytek, seeker of the maiden,
Together with his thirteen warriors,
Could fit inside with horses and armor.
In another corner of the tent,
Beside Bermet, daughter of the peris,
There were eighty maidens,
And all seemed to fit with room to spare.
Seeing this, Akjoltoy
Could not help but rejoice.
At the entrance of the tent,
A guardian stood watching—
Ask not what sort of guardian.
He received his orders from Bermet,
And wherever she sent him,
He brought calamity.
He stood ready with an iron-headed club,
Wearing human noses and ears
Strung as amulets upon him.
Anyone who saw his appearance from afar
Was driven out of his senses.
The ill-fated peri-maiden
Had selected him from warriors of many kinds
And stationed him there in splendor.
The radiant, ill-fated maiden did this:
“If he is a hero favored by the spirits,
If he is a man worthy of his forefathers,
Let him pass among the champions
And strike down the guardian demon.
If he is one who does not take after his father,
Let him squander all his wealth,
Then panic and flee back.
If he is a dark grey-maned hero,
If he is a bloodthirsty warrior-sultan,
He will darken with rage
And charge among the champions.
He will throw everything into turmoil,
And leave the place desolate;
He will wreak slaughter and raise dust,
Attack like a leopard,
And strike them from the flank.
If he is a foolish coward,
He will panic, unable to think,
And flee toward his village.”
Thus she stationed a warrior among the champions as guard,
And set every peril in equal measure.
So wise was the radiant,
Ill-fated peri-maiden,
So profound was her judgment.
She sought to test whether the hero was worthy to marry,
To gauge his courage
And thus discover his lionhearted strength.
If Seytek proved base,
Far from marrying him, she would not even approach,
But would scatter all her possessions,
Transform herself by reciting a spell,
And flee toward the peris.
She pondered wisely,
Thinking deeply upon everything:
“If Seytek proves a hero,
Let us immerse ourselves in celebration,
Eat meat together from a single platter,
And meet face-to-face.
If he satisfies Seytek’s heart,
Let her follow him and die at his side.”
