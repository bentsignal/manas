My mother-in-law departed in mourning,
My own husband fled away.
Argyn and Kyrgyz, dear people—
Let me see the people left without a master.
He who gave his hand to twelve spiritual guides,
My father-in-law, departed as a holy man—
Let me honor his ancestral spirit.
Let me fly down Talas
And reach the great mausoleum;
My father who went to the other world,
Let me visit his tomb in reverence.
Then flying swiftly onward,
Roaming down Ken-Kol,
Unfortunate Aichurok arrived.
The great mausoleum in Talas—
Beside that great mausoleum,
For Manas, gone to the other world,
She recited a blessing, saying, “Amen.”
The surah called “Tappat”
She recited astonishingly fast.
When she looked this way,
Argyn and Kyrgyz had drawn toward the lowlands
And were settled there.
Kumys by day, girls by night—
Such were their days then.
Wearing a sable hat to match,
They swayed about then;
Linked together, the Kyrgyz
Were rejoicing then.
At that time the Kyrgyz people
Had taken whole otter pelts
And split them open lengthwise.
There was an orchard planted in Talas;
He was arrested.
At that time among the Kyrgyz,
The poor man drove a thousand sheep,
The old woman chewed fat tail.
Of the teeming Kyrgyz,
What trouble of theirs should I tell?
With ornamented hair-braids and broad hips,
Beautiful were the Kyrgyz daughters-in-law.
In red dresses, with slender waists,
Beautiful were the Kyrgyz girls.
In black coats and white felt hats,
Handsome were the Kyrgyz people.
More rice than millet and barley,
More silver than tin,
More foxes than gray hares,
Much property heaped at their thresholds;
They wore fox fur and sable.
The orphan had grown to maturity,
Joined his own people,
And Semetey had become their leader.
Aichurok did not know this;
She was astonished at what she saw.
Their fine men wore
Black caps of blue lynx fur;
All their fine men
Were alike in their fearlessness.
They had winged steeds beneath them;
When six or seven went together,
They had strength to match Chynkojo.
All of them rode
Karabair warhorses,
With sieve-like lungs and copper forelegs.
Jilgynduu, Ken-Kol, and Talas—
Delighted to see those lands,
Aichurok now rejoiced.
Delighted to see her people,
The horse's mouth is weak,
You ride like an animal.
The backs of their spears were like hair-braids;
The Kyrgyz people are not here.
Aichurok was amazed:
“Are these my father-in-law's people?
Let me go and see his horses.
When I learned from those who knew,
When I asked the knowledgeable,
Many people told me:
‘His horses are red roans,’ they said.
All the people told me:
‘Your father-in-law's horses
Are dark roans,’ they said.
Let me find out truly and certainly,
And of the noble man gone to the other world,
Let me honor his ancestral spirit.”
As a swan she cried hoarsely,
Unable to see her companion clearly,
Poor Churok fluttered gently.
In the hollow of Uch-Koshoi,
Along Tash-Tulga,
At Kara-Buura and Chatkal,
Where the Argyn lay at ease,
She saw ridge after hilly ridge,
Saw wondrous places among the hills.
Grazing on those hills
Were countless horses, a vast multitude.
In the hills were mottled-headed horses,
Piebald and chestnut mixed together,
Ash-gray ones with rings on their legs,
Fat ones that had never been ridden.
Her gaze could not encompass them;
The teeming multitude of horses—
Their kinds could not be distinguished.
With stallions' manes and mares' bellies,
One kind among those horses
Were fully alike, all dun,
With amulet-shaped marks upon their foreheads.
There were more geldings than stallions;
All were renowned winged steeds,
Karabair warhorses,
Plainly different from ordinary livestock.
Their foals were like horses in their third year;
Such wonderful beasts, curse them!
No one who saw them could leave;
Look at the kinds in that great herd.
One breed among the horses
Was mottled yellow like starlings;
One other part of the herd
Was coal-black.
As she watched the horses,
Smiling Churok was astonished.
“When we learned from those who knew,
When we asked the knowledgeable,
My father-in-law's horses
Along this Talas
Were said to be dark roans.
The horses of distinct appearance,
That looked different from the rest—
The leopard's horses
Were said to be red roans.
When the leopard warrior died,
When the people gathered and buried him,
When they camped along the river,
After the leopard passed away,
Did all the mass of people
Become as though their fire had gone out?
Had they heard the news—
The many Chinese, the great Manchu host—
And overrun the place and settled here?
Had they taken possession of his horses
And put red stallions among them?
Was it through this that his horses
Had become all red?”
Looking on, Aichurok
Her sun grew overcast in the sky,
Sixty cares came upon her at once;
Her heart was torn,
Her tears poured down.
At the sight of the horses, Churok's
Heart was truly troubled:
“Finding no support to rely upon,
No mortal is as unfortunate as I,
Searching hard and finding no husband!
Shall I die seeking a husband?
From Toltoi of the Jediger,
Shall I suffer utter humiliation?
In many places I have seen mighty men,
Yet found no husband to my liking, all alone;
My misery has boiled over.
Black and gray shirt
With what face shall I wear it?
To Toltoi of the Jediger,
As a wife taken in booty,
With what face shall I be married?
I have wandered through so many places;
How can I go on without finding a husband?”
Her thoughts badly troubled,
Falteringly, Aichurok flew.
Opposite Bar-Kol,
Aichurok alighted to keep watch.
As the day grew late, in the afternoon,
When the coolness fell,
She saw another wonder:
From below Tort-Kul,
From the orchard planted by Lord Manas,
From Kanykei's palace,
From Chachykei's enclosure,
With a marten-fur cap upon his head,
Out for a stroll had come
Manas's son Semetey.
A warrior of impetuous spirit,
Beyond Manas, gone to the other world,
That lion had been born far greater.
She studied him in wonder:
“This is my beloved,” she said,
And Aichurok stood watching.
He rode horses hard and ranged the land,
Loosed hounds and flew hunting birds.
His protector was Kojosan;
Those he had pledged himself to were the forty chilten.
One of those forty chilten,
A black-spotted leopard,
Bounded at his side,
With the presence of a thousand men;
Thus it protected him.
Looking to his right,
A bobtailed, wolf-maned lion
Bounded close beside him,
Thus protecting him.
A dragon, a white serpent,
Opened its mouth and hissed,
As though drawing breath to swallow,
With the appearance of an immense host,
It crawled behind him.
The giant black bird Zumuruk,
Poised to snatch at any moment,
Spread its legs and loomed
Above him as he went.
Wondrously beautiful, with narrow eyes,
Took hold of the bridle and bit.
Someone accompanied him.
Seen from the front,
He had the awe of six thousand warriors;
Seen from behind,
He had the appearance of a dragon.
His face was like oiled wheat,
His eyes like mist at dusk;
His right shoulder was broad.
Whenever his fury seized him,
The eighteen thousand worlds
He was a warrior who could lay waste.
His brow was high, his wrath was fierce,
