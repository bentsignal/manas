Unable to cross Zhorgo's ridge,
Unable to cross on foot, poor soul,
You will load your burden on your neck;
You will wail and suffer hardship.
Dear, fierce Kanykey—
When Orozdu Khan's ten sons
Fell into discord, those bastards,
When they seized my spoils by force,
When they struck, bringing cliffs down,
When they intended to kill me,
Causing grievous suffering,
Having her breast shot through—
Dear wife Kanykey
Kindled my extinguished fire.
Then she revived my dead soul;
The fierce woman showed her courage.
She joined what had broken;
She gathered what I had scattered.
My precious wedded Kanykey,
At every time, for my sake,
Tormented her own life.
When Kökchököz and Közkaman
Came wandering from Beijing,
When they said, ‘I am your kinsman,’
When I divided and gave them their shares,
When they ate my fostered foal,
Spurring themselves, claiming equality,
When they said, ‘I am your equal kinsman,’
With grain beer fermented forty months,
When they gave me the deadly poison,
When they were about to cut off my head,
Those pigs, pressing close,
When they were about to sacrifice me,
Mounting Akkula and making him prance,
Kökchököz and Közkaman—
Those two fawning villains—
Dear, fierce Kanykey
Struck them down and made them crawl.
Thus she revived my soul.
Without illness striking, I became paralyzed;
Pierced in forty places,
I barely struggled back to life.
After my eyes have closed,
Let khans die, never mind the common people;
Let those dogs Abyke and Köbösh die!”
With this, hero Manas
Turned toward the east.
Aiming and adjusting his telescope,
He settled himself and gazed.
Like a leopard, tiger or lion,
From atop the black rock,
He stood poised and stared.
Emerging through the haze,
He saw Beijing's corner.
Made into a fortress with towers,
He saw Beijing's encircling defenses.
Seething like ants,
He saw Beijing's people.
Beyond the haze,
With golden doors and gates,
He saw a court in Beijing.
Fitted with golden horseshoes,
Adorned with a jeweled breastband,
Covered with a silken cloth,
He saw a pacing horse in Beijing.
With a cliff forty cubits high,
And a ditch forty cubits deep,
He saw Beijing's moat.
Set up to billow and sway,
He saw Beijing's grounds.
Seething like a lake and river,
He saw Beijing's crowds.
What so many mortals had never seen,
He saw wonders in Beijing.
A black ridge stretching across,
He saw a mountain pass in Beijing.
Seething everywhere like grains of sand,
He saw Beijing's people.
Pied gravel slopes and red sand,
He saw Beijing's road.
Great vultures wheeling with spread wings,
He saw carrion birds in Beijing.
Unable to hold the vast Chinese multitude,
He saw Beijing's land.
Where even the surging Flood could not reach,
He saw Beijing's city.
Appearing beautiful as glass,
He saw Beijing's plain.
With billowing smoke that never departed,
He saw Beijing's town.
Gated cities beyond the reach of sight,
He saw all of Beijing.
With delicate bearing and tottering feet,
He saw Beijing's women.
Clothed entirely in silk,
He saw Beijing's craftswomen.
All alike, with camel-calf eyes,
He saw Beijing's girls.
Seeing the girls, he grew eager.
While the warriors stood shouting,
He resolved to stir up trouble.
“Almambet, Chubak, my Syrgak,
Do your hips not tire from lying down?
Does a short nap not satisfy your sleep?
Is this a garden planted in Keng-Kol?
To stretch out and sleep,
Is this the time we had in Keng-Kol?
Is this a garden planted on the plain?
To sprawl about asleep,
Is this the time we had in Talas?
Golden forelock and tasselled belt,
Azizkan's only son,
Come to my side at once!
I have seen a fortified ridge;
I have seen a people unlike mortals;
I have seen beings unlike humans.
With no mules and no horses,
I have seen a seething, mighty enemy.
I have seen a road of perilous ledges;
Swarming like ants,
I have seen a truly formidable host.
Tell your secret without evasion.
I have seen marvels, Almambet;
Tell the truth without turning aside!”
Almambet, Azizkan's son,
His battle-axe ringing in his hand,
Looking sidelong toward Manas,
Came striding with a clang.
He stood in splendid view.
Your noble falcon, your wolf-maned Alma,
Began and let his words flow:
“Hero Manas,
How could I not have seen Beijing,
How could I not know Beijing's ways?
That visible Inner Beijing—
The place where I took off and washed my shirt,
The place where my mother bore me,
The place where my navel cord was cut and tied.
From childhood I grew to adulthood;
Day by day my affairs prospered.
Great Beijing is the place I have known.
There, its corner dim in the haze,
Tapering into a little inlet,
That is the place called Outer Beijing;
Its people have a vast army.
When I broke through its border,
I, a poor soul who knew no restraint,
When I slaughtered its people without restraint,
Fastening gold upon the banner—
Amid that slaughter,
My mother, who became a martyr,
That is where I left her.
Those are the havoc-wreaking Chinese people.
When they unleash their wrath,
They are a flood that sweeps over everything.
Raging and shedding blood,
That is the place from which I fled.
There, that black ridge stretching across,
That is a source from which tea is taken;
Those are the devilish Chinese people.
Making Sarala saddle-sore,
Making my heart ache,
I am the one who saw it and came.
That Great Beijing there,
Has its dug moat,
Has its great army made ready,
Has its seething misery.
Those who go do not return;
No living soul turns back.
It has an established Chinese people.
From that Beijing there,
Solomon took no accounting.
Never mind the two of us—to Beijing
Alexander left no trail.
However many wars there were,
Its sacred power did not depart.
Never mind the two of us—to Beijing
The revered Ali never reached.
The four caliphs lacked the strength.
‘Your strength will not suffice against Beijing,’
I have thought, Khan Manas!
To Beijing untouched by the holy one,
‘He will gather and drive the people,’ I thought,
‘He will take them there to be slaughtered,’ I thought.
For that reason I did not speak of Beijing.
Beijing is no easy place;
It is no people to approach without thought, Manas!
There, visible as a dark mass,
That is my father Azizkan's garden.
Made and fashioned of precious stones,
That is my father Azizkan's throne.
Making my coat of mail shine,
Making Sarala lunge,
Those were the days when I played there.
In my playful childhood,
Having its base cut from stone,
Having its top cast from sand,
Its fame remaining in Beijing,
The city of wretched Almambet,
The white city like glass—
That is the place Tash-Köpürö.
There, pied sand and red gravel slopes,
Are my father Azizkan's grassy uplands.
The black bird soaring in flight,
When I ruled Beijing,
Deliberately left behind,
Is the spirit of slave Almambet, Manas.
There, appearing tall and lofty,
Is the tethering place of a thousand steeds.
What is visible beyond it
Is my father's pasture called Khan-Zhaylak.
Do not dig my great pit for me.
Forcing me to tell the road,
Do not keep boiling my misery.
Now I shall tell you
Of the wonders far away.
Listen, Khan Manas,
To the answer I give.
There, the golden door and gate—
In Outer Beijing, Esenkan—
That is Esenkan's court.
With four horseshoes forged of gold,
Esenkan's Burulcha
Rides that pacing horse allotted to her.
There, those looming figures,
Who never wear trousers,
Who take no wives,
Are the Chinese priests called monks.
There, where hanging haze pours down,
Here and there rising high,
The black mountain peaks appearing dimly,
That is Great Beijing itself, Manas!
That is the renowned land of Beijing.
If their belligerent fury seizes them,
The many Chinese in Beijing,
Khan Manas, are a flood that will sweep over us.
That is the vast Chinese people.
Khan Manas, Sarala's well-fed strength,
And among the countless Chinese multitudes
The absence of a kindred soul—
That is my lack of fortune.
Renouncing my idol,
Leaving my Chinese people,
I trotted away on Sarala.
The Chinese lying in their multitudes,
My kinsmen, even though hostile,
With what face could I leave them
And come wandering to you, Manas?
The Chinese people's full abundance,
Almambet's recklessness—
Forty khans' peoples, forty tribes,
Their offspring too many for the world.
I shall tell you their boundaries;
In forty separate divisions, their lands,
I shall tell you of their peoples.
There, the rock showing at a slant,
That is the mine where gold is dug.
You may have heard of them:
Those are the Manchu Chinese people.
There, the dark mass on the plain,
That is the place called Kosh-Salaa.
Cotton belt and broad boots,
Kongurbay, the khan of the monks,
That is the ridge he has fortified, Manas.
All the Chinese united,
That is the ridge in which they truly trust.
That pale, towering mass
Is no mountain to cross without reckoning.
The one-eyed giant Malgun
Is no enemy to approach without looking carefully.
There, the dense red mass you see,
That is Kyr-Kayyng itself, Manas.
Dwelling in that place,
Those are the Torgut Chinese people.
They have footmen no horse could carry;
A huge woman with an armful of buttocks,
They have a giantess called Kanyshay.
There, turning into the haze,
That is the road of no return.
Among the Chinese called Shangkay,
There is a khan called Kanzharkol.
Why should I tell it all?
What inheritance would I gain?
Those are the countless Chinese people.”
Then Manas grew sorrowful;
At wolf-maned champion Almambet,
He took deep offense and grew pale.
The reason for his grievance—
When he had humiliated Kokand,
Devastated the Kyzylbash,
Made their girls and brides weep,
When he had raided their people,
However much Manas asked,
Had Almambet not refused to tell?
“At that time, Almambet,
I had known your courage;
I had thought you a lion.
I put my questions together;
‘You must know Beijing's ways,’ I thought,
And then I asked you the road once.
‘Against my skin, a camel-wool shirt—
If I have worn one, let me be a pig!
Great Beijing of those damned dogs—
If I know it, let me be a pig!’
That was the curse you uttered.
Now, when I look at you,
‘The city of the one-eyed,’ you say,
‘The city of his sacred book,’ you say—
‘The city of his sacred book,’ you say—
You chatter on without measure.
If you are truly a worthy man,
Why do you deceive me?
Think, Almambet!
Yesterday we raided Afghanistan and took gold coins;
We took a thousand black-eyed ones.
Then I asked you
The nature of Inner Beijing.
Why did you not tell me then?
If you had told me of Beijing then,
Would I not have drawn the line,
Fought six months without rest,
And made a spectacle of it?
Would I not have taken and shattered
That city of Beijing?
Would I not have brought upon it
The calamity of the end of the world?
If you had told me the truth,
Would I not have made them taste their salt?
Would I not have let the Kyrgyz seize
Their pure and beautiful girls?
Would I not have broken their fortresses,
Going five times by now,
Would I not have made their lords crawl?
Would I not have slaughtered the Chinese
Chiefs who thought themselves so mighty?
Would I not have burned their tents,
Would I not have killed their heroes?
Enough, Almambet—may you never prosper!
May you never become two!
The reason I say this—
Let me return to former times:
Saying, “I shall plunder their gold,
And maiden Saykal, Karacha Khan's daughter,
I shall take as spoil,”
When I gathered Akkula's reins,
The elder Khan Bakay was there,
All forty warriors were there.
When we crossed Bikirtik,
When we traversed broad Kakshaal,
From beside the flowing river
Khan Saykal came to fight,
From the hollow of Uluu-Too.
When Chubak went out scouting,
When Saykal brought devastation,
Chubak was not the only hero,
I was not one to stand and watch:
The forty wolves charged in.
Even then, unafraid,
Untroubled and unworried,
Maiden Saykal stood watching.
When she raised havoc against Chubak,
When dear champion Khan Bakay
Had come down from his horse,
When the warriors' very deaths
Were about to overtake them,
Driving the forty warriors' forty steeds
Ahead of her,
When maiden Saykal, foremost among humankind,
Was about to depart,
You turned a summer day to winter,
And Saykal, most beautiful of maidens,
You chilled upon a summer day,
Turning her garments to ice.
Then you rescued Chubak,
You stooped and brought him slung behind you—
Syrgak, who had been left on foot.
On that journey, for the forty warriors,
You joined what had been broken,
You gathered what had scattered.
You kindled their unburning fire,
You revived their dead souls.
Then Karacha's Saykal
Came rushing out before me.
The forty lion-wolf warriors,
Old Kyrgyl, leader of the forty,
Shuutu, whose hat was like a yurt's felt roof—
She speared three in a row, bringing them down;
All the warriors who offered resistance
She speared and shattered.
Then a red spear of keen steel
You fitted for me, Almambet.
In sixty days of exchanging fire,
You alone joined me
And held out, Almambet.
As we went through those days,
“Against his skin, something no bullet can pierce—
He must wear it,” I thought.
“This Great Beijing standing here—
He must know it,” I thought.
When I saw this valor of yours,
I struck up a conversation,
“Did you know Beijing?”
I asked you here.
“Bulletproof armor against my skin—
May I never prosper if I wore it.
The Chinese people's Great Beijing—
May I never prosper if I knew it,”
You cried, lamenting so much.
Enough, Almambet, my hero!
You meant to rob me of my means,
Gathering them halfway along,
You meant to slaughter my people.
You meant to break me, Almambet,
Making my back ache now.
By withholding Beijing's story,
You meant to chill my body.
It seems you have been sparing
Your forty tribes of Chinese people.
Here, as for Beijing,
It became clear that you were sparing it.
When I learned that you were sparing them,
Lion, gray-maned Almambet,
My heart was deeply troubled.
Hero, had you told of Beijing from the start,
Would I not have thrust, with a shout,
My spear at Inner Beijing?
Would I not have made Chinese blood flow
Across so many, many plains?
Would I not have slaughtered the Chinese
Lords with their red tufts?
Would I not have destroyed the Chinese
Cities they had fortified?
Seeking words, drawing them out,
Your manner of speaking became clear;
The countless Chinese and Inner Beijing—
It became clear that you were sparing them.
So many curious tricks
It seems you had not found.
Saying, “The countless Chinese are my people,”
It seems you had kept silent.
“Why should I tell Manas
My secret?” you must have thought.
“Somehow I shall go to Beijing,
And, making my people my protection,
Depart,” you must have thought.
After Khan Manas said this
And stood expressing his resentment,
Almambet then spoke:
“Khan Manas,
Here is that land of Beijing.
You have been offended all this while—
Cut off my head; here I am!
How could I not know Beijing,
How could I not have seen Beijing?
A curse upon being a subject!
You meant to lodge your guest overnight,
Brooding halfway along,
You meant to make me tell a tale.
Taking offense, you brought me hell;
Scowling upon Tal-Choku,
You brought me great suffering.
You meant to have me scout, Khan Manas,
That sprawling city of Beijing.
You meant to bring down upon my head
Calamity from far away.
You turned my secret inside out,
You meant to make me tell here
The very truth about myself.
The countless Chinese with their red tufts—
Do you think I came without seeing them?
That sprawling Beijing—
Do you think I came without knowing it?
The curving ridge of Kara-Kyr—
Do you think I came without traversing it?
The yellow gold of Beijing—
Do you think I came without scattering it?
Among the Black Chinese and Manchus,
With a cotton girdle and belt
I was bound—let me tell you.
I, a luckless, wretched soul,
In this very Beijing
Was born—let me tell you.
My waist curved like a lion's,
Six years earlier,
Before one such as I was born:
'If one named Almambet is born,
He will destroy us at the root,
The Chinese of deep-rooted Beijing—
He will slaughter them all,'
In the great emperor Esenkan’s
Register it was inscribed.
When they saw this inscription,
Great Beijing took fright
And spread the rumor:
Upon Kakan-Chin's Beijing
He would bring humiliation.
'If Almambet should be born,
He will not spare broad Beijing;
He will put it thoroughly to rights,'
Said the command issued to the people:
He inflicted upon the people
An unheard-of horror.
Of the city with its gates,
He gathered the countless inhabitants.
Whenever a woman bore a child,
'Bring us the child to see,' he said,
Tormenting all his people.
Unable to endure his wrath,
His people could find no peace.
Such, in Beijing,
Was my turmoil, Khan Manas.
That is why, concerning Beijing,
I had not told its circumstances.
Chewing white wheat and swearing an oath—
It is true I did not eat it.
For this reason, it is also true
That I did not speak of Beijing.
'If I happen to tell,
Manas will drive his army onward
And slaughter the whole world,' I thought;
It is true I did not tell.
'If I happen to speak of Beijing,
They will say I know the Chinese situation;
If the Kyrgyz people are wiped out,
Disgrace will remain,' I thought.
'Manas will wreak havoc,' I thought,
'For the poor, countless people's suffering,
He will bear the guilt,' I thought.
That is why, concerning Beijing,
I did not tell you from the beginning, Manas.
From this very Beijing
A khan named Alöökö arose.
Alöökö's Kongurbay—
When humankind heard his fame,
His voice alone made their souls depart.
An ember rising from the hearth,
When he reached nine years of age,
The Kalcha began his mischief.
When he reached eleven years,
Unable to withstand his wrath,
The countless people of Kakan-Chin
Were thrown into disarray.
When he reached thirteen years,
He brought turmoil upon the people,
He seized the monks' khanate.
After that, in Beijing,
He became the ruler.
At that time, Kongurbay
My father's Khan-Zhaylak—
Withholding it by force,
He seized it entirely.
Esenkan's son Börüköz
Also grew powerful in Beijing.
My poor father, who had no child,
Was humiliated worse than anyone.
Those two wretches grew great in Beijing.
My father Azizkan,
For the sake of a male child,
Had taken sixty wives.
Until he reached seventy,
None of the sixty wives gave birth.
My dear father Azizkan
Was left without an heir.
Weeping bitterly in exhaustion,
Distressed by longing for a child:
'Alöökö's Kongurbay
Has sucked the whole world dry,' he said.
'Esenkan's Börüköz
Has become the khan of Beijing.
No heir has been born of me—
What a terrible thing has happened!'
Beijing's khan Karykan,
Alöökö Khan, Azizkan,
And Esenkan—the four were brothers.
All were sons of one emperor.
Over Beijing with its forty gates,
The Chinese people's Karykan,
A man of sacred power,
The Chinese, the people of forty khans—
He was the one who ruled them.
My father Azizkan,
That poor soul, worn out with grief,
Gave six swift horses
To Karykan in Beijing.
In his old age, my father
Wept as he sent a letter.
When the letter reached Karykan,
He took it in his hand and looked.
When he examined the letter's contents,
Chinese words were inscribed,
Written downward.
It was a letter of respectful greetings.
As he sat studying the letter,
Written at its end
Was Azizkan's name.
'I have taken sixty wives,' it said,
'And have seen no heir.
Alöökö's Kongurbay
Has sucked the whole wide world dry.
No child has been born of me—
What a terrible thing has happened!
'To Karykan, khan of Beijing—
Greetings to my brother,' it said.
Let him heed these words of mine:
One who will bear a son greater than Kongurbay,
One who will bear a giant like a hand-held mace—
From the Chinese people with their many households,
Let him parade the maidens of Beijing,
And, choosing from among my people,
Find me a wife.
If he does not find me a wife,
If he does not heed these words,
I shall not turn the plow;
Rejoicing that I have become a khan,
I shall not go to this Beijing.
I shall renounce my idol.
If he will not heed my words,
If he will not find me a wife,
I shall abandon my Chinese people.
Why should I remain here?
For the sake of a single child,
I shall go and join the Burut below.
Alöökö's Kongurbay—
I have suffered humiliation from him.
Through the affliction of childlessness,
I have endured everyone's tyranny.'
When Azizkan, saying, 'I have no child,'
Sent his letter,
Sovereign Karykan said:
'Are these words of his not true?
He was at the people's frontier,
He was a man of sacred power.
He took sixty wives,
Yet was left without an heir.
Let me die rather than live!
One who will bear a son bigger than a hand-held mace,
One who will bear a giant greater than Kongurbay—
From my countless Chinese people,
Choosing from among the many,
I shall find him a wife.'
Saying this, Karykan
Had his booming drum beaten,
Had his war drum roar,
Had all his people summoned.
Sky and earth were filled with dust.
'What has happened?' they said,
The people frozen in astonishment.
In this way Karykan
Brought a marvel upon the people.
When he was about to choose my mother,
He proclaimed to his people
And issued his command.
Of Beijing's forty gates,
He had all forty opened.
Enough that people could never finish eating,
He distributed his durable travel rations.
He had thousands of oxen slaughtered.
To Beijing's forty discerning sorcerers,
'Choose a maiden for me,' he said,
And stationed the forty at forty gates.
He had a mountain of firewood gathered
And boiled the meat in cauldrons.
'Let them gather the people,' he said,
Sending his mighty wrestler Ylama
And his great Kalmyk named Zholoy
Out in every direction,
To gather all the maidens
And bring them into Great Beijing.
Of those younger than thirty-three,
No woman was left at home;
Of those older than fifteen,
No maiden was left at home;
Among the chosen ones of the Chinese,
No craftswoman was left at home.
When they heard this news,
Of the Chinese people of forty khans,
All the maidens assembled.
Sorrow descended upon Beijing.
When my father was to marry my mother,
So many beautiful women and maidens
Perished in the crowd.
Suffering was heaped upon the people;
Shots were fired in Great Beijing.
Into the fortress of Beijing
They all paraded for sixty days.
When no maiden could be found,
Among the Chinese people of forty khans
There was a khan named Sooronduk.
That khan had
Twelve daughters in his care.
The youngest of the twelve girls,
Sooronduk's cherished darling,
Was accomplished among women.
My revered, pure mother
Was a maiden named Altynay.
My mother wore golden silk:
'May I die having borne a child,'
Was the wish she cherished.
At the mention of a child, she sobbed,
Burning like a flame of fire.
While she lamented like a camel calf,
There came into her dream
A white-bearded wandering holy man.
Boys slender as young shoots—
He gathered them in a row.
'This is the daughter of light,'
Said that holy man who had come;
'This is the son of light,' he said,
And solemnized a marriage in the night.
When the dawn broke clear,
When light fell upon the earth,
When my mother rose from bed
And performed her full ritual washing,
The young boy at her breast
Vanished from sight.
'The boy who disappeared—
May I see him once more,'
Was always in her thoughts.
She began to have pregnancy cravings;
A three-month child was in her womb.
Thus was power imparted to my mother.
By always calling me merely Chinese,
Do not torment me, hero Manas:
My lineage was born of purity!
So they brought turmoil.
When she was seventeen,
As for my revered, pure mother,
Kutan the giant with iron ears
Drove her ahead of him.
Driving her there, Kutan the giant
Put her into the procession.
The chief of the forty sorcerers,
A sorcerer named Shuytu,
Caught sight of my mother.
Testing her, finding her fully worthy,
Sitting and examining her closely—
Those forty Chinese sorcerers—
Have you seen their knowledge?
They seized my mother.
Into the hands of the Chinese
My dearest mother
Fell as a captive.
Then my mother Altynay
Struggled this way and that,
Weeping in deep sobs:
'To a Chinese man who barks commands—
Alas, how can I marry him?'
She wailed and cried in anguish.
'I would rather die than this,' she said,
Tormenting her mortal soul.
'How can I speak the words?
With a white-bearded fellow,
How can I lie in his embrace?
How can I let him bind my waist?
The precious one within me—
How can I let them wash him?'
Refusing to yield to the wrestler,
She caused terrible trouble.
The steel sword at her side—
My mother drew it forth.
Of the wrestlers surrounding her,
My dearest mother
Cut off many heads.
After six of Beijing's wrestlers died,
After the Chinese saw my mother's courage,
Their wrestler called Mighty Ylama
Advanced from the flank, prepared.
Kutan the giant with iron ears,
Mangküsh the giant with an iron forehead,
Against my mother, who would not be caught,
Held a net forged of iron
And surrounded her.
They put her in the iron net.
Dragging her around,
Before Karykan,
'We have found a wife,' they said,
And brought my mother there.
My mother endured suffering.
All forty watchful sorcerers
Spoke to Karykan
In this way:
'This woman will bear a giant,
She will bear a great one like Kongurbay.
For this very Beijing
She will bear a city, fortress, rampart.
Laying the people waste,
She will bear a leopard-tiger of vengeance.
If he so desires, she will bear a hero
Who will go away from Beijing.
If you alienate his heart,
Breaking and shattering its defenses,
Sprawling, populous Beijing—
She will bear a lion who will burn it.
If she bears a worthless son,
Cut off our heads,' they said.
'If he is not Kongurbay's equal,
Make us targets and shoot us,' they said.
'If we have seen wrongly,
May the Buddha strike our eyes!
If we have spoken falsely,
May the Buddha strike our words!
If by chance we speak without knowing,
May the Buddha strike us!'
At these words of theirs,
Karykan remained unwilling.
'Let me know one sign,' he said.
'If it is true she will bear a son,
Show me here and now,'
Said Karykan.
After hearing the khan's words,
The forty sorcerers of the countless Chinese
Kept my mother in the palace for six days,
Not letting her see the sun.
When six days had passed,
They let her see the bright sun.
They took my mother out
And then made her urinate.
When they examined where she had urinated,
Two thumb-to-forefinger spans and one fingerbreadth
The urine had entered the earth.
Seeing this, the Chinese
Knew my mother would choose a hero.
If one could be found among mortal men,
They knew she would bear a lion.
Then the Chinese
Gathered their lamas.
Rattling away in Kalmyk,
Chattering away in Chinese,
For my mother Altynay,
They at once solemnized the marriage.
My father married my mother.
In this way, she fell
Into the hands of the Chinese.
With shoes of gold.
In all the different Chinese styles,
They clothed my mother,
