Traveling back from Mecca,
To his father, the champion Baltay,
Lion Chubak came.
“Grown to maturity, become a warrior,
The equal of other warriors' sons,
My comfort, Chubak, has come,” he said.
“Now God has granted my wish!”
His father gave an abundant feast.
Standing rigid when his wrath arose,
Khan Baltay's Chubak,
Who would raise dust among the people,
Was the Illustrious Champion.
Was Chubak's coming from the unseen in vain—
The companion of bloodthirsty Manas?
When he came to Khan Baltay,
That fiercely violent Chubak
Took Akbaltay's wealth
And scattered it as well.
Though Akbaltay's vast herds
Were scattered to the last,
He never so much as frowned.
Chubak, his only support,
Khan Baltay did not resent.
The reason he did not resent him:
He was the spring flowing through his valley,
Of your dear old Baltay,
The little one granted at death's door.
Distant peoples said of him:
“Chubak, Akbaltay's son,
Was hard-won by Akbaltay.
If this child survives,
He will overturn the world!”
One day, while the warrior Chubak lay asleep,
He woke with a start,
And turned to his right.
Toward Khan Baltay's court,
With his hands behind his back,
That splendid, swaggering warrior
Walked in, unperturbed,
Into Khan Baltay's court.
There were khojas in white turbans;
There were mullahs reciting lessons;
There were learned scholars as well;
All the men of knowledge were there.
Walking up then
To his father, Khan Baltay,
Leopard Chubak began to speak:
“Father, where is a mountain to batter?
Where is an enemy to fight?
Is there a pit already dug?
Is there brine already boiled?
Is there a coat for me to wear?
Is there an enemy for me to attack?
Father, Khan Baltay, think!
I have matured and become a warrior;
I am the equal of other warriors' sons.
Finding no embers to wade through,
Finding no arrow to aim and shoot,
If you would hear my words, oh father,
I stand here in distress.
In my dream I saw a wondrous thing;
Such a thing happens in no age.
Sing me your song, father;
Tell your secret as truly as your faith.
Where is tamarisk-grown Keng-Kol?
He who dwells in Talas—
Where is the warrior Manas?
From the people of the Islamic faith,
Is there a letter you have sought and received?
Your mountain horses are nine multitudes.
Oh, father Akbaltay,
Is there a horse to ride to war?
Father, Khan Baltay, hear my words.
A swift steed to ride to war—
I have searched and cannot find one.
To lion prince Manas,
I shall not rest until I come!”
Before he had finished speaking,
Before he could close his mouth,
That warrior Akbaltay,
Shimmering like flowing mail,
With all his resources still at hand,
Began to speak with a radiant face:
“My Chubak, granted in old age,
Look, oh my people,
My little one who will destroy the governors!
The hour of my wish's fulfillment has come.
From God Most High,
This was what I had asked.
If you find an enemy to fight,
Let us go forth in support.
You have spoken true and worthy words;
Let us receive them and listen.
You began to speak quietly;
The youngest of the six lions,
God's beloved mortal,
The generous prince Manas—
You have just named him.
Let a son be unruly;
If he is not unruly, let him be nothing!
If your father Baltay does not die this year,
If he does not see the other world,
I shall make the stars in heaven pale;
To lion prince Manas
I shall make you a friend beyond this world.
If I do not braid the camel's tail,
Saying, ‘Here is your companion for life,’
To that peerless, bloodthirsty one,
If I do not give you as his companion,
Then I, Baltay,
May I never see Judgment Day!
God has brought me to my heart's desire.
God Most High, without stint,
Has given me
The valiant child Chubak.
What you have said is true as well;
All you have said is true.
My darling foal,
My plane tree with its single root,
Whether I walk or stand, now
I shall pray for your life.
To raid Akbaltay,
Many jealous ones lie in wait;
Many Chinese stand at my flank,
Many enemies seethe around me.
My son, the mountain horses are nine multitudes—
