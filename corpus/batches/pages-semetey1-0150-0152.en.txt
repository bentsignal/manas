Almambet, son of Khan Azyz,
Fled the unbelievers and became an exile,
Scattering all he had gathered,
Seeking faith alone.
Staining his golden throne with blood,
Saying, ‘He openly refuses to obey,’
Saying, ‘He will not heed my words,’
He slew his father, Khan Azyz, and laid him low.
Staining his silver throne with blood,
He laid his father Kun Zhang-Zhung low.
Coral rank-buttons and coral stones—
Among the governors he slew a thousand khans.
Whose armguards loomed large—
Among the governors he slew a thousand men.
The Chinese with rank-stones on their caps—
He slew their finest men and made an example of them,
Upholding the truth of the words ‘In God's name.’
Your champion brought devastation upon Beijing.
The golden throne he had occupied in Beijing—
True righteousness having entered his thoughts,
He utterly renounced it.
Planted from yellow willow,
His willow remained in Beijing.
Made of pure gold,
His throne remained in Beijing.
Forty tightly secured doors—
All remained in Beijing.
Forty Chinese tribes, a valued people—
His village remained in Beijing.
Parting from the wife he had married,
That Burulcha, Esen's daughter—
His wife remained in Beijing.
Renouncing his gold,
He stirred up strife in Beijing.
Saying, ‘Oh, how can I remain in Beijing?’
From the black-burning hell,
Saying, ‘I shall free my soul,’
Rather than be khan of this Beijing,
Saying, ‘I shall reach Mecca and settle there,’
Rather than be khan of these unbelievers,
Saying, ‘I shall be a Muslim's slave,’
Saying, ‘How can I remain in Beijing?’
From the hell stretched out before him,
Saying, ‘I shall free my soul,’
He scattered what he had gathered,
Seeking faith alone.
He regarded as true
The holy word, the Quran.
He regarded as One
God alone, the Creator,
And scattered the countless Chinese.
Then, among those left alive,
Kongurbay, son of Alooke,
Fought without rest for six months.
Back when he was twelve,
He entered Great Beijing and turned back.
His mother Altynai—
He was parted from her at just twelve years old.
Suffering hardship, seeking a spiritual guide,
Fleeing bareback in search of faith,
While clouds moved across the sky,
He befriended a saint.
Killing the khan of Altai,
Hearing that he had entered Khangai,
Killing the khan of Khangai,
Finding no support upon the earth,
Hearing that Kokcho had come,
He befriended Kokcho.
When he came and settled in Sary-Arka,
That slave Kokcho's treasury—
All his chests—
When they filled with yellow gold and riches,
His yurt walls grew rich and greasy,
When he met Almambet,
Kokcho of the Kazakhs
Became a truly imposing khan.
His yurt’s felt walls grew rich and greasy;
When he encountered Almambet,
Kokcho of the Kazakhs
Became khan of the lands all around.
Of those happenings long ago:
She was my elder sister,
That woman Akerkech, on whose account—
Akerkech, Kokcho’s wife,
Whose wisdom surpassed other people’s—
While he was in her village, they called him her lover.
When Almambet was in Great Beijing,
I heard he had risen to high rank;
On Akerkech’s account,
Kokcho and Almambet,
I heard, had fallen into discord.
Kokcho, Aidarkan’s bastard piss,
I heard, had stirred up a quarrel;
The mass of Kazakhs, in the reeds,
I heard he had penned them up.
Almambet, born a leopard,
Had taken his whip to Kokcho,
I heard, beaten him and gone.
Back when he sought a place to turn,
When he came from Bukhara, worn and wandering,
When tribute was collected from the religious masters,
Aruuke, Agynai’s daughter,
When my Almam took her in marriage—
Golden forelock, tasselled belt,
Khan Azyz’s only son—
Then he came and settled in Talas,
And became Manas’s warrior companion.
A gray among the white horses,
They became friends unto the afterlife.
Then Manas spoke:
“For the champion who has come from afar,
If I do not grant him a horse,
Will Allah not make us a laughingstock?
If we do not grant him a coat,
This champion who will enter battle,
Will God not make us a laughingstock?
Go, my senior wife,” he said,
“Do as I ask,” he said.
“My khan father Temir Khan’s Aktulpar—
Visit your father’s home,
And somehow bring it back,” he said.
“If you bring back Aktulpar,
To Almambet, Khan Azyz’s son,
Grant its reins as his own,” he said.
Then I was skilled above other women;
Then your child Kanykei,
A beauty among the dark-complexioned,
Radiant among Muslims,
The youngest of twelve daughters,
I, the fierce one, God’s beloved servant,
In the days when I was playing,
Still a playful child,
My khan father Temir Khan’s darling.
Then my lord said:
“You will do as I ask;
You were a cherished little daughter;
Your father Temir Khan’s swift steed—
Somehow ask him for it and return.”
Uncle Bakai, listen!
When Almambet came from Beijing,
God had granted my wish.
Then I, the fierce and luckless woman,
Had only reached twelve years of age.
I took Shuutu as my companion,
Then loaded seventy camels with gold coins,
When the camels’ tails were braided,
A white kerchief set upon my head,
When I, the luckless one, came home to Bukhara,
Presenting the gold as a gift,
So many camels I gave and gathered.
Calling “Father!” in distress,
Begging Temir Khan,
I suffered terribly;
I asked for my father’s Aktulpar.
He would not heed what I said;
He would not give me Aktulpar.
When he poured the gold into his coffers,
When my khan father tormented me,
From the khan’s city of Bukhara,
He left me hollow-eyed and forlorn;
He would not heed what I said;
He would not give me Aktulpar,
And sent me off alone, empty-handed.
“Oh, shameless Kanykei!
She will not do as I ask,” he said.
“When I wish for Aktulpar,
She will not visit home and bring it,” he said.
“This worthless, furious woman is no good,” he said.
“To her father, Kanykei
Is not worth a single yearling,” he said.
Then the blood-drinking leopard
Laid punishment upon my neck;
When I pleaded, he would not listen;
He showed me no affection,
And would not lie in my embrace.
I settled along the river;
On account of Aktulpar,
For six months I was a neglected wife.
The ten commands the saints had given,
I no longer called a blessing.
Think of it, Uncle Bakai:
That sickle-nosed, dark Sart,
I no longer called him father.
I went back to Talas empty-handed.
“May you perish, Father!” I said,
And renounced him as my father.
When I visited my father’s home,
When did I ever obtain Aktulpar?
“Even if I die, I will not see his face,” I said;
I made that promise to God.
That vulture-like, dark Sart—
My father—what would I gain by going to him?
He who dwells there in Sary-Arka,
Born of my elder sister Akerkech,
He was the child’s cousin through their mothers;
Let me go to Umotoy.
Uncle Bakai, my sultan,
I have something to tell you:
On account of Aktulpar,
When I was a young bride, we quarreled;
That was when my father lost my affection.
I, unfortunate Kanykei,
Went away weeping and weeping.
“He does not value me as much as one yearling,” I said,
And renounced him as my father.
Oh, Uncle, Khan Bakai,
My precious hair, thick as a saddle-girth,
Parted from my saint,
To that grim-faced scoundrel—
How did I, poor soul, go to him?
When my allotted death comes, I shall die;
Temir Khan, with his ways—
Uncle Bakai, how can I face him without pain?
Your kinsman by marriage, Temir Khan,
Was a man whose conduct cost me dearly.
Temir Khan knows no reason;
He was a miser over his livestock.
When I wept, he would not listen;
He did not value me as much as a yearling;
Such was the temper he possessed.
