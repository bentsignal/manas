Beneath him he had a thoroughbred steed;
The great blue-gray-bearded Manchu,
Though he had grown old and might die,
Still had strength enough to lay waste to the world.
The corners of his two eyes
Were like graves gaping open;
His broad forehead spread wide,
Like the seat of honor occupied by a rich man;
His upturned mustache
Was like the handle of a greased axe;
His huge, jutting nose
Was like the sheath of a selebe saber.
If you sit and ask of his renown:
Born in Outer Beijing,
Kongurbay had stirred such turmoil
Not only among the Chinese, but throughout the age.
Dust rose wherever he traveled;
At his voice, life fled.
He was the man who went forth to conflict,
That was the heedless warrior.
Seeing his courage, the khagan’s men
Carried out his spoken word.
Against the sudden coming of an enemy,
They made him the fortress of Outer Beijing.
Have you heard the news?
He slew whoever fought him;
The resourceful Kalmyk
Had cunning as well.
He was a master who knew the enemy’s stratagems,
And had power as well.
He was the champion displayed for all to see;
If you came to fight him,
He was the mightiest khan of the Kechil.
His wisdom was great, his venom strong—
Have you heard the news?
He leveled the ground where he fought.
The Kechil khan, the great swine,
The khan of Beijing, the foremost swine.
The great black horse that pranced
He sent among the Ong Soloon,
To have the judges inspect
What its flesh and form were like.
A golden saddle with arched pommel,
The doomed one took
And had placed on Algara.
Sixty wrestlers and countless Soloon,
Wondering what his plan might be,
The great white-bearded Manchu
Frightened all those people.
They fretted and wailed;
His stricken people cried aloud.
When he had reached old age,
They asked what would become of the noble hawk:
“The hairs of his beard are white;
It seems his time has come to depart on a journey.
Past eighty and nearing ninety,
Such is his condition now.
Unable to take a companion as escort,
Unable to take a vast host of ten thousand,
Alas, the poor old father
Seems to have grown aged.
Meeting by chance and in vain
To feud with blood-drinking Manas’s son—
Is it time for him to set out on his final journey?
Now that his beard is blue-gray,
The gray-maned wolf himself will surely not come.
Father, noble hawk, what shall I do?
Surely he will not see Beijing again.”
When the Kalmyk mounted to depart,
His nobles wept and his khan wept,
All that people wept,
The mass of people grieving.
“Alas, the poor brave man,
He has grown old,” they wept.
Whichever one you looked at,
Wept, consumed by personal grief.
“He was the precious khan we cherished;
May he return,” they wept.
