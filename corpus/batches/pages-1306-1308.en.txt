Kongurbay sent out the word.
When Kongurbay spoke thus,
The great Chinese multitude stirred.
The people of forty khans united
And rushed in without looking back.
Some with bricks, some with stones,
They prepared to build a fortress.
You could not tell if they were insects,
Nor tell if they were people,
Nor know if they were grass,
Nor know if they were a lake.
The Chinese people of forty khans
Arrived without looking back.
The bricks and stones in their hands
They set down, one apiece, as they passed.
Each took a double handful
From the ditch he had ordered,
Scooping it out as they passed.
Look how numerous the Chinese were!
They made the wall forty cubits high;
They made the ditch forty cubits deep.
Outside that ditch,
The two clans, Kuyku and Shiyku,
The Chinese ran around in a ring.
Poor Chubak and Almambet
Despaired of their wretched lives.
Then Almambet,
Who was worth many men in combat,
Who had struggled for kingship from the start,
Young goshawk Almambet—
The customs the Chinese practiced,
Had he not come knowing them?
“If the Chinese do this, it bodes no good;
They certainly will not spare us.
Dear Chubak, my soul’s friend,
Without wearying, against the Chinese,
Let us employ a stratagem
And preserve our lives this way.
Among unbelievers as thick as cattle’s hair,
Let us die when our appointed time comes.
Manas, the lion of the world—
Unless we two have died first,
How could we give him up alive?
Turn Kogala’s head,” he said.
“My Chubak, born a wolf-man,
Do not let their many champions climb;
Hold the mountain slope,” he said.
Azizkhan’s Almambet,
Sarala with lean, cowlike haunches,
Had he not saddled and brought him?
Cloudy by day, clear by night,
Disrupting the weather’s whirlwinds,
Without stint, his incantations
Had he not come reciting?
A mere Muslim
Your Almam had become, had he not?
Beijing of the forty gates—
The Karykan who ruled it
Had been your Almam, had he not?
Upon Karykan
Merciless trouble
Had he not come bringing?
“A weather stone is needed to save our lives,”
Your renowned champion Almam said.
Karykan’s weather stone,
Had he not brought it away?
Rattling in Kalmyk,
Babbling in Chinese,
Karykan’s weather stone,
Into a bowl of plain water,
He lifted and plunged.
Thus Almambet
Worked a mighty stratagem;
He cast a spell upon the weather.
Clouds thundered from the sky,
Rolling down toward the earth.
The face of heaven closed over;
The earth was shut within clouds.
Thus Almambet,
Sarala with lean, cowlike haunches,
Your precious lion tethered.
Turning summer’s day into winter,
The lightly clad Chinese
He made whimper like girls,
As your precious warrior worked weather magic.
Though rain fell, they massed together;
The many Chinese pressed upon them,
Preparing to seize them.
The people of forty khans boasted;
The mighty warrior Kongurbay
Gave orders to them all.
Once they heard the word,
They swarmed together like ants:
Beijing’s host as countless as sand.
Let us leave this here
And begin upon this other side.
With the twelve khans’ army,
Great Bakai, left in the rear camp,
Woke from sleep in alarm.
He turned onto his right side,
Hurriedly performed his prayers,
And feared for Khan Manas.
He saddled his gray battle steed,
Crying out in haste,
Calling for Ajybai:
“Come, Ajybai, bound to my soul!
Last night I had a dream;
I saw something to fear.
The Chinese massed as enemies;
Of the wolf-man, champion Manas,
Few days of life remained.
If my feeling is right, dear Ajybai,
With his long ears drooping,
Aibanboz must have stopped moving.
Upon the warrior Manas’s head,
The Chinese, sweeping over him like a flood,
Must have brought the Last Day.
From the dream I saw last night,
If my feeling is right, has he died?
Even if the warrior Manas has not died,
He must be as good as dead.
What can I tell you, dear Ajybai,
If we lose the warrior Manas...
Grant us a time of goodness!
If God ever created a living soul,
He was a lion born a leopard,
