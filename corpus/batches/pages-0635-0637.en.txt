My lion Manas, heed my words.
Its base and its stock,
Cast of lamb-liver steel—
Its like could not be found.
That peerless iron, Almabash,
Makes no distinction between far and near.
It would not fail for sixty days;
When they heated and forged it,
They twisted six grooves into it.
No man could find another like it;
The foes it met lay fallen.
When slung upon the body, that wondrous thing
Was a mirror upon the shoulder blades.
When turned against contending foes,
It was the solace of a life held in trust.
My blood-drinker, that you would be born
And come forth, I had known.
For the noble, fierce-maned one to take,
To bring slaughter upon the world—
My magnanimous one, for you yourself
I had been fitting it.
Its stock was cast and skillfully worked;
The Black Smith, David's friend,
That poor fellow made it.
Having its stock fashioned from tempered steel,
As the price for that wretched thing,
I had exactly a thousand young mares given.
I had the steel drawn slender,
Had it fired to split a target,
Had skilled hands fit it together,
Had mighty experts test it.
That formidable Akkelte—
I had its sight set with a jewel,
A sight that satisfied the heart.
Gold and silver together
I had fastened upon its side.
I had it wrapped in precious cloth
And kept where people would not see it.
If we find it, that is the treasure;
It will meet a fortunate hero,
That tempered steel whose like cannot be found.
A living gun, that wondrous thing:
When it sees the massed enemy,
Blood flows from its ears;
It cracks and fires of itself.
When the enemy draws near,
Its bullets flash as they fly forth;
Its voice bellows like a camel calf.
Its bullet striking squarely—
No enemy can withstand it.
Where it strikes is flame, a blazing fire;
No man can stand and face it.
If the fired bullet strikes straight on,
Not a handspan of bone can be found.
That peerless gun Akkelte—
I knew that it was alive.
For my fierce-maned Manas to take,
I had dedicated it to you!
Writing words in Arabic,
I placed an inscription in its middle;
I gave it the name Akkelte.
If my champion came into my hands,
For my impetuous Manas to take it,
Upon the enemy who came to face him
To heap distress and tribulation,
Of Beijing, untouched by Chaliyar,
For my fierce-maned one's strength to suffice
To seize its khanship—
I had been fitting it for this.
I had it wrapped in silk
And covered with leather.
Back then, riding my gray steed,
Unable to find you, my fierce-maned one,
When I was grieving greatly,
Back then, turning my gray steed's head,
With fire burning through my very innards,
When I could no longer bear it,
Where my cord was cut and my birth-clothes washed,
Where birth-blood fell, I had no land.
To know how I was faring,
I had no dear Kyrgyz people.
Sleeping, in my dream,
I saw Manas indistinctly.
‘Where shall I find him?’
I kept wandering in sorrow.
A fortress-house set across the way—
When my hawk came to mind,
My wretched life seemed less than a handspan.
Somehow to find Manas,
To attain my wish—
If I could not find Manas,
From this deceitful world
To depart in sacrificial death—
Without sparing my wretched life,
I rode far and wide.
What ears had heard of and eyes had seen—
I wandered through every land.
I brought hardship upon their game.
For six months, seven months, I myself
Went away on a long hunt.
Frenzied, chasing wild asses,
Crossing hills and rough ridges,
Treading many kinds of land,
Without a cloud the size of a knucklebone breaking away,
Without the mountain's edge appearing,
Beyond the reach of the eye,
The towering snowy mountains—
Where they led could not be known.
Finding no hollow the size of a saddlecloth,
Finding no hill the size of a knucklebone,
Pursuing wild asses, drawing close,
I wandered lost for six months.
By a pine growing on the plain,
I slaughtered a mare with a foal.
Not a crow gave a caw;
Not a raven gave a croak.
‘This land is deserted,’ I thought.
The sacred Akkelte
I put away in a hidden store.
When two years had passed,
Counting them in months,
When twenty-four months had passed,
As the people moved up to the highlands,
In the season when grazing blackens horses' tongues,
When the poppy grass turned red,
