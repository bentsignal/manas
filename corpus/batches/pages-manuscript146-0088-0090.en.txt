His army formed a surrounding host.
In his great host there were champions,
Those who saw them fell into grievous distress.
The reason for their lament was this:
That hero Chyngteñir,
Whose watching wrath was fierce,
From the multitude that could shatter forty,
A prodigy sprung from a wild boar,
His rage could tear hell apart,
No man sent against him returned,
Even the black-headed stag stood amazed,
He allowed no equal to challenge him,
In the eighteen thousand worlds,
No such courage had been heard of,
Among heroes, he was the foremost,
He was champion and khan alike,
He was the chosen one of fourfold strength.
He was khan of the countless Chinese,
A soul greater than so many others,
The hero above all men.
When he drank blood, he was never sated,
Countless myriads of troops did not daunt him,
When blades clashed, none could reach him,
No man could match his force.
In the span of his own life,
The world had brought forth no equal.
At his birth, as the tale tells,
The earth shook and awakened,
Saying, “He truly is Tengir,”
He was given a mighty name.
To that hero Chyngteñir,
What mortal sent could approach?
Who could gird himself and fight him alone?
Was any such place left in all the world?
Such was Chyngteñir’s nature,
The sun set at the sight of his power,
Forty cried out as lives fell,
The people scattered as bodies lay,
He became a star over the world.
Rising past the dawn and glowing,
Shimmering at the mouth of dawn,
The birches’ crowns whistled,
The branches at dawn bent low,
The face of the sun was outlined,
The fierce men clad in armor,
Those who went armored in stone,
Holding sway over a thousand households,
The khan’s valiant heralds,
Came out in ranks,
Before great Tengir,
Bowing low in homage,
The leaders of the people stood proud,
His narrowed eyes flashing fiercely,
