Your daring Chyng has become mighty.”
Thus Chyngteñir
Was entered into the chronicles,
And among the many people of China’s forty tribes
He became known as Chyngteñir.
No living soul could withstand his fury,
No people could match his stature,
And none dared cast an evil eye on him.
No one remained to stir up
Gossip, rumor, or strife.
Blue-gray like sharpened steel,
He grew keen and became lord of the nation.
He besieged and drove out
The hostile khagan, and became khan;
Whoever opposed him
Met his death there.
They properly placed the crown upon him,
And raised him onto the golden throne.
The two reins and single bridle
Were placed in Teñir’s hands,
Giving him rule over the khagan’s people.
At seven he was already full of ardor,
A giant among all the people of the earth.
Until he reached eight years,
He rode a stick and went on foot.
When Teñir reached nine,
He cast childhood away like a worn hide
And brought all Beijing beneath his command.
When he reached eleven,
His father Kite died.
When he reached twelve,
He buried his own father.
He made white deer serve like cattle,
And brought all Beijing beneath his command.
He had the tomb plated with yellow gold
And covered with cloth of gold;
He dug a resting place within the great mountain,
Excavating every feature in perfect measure,
A grave surpassing all others in the world.
He plated it with yellow gold,
Had the coiners praise their workmanship,
And the craftsmen fit everything together.
All around the great mountain
He enclosed it entirely with stone.
“So that no beast or living creature,
Not a single soul, may ever pass through;
And whoever ventures there unawares
May not remain alive,”
He set guards to watch it.
Surpassing all men in his deeds,
And following the Chinese rite
That beasts must not enter
And trample the place,
Lest they desecrate it,
Chyngteñir, whose hands knew
How to correct every flaw in the work,
Entrusted his father to warriors in jackets,
To pure men armored with stone,
To champions clad in mail,
And to many others,
Even to great tusked boars,
To guard his father.
“He truly is Teñir,” they said,
While old and young cried out in amazement.
Blessed by Kyzyr, he gained strength,
Seized the whole Chinese nation,
Drove them into a single hollow,
And packed them into a single pit.
When anyone died, he had the corpse
Cast into the fire and burned,
Enforcing his supreme decree.
Over the vast Chinese realm
And the many Kalmyk and Shibe peoples,
He became an absolute khan;
Whoever opposed him was slaughtered.
He was mounted upon the horse called Chyngteñir,
And Teñir remained to rule the nation.
