Since he came to this Altai,
Many years have passed.
This Burut's wealth
Has surpassed the world's.
Holding a feast when his wife gives birth,
Such is this Burut's boldness!
Slaughtering mares without distinction,
Slaughtering all four kinds of stock alike,
In this city of Kakan,
Who has slaughtered such livestock?
Those who went to his feast were sated;
In this city of Kakan,
There is no man like the Burut.
His liquor and his kymyz
Were too much for the world to hold.
Ulungtung, Dang-Shang, Bakburchun,
We have seen this people's rulers.
He devised plans no one had seen;
Of the jacket-wearing Chinese,
Above even the strongest,
This Burut held his feast.
People settled at his feast;
The Black Kalmyks' and Manchus'
Children had rump portions tied on for them.
Full-grown livestock were slaughtered for his feast;
If you ask the child's name,
He was named Chong Jindi.
Holding a feast when his wife gives birth,
Devising plans no one had seen,
This Burut has gone mad!
In the great, paradise-like city of Beijing,
Our ruler Esenkan—
Let us send word to him.
Of this Burut Kyrgyz who slaughtered livestock,
Let us tell the news.”
From the Black Kalmyks and Manchus,
All their people gathered.
To Esenkan of Beijing,
To the kakan's stronghold,
To the swarming Chinese people,
To his house ringed with fortifications,
To his tightly secured city,
To Esenkan of Beijing,
The Kalmyk people, their goodwill gone,
Gave six swift horses,
Gave a letter the size of a palm,
And a kettledrum made of copper.
Gripping its handle and swinging it,
To the kakan's city
They sent a great executioner.
Then that great executioner,
Traveling day and night,
Riding far without sparing his horses,
Fording surging waters,
Crossing many looming mountains,
Hurrying along the road,
Riding far without sparing his horses,
Traveling many days between,
Appearing through the haze,
To the settlement of Outer Beijing,
That executioner arrived,
At Esenkan's settlement.
Outer Beijing with forty gates—
Its jaysang guarding the gate,
One of those at the entrance,
Its monastery where monks lived—
Do not ask about that monastery.
His ox grows old, he himself dies;
Of the person who has died,
They measure out and divide his livestock.
They consecrate his lead goat;
The horse he rides is sacred.
Look at this people's practices:
He does not consort with women.
He saddles an unbroken horse;
Look at this people's practices:
A great idol made of bronze,
They bow to it, calling it God.
Beside Esenkan,
Some thousand monks stand.
That great executioner who had arrived,
Hurrying, scurrying, stumbling,
As if a demon had struck his wife,
As if he fled with pursuers close behind,
Before Esenkan
He began to stir up trouble,
Came in telling the wonder:
“Of the Burut driven into exile,
He came and settled in Altai.
Wandering from his people,
He became our subject.
His heart swelled and grew at ease;
He grew rich mainly by sowing grain.
When that Jakyp grew rich,
He devised plans no one could conceive,
Slaughtering livestock like an emperor,
He held a feast when his wife gave birth.”
As the great executioner spoke,
Esenkan grew angry:
“To hold a feast when one has a child
Is the custom of all peoples.
That Burut driven into exile,
If his four kinds of stock thrive,
Damn your fathers' graves, Kalmyk people,
If he came among you and grew rich,
Should not a man with stock slaughter his stock?
At any time there is some in the ditch—
Should not the people name the child?
Rich Jakyp has livestock;
‘He slaughtered stock like an emperor,’ you say—
Why have you become so frenzied?
Should he not slaughter the stock in his hands?
Should those at his feast not eat their fill?
Should the people not gather
And name the child?!
That Burut who has a child,
Will he look his livestock in the eye?
‘He slaughtered much livestock,’ you say—
Is it right to come to me with that?
‘A subject slaughtered his livestock’—
Why should I grow upset?
‘My Burut slaughtered his livestock’—
Why should I be jealous?
If my subject holds a feast,
Can a man who resents it prosper?
You ate your fill at his feast and went mad;
You rushed here greedily.
Can one called a man prosper so?
Can a human being be like you?”
He drove the executioner away,
Humming without a care,
Esenkan lay down.
The words the man had brought,
He did not value as much as a trouser-leg.
Then the hero Manas,
Day after day passed,
The appointed time arrived.
Mighty Manas reached seven years;
His given name was Chong Jindi.
Of that dark-maned sultan,
Who knew he was a lion?
Reaching eight, he grew lively;
He did not follow the proper path.
