To anyone entering with joy,
It revealed a vision of paradise.
My poor elder sister Kanykei
Had taken a weaned lamb, its sweet head,
And boiled it tender.
She sliced rump meat and set out neck fat;
Upon the tablecloth
She laid seventy kinds of food.
The very sight filled the heart;
All those foods
She set out for Semetey to eat.
Through her thoughtful arrangements,
Every one of her chests
She filled with treasures of yellow gold.
She has costly goods and resources;
She has red gold as well.
Even if she faced hardship for forty years,
This whole Bukhara,
My precious elder sister Kanykei
Has the means to buy.
When my brother-in-law Manas was alive,
Great heaps of wealth were gathered.
In Bukhara, Temir Khan and Shaatemir,
Koloykan and Ismail—
The wealth of these four,
If we gathered every bit of it,
Would not equal even one portion of my sister’s.
When my brother-in-law Manas was alive,
What a woman my elder sister must have been!
My brother-in-law who married this sister of mine—
What complaint could he have against God?
Of the ravine running down this hillside,
Would that I became the spring!
Of Manas, who has gone to the other world,
Would that I, poor soul, became
The only child won through such suffering!
This Kara-Kyr stretched out before us—
Ah, would that it became my line for tethering foals!
This precious elder sister Kanykei—
Would that, instead of my sister,
She became my mother!
If I were Manas’s son,
If I were my sister’s camel calf,
There would be heaps of gathered wealth;
Against Almighty God,
Ah, I would have no complaint at all.
From my generous brother-in-law Manas’s
White loins, would that I had sprung!
My precious elder sister Kanykei—
Would that I had kicked my way out of her womb!
For Manas to ride for six months,
Would that I became his swift steed!
Of this precious, unfortunate mother,
The only son won through such suffering—
Would that I became her lamp!
If I were Manas’s son,
If I were my sister’s only child,
My heart would be content at the thought;
Against Almighty God
I would have no complaint at all!
This unlucky elder sister of mine
Is not one who should be left childless;
How could she have been left childless?
My brother-in-law gone to the other world
Is not a khan who should be left without a calf;
How could he have been left without a calf?
What God decrees
Leaves one helpless.
This sister of mine who weeps—
By my stately brother-in-law Manas,
How was she left ‘childless’?”
His heart smoldered with grief;
Unable to bear looking on,
Semetey grieved.
Saying he would go hawking, Semetey
Rode out from Bukhara,
Semetey grieving still more,
His precious eyes, like a saker falcon’s,
Filled with tears without blinking.
Mournful, unable to take heart,
Semetey turned back
And burst into deep, resounding sobs.
Once, Kanykei had come in mourning,
Semetey had fled to Bukhara.
Kanykei had come a widow,
Semetey had come a wanderer.
Plundering riches, they prospered,
The fierce one had entered Bukhara and grown,
A lion champion upheld by his patron,
His twelve limbs all in proportion,
Manas, who had gone to the other world—
He had become a warrior to match him.
An orphan, he had reached maturity,
Somehow found his place among the people,
Into Temir Khan’s city
He had wandered and grown mighty.
To the city of Bukhara,
The boy had fled and grown great.
What was severed had been joined;
What was scattered had been gathered.
The valiant boy Semetey
Had not yet donned the gift coat no arrow could pierce;
The valiant woman Kanykei,
He did not yet know the poor soul was his mother.
Until he reached twelve,
He had studied his lessons in Bukhara.
When his belly was full of tea,
When he had finished his meal,
Having eaten neck fat with rump meat,
Having eaten belly fat and honey,
Gnawing sugar like snow,
Sipping his black tea,
When the tablecloth was gathered,
When he had just begun to pray,
The Quran’s “Say: He is God, the One,”
He recited devoutly and dedicated;
He recited many chapters by heart,
The chapter called Tabbat
With astonishing speed.
His sister stood weeping bitterly,
To Manas gone to the other world,
To his spirit, dedicating it.
When he held his palms out,
When he had just given the blessing,
Your dear sister-in-law Kanykei,
Speaking softly in distress,
To her own son, the poor soul,
Pleaded, calling him little brother:
“My valiant little brother, listen.
When your valiant brother-in-law died,
When this head of mine met suffering,
Breaking up our countless riches,
When the foolish idiots divided them,
Upon this looming, dark head of mine,
My bloodied face was torn.
Not dying, my wretched soul grieving,
Bereft of your brother-in-law,
Finding nowhere to flee,
Finding no people to stand by me,
Showing how defenseless my widowhood was,
When they laid hands upon my court,
The twelve doors and gates
They flung wide open.
When the fools plundered it,
When they robbed my riches,
When they wrought utter havoc,
Revealing my distress,
My venerable old mother,
I brought her from Talas,
Carrying her upon my shoulders.
My river of mighty grandeur,
My venerable old mother,
Has reached ninety-five,
My saintly one, now so advanced in years.
If you would see her, my heart would be content,
The venerable old woman of pure faith—
I have no sacred refuge but my mother.
This venerable old mother of mine,
My darling, sacred mother,
The shelter above my head,
From the Kyrgyz with their sovereign,
Carrying her upon my shoulders,
I brought her, my good fortune.
If you would see her, my heart would be content;
I have no sacred refuge but my mother.
My pennon upon the spear,
From the two tribes, Argyn and Kyrgyz,
I deliberately brought my mother,
The gift I brought with me.
A ship sailing upon the water,
Your sultan brother-in-law’s mother,
Her brow gives strength when seen.
If you would see her, my heart would be content;
For me, your bereaved, unfortunate sister,
She is the sacred refuge I look toward.
The cherished jewel the courser left behind,
For your luckless sister,
Who looks to her as a banner,
She is the mirror above her head.
When I see her, my belly is full;
I have no solace, no people.
Oh, little brother, listen:
I have no sacred refuge but my mother.
She bore one son, a lion champion;
She bore one daughter, a warrior.
The son she bore, hero Manas,
Was chief of so many people;
The daughter she bore, Kardygach,
Was guardian of the people of the world.
When the khan of Kakan stirred,
As stars of equal standing,
Meeting the maiden Saikal,
Becoming her companion,
She stood beside her, matching her prowess.
Kardygach and the maiden Saikal,
These two came out for single combat;
For seventy days they fought with spears.
Many a giant the size of a house died;
Swift runners on foot, too heavy for horses to bear—
There was a mighty upheaval.
She was a beauty among women,
Kardygach, daughter of Khan Zhakyp;
Khan Saikal, daughter of Karacha,
Was a mighty maiden alongside her.
Khan Chubak had sent a letter,
And Kyrgylchal, chief of the forty.
Offended when they were blamed,
Forty tribes of Chinese, many Manchus,
Had come on this account.
Thus, making ready,
Alooke’s Kongurbay,
With cotton belt and broad boots,
The monks’ khan, that warrior swine,
Six thousand Chinese, countless governors,
Their champion Zholoi, Ylama,
The Manchus’ khan Ezkara,
The countless Chinese with myriads of wrestlers,
Heard the news from the written letter:
The forty companions had fled together;
Khan Manas had been left alone;
They heard that things had gone badly.
When the unbelievers came swarming,
The maiden Saikal stood on watch,
And Kardygach went on patrol.
White falconets, red banners—
Against the two of them they raised,
Kongurbay and countless Chinese,
A shouting tumult.
White standards, red banners—
The Chinese beyond, countless governors,
Against this maiden raised
A shouting tumult.
