What has happened to the people?”
He was the master of the land and nation,
The giant champion of Beijing,
The khan of its tribal people,
The mainstay within their nest.
The moon had waned and the year rolled on;
“Oh, my child, how many days have passed since then!
The might of one champion
Was equal to that of countless warriors.
He was khan of the forty tribes,
Foremost among the spearmen,
A lion among marksmen.
Great Bilgich and I
Were like ravens croaking as they seize their feast;
Among the forty tribes of this Chinese nation,
He was the son of my own kin…”
Saying this, he stopped;
Look at that infernal man—
He was lamenting great Bilgich.
At that very moment great Bilgich
Entered through the gate.
His Kerik horse wore mourning,
And poor Bilgich was wounded.
His face was pale and haggard;
Good heavens, his figure and bearing,
His form made for the saddle,
His heroic stature and presence—
Far worse than before,
Bilgich’s appearance was utterly ruined.
His color gone, he reeled in confusion,
His power spent and body wasted,
All his strength exhausted.
His mustache bristled hair by hair,
His beard stuck out strand by strand.
His eyes had sunk into their sockets;
His parched lips never closed,
And thick spittle gathered there.
With a life scarcely alive,
With only such strength as kept him from expiring,
His vigor exhausted and power gone—
When they looked upon brave Bilgich,
Black blood had drained from his body.
At times he could regain his speech,
At times he could not speak;
At times his eyes could see,
At times they could not.
His lower lip sagged open—
Great indeed was the torment he had suffered;
His upper lip was drawn away,
And he had returned halfway among the dead.
His fine mustache drooped,
His dim eyes bulged;
He groaned and coughed,
Like one about to depart for the other world.
Great Bilgich, grievously afflicted,
Beat his lungs with every breath.
Beijing’s mightiest boar,
His spirit narrowed and burning with pain,
Thus had great Bilgich arrived;
No soul could endure his groaning—
Such was the state of great Bilgich.
When Bilgich appeared like this,
They had the great doors shut,
And slaughtered eighty black bulls
As sacrificial offerings.
Red-gowned and slender-waisted,
Beautiful women with dainty feet—
For women’s hands were said to bring relief—
Were set to tend him.
They shouted for the skilled healers
And summoned all the physicians.
The physicians of China,
Those who diagnosed by feeling the pulse,
The most accomplished masters of medicine,
Their experts standing forty rows deep—
Now the great physicians examined him.
“Alas, this Burut foe is formidable!
How many men among us
Have been ruined by him?” they said.
