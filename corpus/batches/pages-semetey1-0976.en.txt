Toward Buurul, standing riderless,
The bewildered Semetey
Ran and mounted in haste.
That white swan would not release
Kojosán’s falcon;
It clenched the short leash,
Tucked it beneath its wing,
Beat the water with its tail,
And thrashed the dark lake with its wings.
It went farther downward,
Then sped away like the wind;
When he looked, it vanished from sight.
The great white swan was escaping,
And no one could tell which it was.
Your young, blue-maned prince
Had a true courser beneath him,
Which raced with a flying bird;
The white reins steeped in tanning liquor
Contracted within his palm.
He pursued the white swan
Upon the true tulpar Buurul.
The white swan’s uncanny power
Had carried the falcon away.
With a camel-like forelock and swept-back mane,
Buurul was a true courser;
He laid his ears flat like a wild ass,
And with no escape in sight,
Churned the earth like a whirlwind.
He gathered himself in bounding strides,
And surged as though diving into a hollow.
From a ridge curved like a saddle bow,
He bent low and entered beyond it.
They drew close;
Your swaggering madman
Raised the patterned spear in his hand
And cast it badly.
Seeing that escape was impossible,
What could poor Chürök do?
It farted with a crack and shat with a snap,
And cast down a walking fog.
When he looked, nothing could be seen,
And no one knew where it was.
Clouds split open in the sky,
And rain poured onto the earth;
Clouds wheeled across the heavens,
And eight months’ hail poured down.
A squall went shrieking,
Through ravine, slope, and plain;
A foul fog roared,
And a walking mist descended and blinded the eyes.
Rain covered every lowland,
Hail covered every upland,
And the sun was hidden from view.
The falcon was carried away,
And that Chürök escaped.
Nar Buurul opened his stride;
The tiger, his father Manas,
Seemed to have died that very day.
The tiger grieved,
Having lost Akshunkar;
His anger rose and his face darkened,
And his dark rage swelled.
He shouted in fury,
Calling Kulchoro and Kanchoro,
Summoning the two of them.
His bellow split heads,
His cry split stones;
His eyes blazed like a lion’s,
And bloodlust truly seized him.
He blackened in his dark fury,
And when the exhausted prince stood there,
Kulchoro and Kanchoro,
The two choros, raced one another,
Their stirrups striking together;
They fought the bits
Of their two equal tulpars.
When they drew near the prince,
He poured out bitter reproaches,
