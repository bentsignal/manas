When they put betrothal earrings on me,
When they bound me to marriage,
I was still a little girl,
My hair not yet long enough to braid,
I, your unfortunate mother,
Then a beauty among women,
A maiden of fourteen.
When your wolf-maned father came,
Manas the leopard, towering proudly,
Pressed me more and more,
A gray pup the size of a fist,
Whose eyes had not yet opened,
Your father, Khan Manas,
Gave to me, your wretched mother.
Your khan father Manas swore an oath,
He made a solemn promise:
“If you raise Kumaik for me,
I shall surely show you every care;
Throughout all the lands within my reach,
I shall make you queen.
I shall not lay a whip across you,
Nor take another wife to rival you.
I shall not show my baser nature,
Nor let a harsh word touch you.
Neglectful care will bring disgrace;
Kumaik is entrusted to you.
Hang an amulet about his neck,
Take good care of Kumaik.
If you care badly for my dog,
You will see the foolishness
Of your husband, wolf-maned Manas.
If I, Manas, remain alive,
And this Kumaik here—
If you take poor care of him,
This very Bukhara's
Treasures I shall plunder.
For this dog Kumaik,
I shall wreak great havoc.”
Your father often said this;
When he came walking slowly,
The gray pup the size of a fist
He handed over as a sacred trust,
Giving it to me, your wretched mother.
When I took it in my hands and looked,
It had little prospect of surviving.
If the hound were carelessly raised,
Bringing black night upon my wretched head,
It was likely to die.
“My resources will utterly fail,” I thought,
“Our deaths will come together,” I thought.
“If this pup is left untended,
It will starve to death,” I thought.
I fed it milk from my mouth;
Thus I raised the hound.
I fed it meat with my hand,
I spent my days in anxious care.
Without recoiling in disgust,
I made my tongue a teat.
I levied tribute upon Bukhara
And had a young mare slaughtered.
The young mare's sweet rib fat
I cut away with my teeth,
And let it suck from my tongue;
Thus I raised the hound.
Without resting on my elbow,
Without a moment's sleep,
Thus I raised the hound.
Without rest throughout the day,
Without sleep throughout the night,
Thus I raised the hound.
Winter passed and spring arrived;
My wits wandered, my strength failed.
Fanged beasts were deadly enemies of the hound.
At my father Temir Khan's great court,
I had a pit of forty ells dug.
I had Kumaik placed inside;
Forty hunters from Bukhara—
Over Kumaik
I had them stand guard.
Keeping lions from seizing him,
That Kumaik you have seen,
Keeping him from needless death,
I barely managed to preserve him.
When six full months had passed,
The Kumaik I was raising,
When the time came to prowl,
Would bite and fight even a tiger.
When the time came to play,
He proved a blazing ember;
No fanged beast was so unruly.
The steel collar around his neck
He bit and tore apart.
I, your unfortunate, luckless mother—
Before I could cry out and protect him,
Into the saxaul embers
He struck his tail.
His tail touched the coals;
Two fingerbreadths of it were burned.
God had taken away my sense.
That day your valiant, princely father,
Furious and looming proudly,
His nose bulging like a leather flask,
Arrived on a bridegroom's visit.
Inspecting Kumaik,
He saw the tail and raised a quarrel,
Finding fault with my wretched self:
“Disobeying what I said,
Failing to value me—
God who strikes has struck you!” he said.
“She has grown insolent!” he said.
“The precious creature I told her to raise,
She has made a wretched pup!” he said.
Seizing my arm, he twisted me round,
And kicked me beneath the arm.
Your khan father, acting the lion,
When his swinging foot struck home,
All twelve of my ribs
Were jolted by the sudden blow.
Two ribs took the blow squarely
And were utterly broken.
I was one who somehow did not die.
They blew breath into me, a wretched soul;
I barely recovered in six months.
I did not snarl, wretched as I was,
Nor make a fuss about my face and brow.
Like some bad wife,
I did not answer your father back.
I bore my suffering myself;
I spoke no words to wound your father deeply.
Because of what had happened,
Because of troublesome Kumaik,
Laying misfortune upon my neck,
For the sake of a single dog Kumaik,
Your father ceased to share my bed.
I camped along the river valley.
Because of Kumaik,
Your khan father would not lie beside me;
For six months I was a neglected wife.
I made a coat no bullet could pierce
For your father, a saint in his lifetime;
My foal, now let me tell you of Kumaik:
Though he would speak, he has no tongue.
Kumaik, left by your father,
Is wiser than other beasts.
Crouching like a quail,
His tears pouring down,
Kumaik kept mourning.
For six days he ate nothing;
Kumaik wasted away.
At the place where your father was washed,
For sixty days his voice never ceased:
Kumaik kept howling.
Not rising to go prowling,
Kumaik remained crouched down.
His tears welling up,
Kumaik let them fall.
Mourning your father,
Kumaik wasted away.
“When even a beast is like this,
How can a person stand it?” he said.
No mortal stood indifferent,
When he saw Kumaik,
That valiant Bakai of yours wept.
Across the crests of six ridges,
When we fled this way,
To my bitter sorrow,
Kumaik was left behind
In the hands of the six villains.
My words to God were a lament.
To that old wretch Abyke,
Little Kumaik
Had some attachment.
He must have tricked and caught him,
And taken him out hunting.
When set on a bear, he would not bite;
He would not serve faithfully.
He must have lowered his head to the ground
And lain there howling.
“His dog is an enemy too; it will never prosper,” he said.
“It is not as when Manas was alive,” he said.
“The man who takes it along will find no peace,” he said.
“It does not serve as it did when Manas was alive,” he said.
Tying him by the collar to train him,
Dragging him under the whip,
They must have left him mottled red.
They must have beaten him with blows,
And treated him with a dog's abasement.
When the people moved uphill,
“May his strength fail like this,” they said.
“May his death come like this,” they said.
They must have fed him spoiled lungs.
“It is not as when Manas was alive,” they said.
Poor Kumaik must have suffered such abuse.
When the people moved downhill,
“May his resources fail like this,” they said.
“May his death come like this,” they said.
They pounded the barley
And must have fed him gruel made from its bran.
The bran must have pierced his lungs.
This year Kumaik
Must have become burdened with anxiety.
They must have left him mottled red,
Tormenting and beating him.
Kumaik, wiser than a human,
Must have lowered his head to the ground.
This year the creature
Must have counted up the years.
“Since the wolf-maned leopard died,
Twelve years have passed,” he thought.
“My orphan, left by my friend Manas,
Has reached twelve years,” he thought.
“This year the orphan lad
Has reached the time to come,” he thought.
“The one God
Has reached the time to grant my wish,” he thought.
“My furrowed brow will clear,
My anxious suffering will ease;
God Most High
Is about to grant my wish,” he thought.
“My orphan, left by the magnanimous hero,
Is about to come,” he thought.
He must have lowered his head to the ground.
This year Kumaik
Must have counted up the years,
And crossed the little mountain pass,
