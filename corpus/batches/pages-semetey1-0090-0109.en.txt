And four yearling horses,
Slit this way and that.
The ladies who had come in anguish
Saw it plainly,
Tugging at his leg in distress,
Pleading with their uncle who had hunted,
With Khan Bakai.
Knowing their hunger,
Precious old Bakai
Cut up the joints without delay;
Your resourceful uncle butchered the game.
The travel kettle from the saddle straps
On a hearth of three stones
Your sister-in-law Kanykei set.
Rump and sweet, fatty meat
She put on to boil.
Your widows ate their fill.
The marrow from the bones
He knocked out for sucking
And gave to Semetey.
Rekindling their extinguished fire,
Your ruler, old Bakai,
Brought their dead souls back to life.
He gathered what was scattered,
Joined what was broken.
Nourishing their wasted bodies,
He filled their hungry bellies.
Your uncle, old Bakai,
The saint who had seen everything,
Dry meadowsweet and dry serviceberry,
Firewood that gave no smoke,
Uprooted whole.
Enough to last seven days,
He prepared their firewood.
Enough for a generous month,
He prepared their meat.
The lion turned his head;
To unfortunate Kanykei
He offered counsel:
“Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Do not pick up Semetey
And walk away to boil my misery.
All the provisions made ready for you—
Do not scatter them and boil my misery.
Setting your face toward Kara Khan,
Do not flee and boil my misery!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
You were a slavewoman built like a hero,
You were a dark-hearted child.
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
You were a soul of sacred power.
Hear my words.
Unless I die, I shall return;
Wait to look into my eyes!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
If you refuse to hear me,
My child, I shall know your humanity.
Semetey, whom I cradle—
If you leave without showing me my only one,
I shall wander, sobbing and wailing.
If what is told is true,
On the coming Day of Judgment,
I shall seize your collar and cry out,
Demanding him from you!
If, after taking your words to heart,
I leave with a lie,
You will know my foolishness.
Saying, ‘You went away with a lie,
You left me in the wilderness,
You brought me to ruin this way,’
When Judgment comes,
My foal, make me a black donkey and ride me!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Tie up the hair at your nape.
Worry for me seven days.
If I remain away beyond seven days,
Shoot gazelle, eat, and go.
Say, ‘Saintly Bakai, the accomplished one,
To whom seven saints gave their hands,
Must have died,’ and go.
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
If I remain away beyond six days,
Scatter all your provisions and leave.
Saying, ‘My lion uncle has died,’
Flee toward Kara Khan, your father's home!
Go by a safe road.
Saying, ‘My lion Bakai has died,’
Abandon hope of me and go!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Climb a ridge and pause.
Saying, ‘My hero Bakai has died,’
Lament me there before you leave!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
If by chance I fail to return,
Lie low by day and flee by night!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Do not linger in grief; flee on each day!
Do not reveal your widowhood.
You were accomplished, Kanykei;
Do not let your tongue abuse God.
When you flee to Bukhara,
Do not reveal your widowhood.
Do not let a bird devour the swan.
This Semetey, whom my companion left,
Do not let them call a fugitive boy!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Until twelve years have passed,
I shall have no peace with those destroyers!
I shall take my caravan from Ken-Kol.
If you bring the orphan to show me,
From those swaggering dogs,
Unless I die, I shall take revenge.
I shall take my caravan from Talas;
Making a spectacle before my people,
Unless I die, I shall take revenge.
Until Semetey turns twelve,
I shall plunder their possessions.
In this very Ken-Kol
I shall wreak pitiless slaughter.
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
I shall raid their racers from the summer pasture.
Unless I die, you will hear
Of the havoc Bakai has wrought!
Until my orphan reaches twelve,
I shall plunder their possessions.
Upon those reeking dogs
I shall inflict thorough slaughter,
Taking great revenge!
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
I shall raid their mares from the tethering line.
I shall make the villain pay
For his remotest wrongs!
Not their worthless ones, but the slaves'
Great men I shall seize.
If God grants it, Kanykei,
Making a sacrificial example upon your road,
I shall offer the dogs in sacrifice!
Those raging slaves
I shall trample underfoot.
If I, the old man, remain alive,
I shall scatter the dogs' four kinds of livestock!
You whom Manas left as a precious trust,
Until I return again,
Until I see your fair face,
You were a soul of sacred power—
My precious mother, farewell!
Travelling seven days on the road,
Until I return to see you,
Until I see your accomplished face,
Beauty among the dark-complexioned,
Radiant one among Muslims,
My child Kanykei, farewell.
Kyrka-Tash of broad Talas—
I shall trot down Kyrka-Tash.
If a stray bullet strikes from a ridge,
I shall behold Judgment.
If I do not behold Judgment,
If my death does not come,
In seven days I shall return without delay;
My orphan, I shall see you.
The road to Bukhara is long.
Let me prepare a coat that will keep you from freezing.
Let me prepare, my child Kanykei,
Your clear and open road.
Writing my letter straight to Bukhara,
Unless I die, let me return with
A steed that will not tire
Though you travel six months without rest.
Let me prepare your food and milk.
Let me give you, my child Kanykei,
A horse to ride to Bukhara.
The seven khans upon the earth,
When word reaches them,
Loaded seventy camels with cloth.
Kanykei, when we went to fetch you,
From your father Temir Khan's city
You came forth, my foal,
Riding a bay pacer in splendor.
Now, parted from Manas,
Your breast slashed open,
Grieving and weeping, my foal,
How can you go on foot,
Weeping in sorrow, stumbling along?
To your father Kara Khan's city
We drove livestock to arrange the marriage,
Bowed in homage and feasted on fat,
Loaded eighty-one camels with cloth,
Clothed you in a fitted coat,
Mounted you upon a racing steed.
From your father's royal city,
My foal, you came radiant.
Now that Kojosan Manas has died,
You have no food or fat in hand,
Not even a thin yearling beneath you.
How will you go on foot,
Fleeing, weeping, stumbling along?
From your father the sovereign khan's city
You came, my foal,
Shining in your coat of mail.
With no filly or yearling beneath you,
My child, how have you gone stumbling now?
Along Uch-Koshoi's lower road
Let me trot.
If my death has come, Kanykei,
Let me behold the other world.
Fighting with spears, breaking the ranks,
If my fate has come, let me die.
Unless my fate comes and I die,
Let me return within six days.”
So spoke Khan Bakai,
Shaking hands and saying farewell.
Their hearts were torn apart;
Tears poured down.
The widow cried out weeping.
When Khan Bakai said he would go,
As though his father were departing,
Unable to find any peace,
The orphan too sobbed bitterly.
Chyiyrdy grieved and wept,
Giving Bakai her solemn charge:
“My hiding place is a stone cave;
Semetey is my hope.
Hero Bakai, my strength to rely upon,
Hear my words clearly.
If God grants a clear road,
My Bakai, return and look into my eyes.
Survey the junction of Ken-Kol and return,
Saintly Bakai, Kojosan.
Keep Semetey in your thoughts—
Your only one, your orphan.
Let me be your sacrifice, Khan Bakai!
Do not charge off in exhaustion
To the six villains; return.
We remain in the wilderness.
Khan Bakai, come back within six days!
You were an impetuous hero.
Khan Bakai, return without scouting Talas's junction.
We remain in a cave in the earth.
Accomplished Bakai the leopard,
Come back within seven days!
If you stay beyond seven days,
Grieving and weeping without cease,
We shall abandon hope of you.
We shall scatter the wild sheep's meat
And walk away weeping in exhaustion.
Oh, Khan Bakai, my foal,
We shall watch the hills,
We shall grieve for six days.
If you stay beyond six days,
We shall scatter the wild ram's meat.
Despairing of you, the renowned one,
Oh, Khan Bakai, my foal,
We shall wander away over the ridge.
The spring that flows from the hills,
Do not forget, Khan Bakai—
The orphan Magnanimous Manas left behind!”
So Chyiyrdy spoke,
Showing her widow's plight.
Before the accomplished lord Bakai,
The child, solid as a tombstone,
She made wave his limbs and laugh.
Khan Bakai took him as his hope,
Saying, ‘The morning star my lord left,
Our willow growing beside the river,
The only child Manas left behind.’
Saying this, Khan Bakai—
Allah's sun clouded over in the sky.
His heart burned in bitter grief;
Sixty cares came into his head.
His tongue tied, he could not speak;
His heart turned sick.
Unable to keep looking at the orphan,
The poor man turned his horse's head.
Entrusting him as a precious charge,
Khan Bakai set out toward Talas.
The banner fluttered in his hand.
The gold-cheeked battle-axe
Glittered at his wrist.
Blue boots stood stiff upon his feet;
A blue spear stood erect in his hand.
His fearsome sword hung at his side.
If you considered your uncle Bakai,
He appeared an enormous mountain.
Blue flame came from his mouth,
Hissing here and there.
His precious teeth, broad as doors,
Crunched as though grinding millet.
Changing aspect with each step,
He muttered against Talas's six villains.
His face was like oiled wheat;
His eyes were like dark mist.
As he rode, curses broke from him;
He seemed a life-taking demon.
At a careless glance, a thousand men;
Look closely, and he was one.
Lucky when sent ahead—
Such was your uncle, hero Bakai.
Behind you, worth a host of troops—
Such was your champion, Lion Bakai.
Dying of sorrow,
That was Bakai's time of grief.
That was the whiteness of his beard.
Without sparing his precious life,
Your fierce Leopard Bakai
Was setting out on his journey.
Concealing his frame, vast as Ken-Kol,
On a day of grief and hardship,
Khan Bakai rode in sorrow.
Having sent his Semetey into flight,
His wings broken,
He thought, ‘How can I stay alive?’
That was his time of grief.
Parted from Kanykei,
Whom he had relied upon,
Mighty Bakai wished to die,
But could not take his own life.
Unable to bear it, he howled and wept.
Ak Chybyk upon his broad shoulder,
A spear glittering in his hand,
The gray steed beneath him
He lashed now on one side, now the other,
Spurring on this side and that,
Without looking around,
Without sparing life at all,
Without surveying his surroundings,
Then Bakai the leopard
Spared his life not a hair's breadth.
Khan Bakai's steed
Had lowered its head to the earth.
The wind could not match its trot;
It had broken into full flight.
No beast could match its stride;
Its gallop had become extraordinary.
Tossing its forelock to the sky,
Plunging its hooves into the earth,
The black steel bit
Clattering in its throat,
Its tail, like strands of silk,
Lashing its flanks,
Its mouth gaping wide,
White foam mixed with blood
Splashed upon its breast.
Its feet thundered on the ground;
The black steel bit
Clashed in its throat.
When it ran downhill,
Its forelegs struck like a deer's.
When it met rises and descents,
Its mane and tail streamed out.
When it reached the level plain,
It raced the flying birds.
The white reins in knotted braid
Bakai pulled with both hands;
Both his arms grew numb.
Jerking like a woman whose husband had died,
That immeasurably swift steed—
He wrestled with its mouth.
The ground it trod flew into fragments;
Clods as large as little huts
Whistled above its head.
Tearing up the face of the earth,
Racing on the gray steed,
Fierce Bakai the leopard,
The saint who had seen so much,
As dusk fell and the sun set,
While the first people lay quiet,
Spurred toward Talas
And entered in the night.
Broad Budurmak valley—
He searched its corner.
Finding no horses in the sheltered hollow,
He turned aside.
Through Tekechi Khan's and Shygai Khan's lands
He rode, trampling onward.
The broad valley of Zhylgynduu,
He searched that broad valley.
“Vanish with your horses!
Have I become one who merely smiles?”
He grew furious.
The gray steed beneath him
He struck with the whip.
Its flesh split like a sheep's;
The gray steed under the lash
Stretched its back like a hare.
At the mountain's final spur,
At the blunt jutting point,
Bakai pressed onward
To Kara-Buura and Chatkal.
When the Kyrgyz lay at ease,
When Khan Manas lived,
Strong winds and breezes
Would pass across the hills;
The great mottled-headed herds
Would come in to rest.
Bakai rode through,
But finding no horses there,
He grew furious.
To Kabak and Ak-Chiy
He drove his horse and reached them.
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshteks—
To Zhamgyrchy's great headquarters
He rode, trampling onward.
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshteks
Had donned an iron coat
And mounted Telkyzyl.
Mottled-headed horses filled the hills,
Khan Manas's countless horses,
Thoroughbreds and chargers mingled.
When they had plundered the possessions,
When the six villains came and wrought havoc,
Extinguishing the fire he had kindled,
Erasing Manas's name,
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshteks
Climbed a hill and shouted,
Raising only the battle cry, “Kobosh!”
He took possession of the horses,
Acting the master from outside.
A whole otter pelt
He had slit apart
And staked upon the knucklebone game.
His nose bulged like a leather bottle.
(Claiming ownership of the horses,
He swelled as though their master.)
When you looked at Zhamgyrchy,
He swelled like a slave whose master had died.
When Bakai came to the bluff,
He shouted in anger,
Calling, “Who are you?”
“I shall slaughter Zhamgyrchy,
I shall put him to death,
I shall gouge out both his eyes,
Rip his belly open,
And the dark blood within
I shall swallow by handfuls to my fill!”
Accomplished Bakai the leopard
Arrived before an arrow could.
The Eshteks' champion,
Hero Zhamgyrchy, that warrior,
From atop Telkyzyl
Bakai the leopard
Struck down as he swept past.
As he turned to cut off his head,
To knock it off like an apple,
The reckless man of the Eshteks—
As he bent to behead him,
'Here stands a city with its sovereign;
Sovereign Bakai the leopard;
If you would take my head, here I am!”
Dismounting in distress,
He poured out his words to Bakai,
Pleading at once:
“Here are the honored Kyrgyz people.
Uncle Bakai, if you would shed my blood, here I am.
Do not dig my pit;
Uncle Bakai, do not boil my misery.
Do not destroy this wretched life.
Do not point your sword at us;
Do not end this life by cutting it off.
Uncle Bakai, you are my khan;
You are the one who can find my way out.
Since my hero Manas died,
You are my strength to rely upon,
Renowned Bakai, my lion.
My valiant uncle, hear my words!
I was poor and became rich;
I was alone and grew many.
I trained a harrier into a hunting bird;
I gathered different tribes and made a people.
I trained a little falcon into a hunting bird;
I gathered wanderers and made a people.
If you kill me,
Uncle Bakai, the harrier will return to its place,
My gathered people to their own folk,
To the old condition in which they grew as slaves.
The little falcon will return to its place,
If you kill me,
Just think, leopard warrior:
Those scattered people will return to their former lot.
If you take my head,
My Black Cave will stand empty,
The dark forest will lie deserted.
Of Manas Khan's countless horses,
Among those countless horses,
If none of the countless racers pleases you,
I shall put him beneath you:
Take Telkyzyl, my winged steed!
If your orphan is hungry,
If your widow has no means,
Take my horse, Telkyzyl!
If your provisions prove too little,
Uncle Bakai, fill a leather flask with my blood.
Old man, I am in your hands;
Take your old man, one like Baimyrza;
If you want more, if you need it,
Hero, sacrifice me and take my life.
Take my gold and jewels, weighed by poods;
For the sorrowful ones to ride away,
Take my saddle, offered as a gift.
Prepare dried provisions, take plenty;
So your orphan will not freeze,
Take a coat of the finest quality.
The gold and cloth from far away—
When they were plundering his camp,
I had no strength against those slaves;
I have no coat that stops a bullet;
To rescue your lady and your orphan,
To wrest them free,
Nor have I sixty thousand troops!
Of Manas Khan's countless horses,
I keep seeing the swift racers;
The racers are in those dogs' hands;
That is what grieves me.
The horses in the mountains have mottled heads,
With noble steeds and chargers among them.
The one who would choose and ride the finest racers—
(I have been left desolate here)—
The male child left behind is still young.
When they raided Manas's camp,
Telkyzyl the horse was well fed;
To meet them in a slaughter,
To thrust a spear without holding back,
To take revenge like this upon those dogs,
My ocean, Bakai, was absent.
When my ocean, Manas, lived,
He was a sacred soul.
One year after his death,
To hand it over in trust,
He had given his command.
Fasten it to your saddle straps;
The inheritance left by my khan,
Leopard warrior, Uncle Bakai,
Take it to my orphan.
When leopard Manas died,
When this wretched soul knew grief,
Eleman's hero Toshtuk
Gave it with a prayer.
Its collar is gold, its sleeves are copper,
Double mail with rings like larks' eyes,
A broad jacket shaped like a robe.
With prayers and bitter weeping,
Eleman's Toshtuk
Placed it in my hands,
Dedicating it to my dear Semetey.
Uncle Bakai, old man,
Please heed what I say:
When he reaches twelve years old,
Let them dress Semetey in it.
This coat of fine white cloth,
Please give it to Kanykei.
Uncle Bakai, bringer of good fortune,
Let me set that aside:
The milk the orphan will drink,
The horse the widow will ride:
Let me begin with these.
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan,
The old man, father of his people,
Whose blessing enriched the nation,
A saint resembling a prophet,
Made a pilgrimage to Mecca,
Received a letter from Aikojo,
Received the blessing of Islam,
And was called Old Koshoi.
He saddled his horse like a leopard warrior;
Those who heard of him were frightened.
In Jerusalem, Egypt, and Shaddat
He studied for twelve years.
Receiving a letter from the caliph,
He was called Leopard Koshoi.
There in the broad Talas valley,
Beside tamarisk-covered Ken-Kol,
When Manas died in Ken-Shor,
When this wretched soul knew sorrow,
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan
Was a sacred soul.
Then, mourning for Manas,
He gave a horse in mourning,
Saying, ‘Give it to his orphan,’
And entrusted it to me.
Kept among the horses,
There was the winged steed Taitoru.
Counting his years beyond the first four,
He has reached forty-eight;
This year the mighty charger has filled out.
Restless among the horses,
Taitoru leaps and plunges.
Turning every way,
Neighing loudly and terribly,
He wishes to speak but has no tongue;
Taitoru goes about weeping.
If I tether him, he will not stand;
If I release him, he will not graze.
Were my champion Manas alive,
Girding his waist firmly,
My champion could ride him to Beijing.
Taking his black spear in hand,
He could ride him against the Kalmyks.
Shouting and taking up his sword,
He could ride him to shatter the world.
Tightening his girth securely,
He could ride him to rout the Oirats.
On a journey lasting six months,
His back and head would bear no sores;
His rider would suffer no pain.
Ridden on scouting duty, he would not tire;
Ridden with the rear guard, he would not fall ill.
Taitoru, there among the horses—
Uncle, catch him and lead him along.
Please heed my words:
Taitoru, there among the horses,
Was the horse that Koshoi gave;
Please give him to Kanykei.
Uncle Bakai, my sultan,
Let me set this aside,
And begin with something else.
‘Evil-hearted Karymbai—
I have suffered humiliation from him,
I have suffered his great arrogance.
When I am out among the horses,
He says, “My path runs through the valley”;
What need to tell the rest?
“Manas's Kanykei—
I struck and split her side,” he says,
And swings his spear about.
When bloodthirsty Manas lived,
Akborchuk, whom he would not give when asked.
“I shall cast my cruel catching-pole,” he says.
“The pacer he would not give for begging,
I shall now seize from the open plain,” he says.
“I shall ride it to feasts and memorials,” he says.
“I shall make him step out briskly,” he says.
“I shall take the horse Akborchuk,” he says.
He has driven me to desperation.
Evil-hearted Karymbai—
I knew that swine's stupidity.
I did not yield an inch to his words;
Manas, who has gone to the other world—
I have honored his spirit.
Catch Akborchuk and take him along;
Uncle Bakai, do not delay, leave quickly!
To the orphan Manas left,
Give his coat before he freezes;
To the widow Manas left,
Give food, for she will go hungry!
Uncle Bakai, give your coat to keep out the cold.
Kanykei has been left in mourning;
Semetey has fled.
Give your answer promptly!
The black-bladed battle-axe—
Uncle Bakai, who will haft it straight?
The Kyrgyz people Manas left—
Who will lead them without causing grief?
At the head of Urbuz are three summer pastures;
Uncle Bakai, we have camped along those three pastures.
Parted from Kanykei, who cared for us,
We have earned a bad name now!’
So saying, Zhamgyrchy,
The dear man, sent him on his way.
Without stopping, Bakai, Kojosan,
The leopard warrior, could not stay still.
The mountain herd held noble steeds,
With many winged horses among them.
Bakai galloped on without stopping
And entered the herd.
When Bakai came among them,
Taitoru, there among the horses,
With no load upon his back,
Wishing to speak but having no tongue,
Let out a resounding neigh
And came loudly neighing from the herd.
He turned his neck like a stag.
Taitoru, noblest of animals,
The charger, before Bakai,
Turned and stood there.
Tossing his forelock skyward,
Taitoru leaned toward him;
At Bakai's left knee,
Taitoru stood sniffing.
Then sniffing once at his right side,
Drawing in his belly and snorting,
Flattening his right ear,
The crust of the dark earth—
At the sight of Khan Bakai,
He pawed and scattered it.
‘When his animal behaves like this,
How can my human soul be so strong!’
Then Bakai, the leopard warrior,
Could not hold his fury;
His heart burned with grief,
He grieved within and wept low.
From his dear blackcurrant eyes,
Tears streamed a whip's length.
Though called Hero Bakai,
His ribs bent beneath him;
His dear falcon eyes
Shed tears with every blink:
‘My companion Manas, if only you had not died!
Bakai, if only you had not outlived your companion!
O almighty Creator,
God, would that you had not taken my twin!
Parted from his twin,
Would that suffering Bakai had not remained!
No human is he, O God,
Yet even an animal behaves like this.
This haughty woman, this young child—
Their belongings have been plundered;
God has cursed his own kin!
