Let me tell you what lies beyond:
There is the lake Kara-Dengiz.
If mishap does not trip his feet,
If God does not take the courser,
Let me tell you what it is like:
There is a lake where land disappears.
Drive them all the way there and let them go;
That is the ground for Taitoru.
If you will not heed these words of mine,
To Kara-Dengiz, that great river,
If you will not drive them all the way,
Unable to lower his head to the earth,
Unable to stride like a wild sheep,
If Taitoru falls behind,
Wretched Eshen, if you will not mend your ways,
If you will not follow these words of mine,
If you scorn me because I am a woman,
I shall seize you along the road,
Drag you upon the road,
And tear you apart for the dogs.
I shall gouge out your eyes;
Old Eshen, I shall slaughter you.
The black blood flowing from within you,
I shall scoop up and drink my fill.
Old Eshen, if I do not slaughter you,
If I do not drink my fill of your blood,
My back hair
My very father who begot me,
Temir Khan—may I marry him!
With people calling me a vagrant woman,
How could I remain alive?”
Having said this, Kanykei,
The furious woman, turned and stood.
Hearing this, old Eshen
Was already frightened out of his life;
God had struck Eshen now.
Old Eshen stood thinking;
The haft slipped from his hand;
His heart burst from its casing;
The red blood drained from his face;
Old Eshen’s strength was gone.
His chest began to rattle;
His beard parted strand by strand.
God’s sun in the sky was clouded;
Sixty cares came upon his head at once.
Eshen thought and whimpered;
He pondered, trembling.
His heart was torn with fear;
Kanykei, speaking there,
Seemed like a devouring demon.
He thought and whimpered;
Eshen quivered within.
“If she means to seize us, she will,” he thought.
“This accursed woman,
If Taitoru falls behind,
Leaving not one of us,
Will offer us up as sacrifices,” he thought.
“Leaving none of us at all,
She will wipe us all out,” he thought.
“If she attacks us in the open plain,
From a bitch like this,
Who could beg our lives as a gift?” he thought.
“If her Bay falls behind,
The raging woman will torment us,” he thought.
“If your raging woman attacks,
Who could beg our lives from her?” he thought.
“If she means to destroy us, she will,” he thought.
“This troublemaking bitch
Will raise a commotion,” he thought.
The reason she could do so:
She was a fierce warrior indeed,
Who had thrown the whole world into turmoil;
She was built like a warrior.
When she was the fairest of women,
When this luckless one was a girl,
She grew to maturity like a doe,
Her hair coiled about her head;
She grew to maturity like a deer,
Her hair coiled about her feet;
She stretched gracefully like a tawny doe.
Her speech was sweet, her words were honey;
She became the fairest of women,
A beautiful girl from Bukhara.
Her renown raised dust;
A glowing coal came from the hearth.
Here in this Bukhara,
The fierce woman grew mighty.
Still a playful little child,
She had not yet reached fourteen.
Hero Bakai, the saintly elder,
When the clear-sighted champion saw her,
Disregarding the countless wealth
And livestock he had gathered,
Loaded seventy camels with gold coins
And set out from Kyrgyz Talas.
The beauty among the dark-complexioned,
The radiant one among the Muslims,
He sought then as a bride for Manas.
When the people moved to higher ground,
In the season when horses’ tongues turn dark,
Loading seventy camels with gold coins,
Driving seven thousand barren beasts loose,
When Manas came as the bridegroom
And gave them to her father, Temir Khan,
Her father, Temir Khan,
Gathered treasure and grew rich.
His heart overflowed and was at ease;
Taking the bride-price, Temir Khan
Grew wealthy among the thronging people.
Conceiving a fatal plan,
He gathered the people together.
Out of his joy, Temir Khan
Held eighty feasts by day;
Even the sorrowful rejoiced.
Khan Manas’s ceremonial honors,
In Bukhara with its iron gates,
Were then inscribed upon the door.
Religious masters in motley robes—
When sixty of them were gathered,
Mullahs who read their lessons—
When many had been brought together,
For fierce, bloodthirsty Manas,
When they urgently gathered the people,
A curtain with a silken weft,
When they drew it across this side,
Gold by the pood, bright red gold,
When they took it as the bridal attendants’ fee,
Seventy young wives and ninety girls
Stood as go-betweens.
When they went to put her to bed,
Hanging misfortune about his neck,
As a maiden, with Manas
She refused to lie in his arms.
We saw the trouble that luckless one caused:
Drawing out her dagger,
Before Manas had lain down,
She struck and split his forearm.
“She has grievously humiliated me,” he said.
“Before I even lay down, this girl
Stabbed my forearm open,” he said.
He found a quarrel in the festivities;
Laying Bukhara waste,
Khan Manas raided it six times.
For the people of this Bukhara,
Because of that arrogant, luckless one,
The wide world became narrow.
These people of Bukhara,
For a donkey’s offspring,
For even its foal, then longed in vain.
As a married woman, this fierce one
Upset customs and ruined the people;
When this woman was a girl,
She shook the world and ruined the people.
The wretch who stabbed Manas,
Would she refrain from stabbing us?
That crafty, luckless one who did that to Manas—
Would she have pity on us?
“Let the fierce woman be the hero,” he said.
“If to the river Kara-Dengiz
We do not drive them all the way,
May our seven forefathers be unbelievers,” he said.
“Like a bare horse-tethering rail,
May we pass away with nothing.
All the way to the place you named,
If we do not drive your horse,
If we do not obey your words,
May we die as unbelievers.”
Licking deadly poison,
Swearing an oath on his life,
He chewed white wheat flour;
He swore upon the Holy Quran;
He made his promise firm;
Then he made a pledge that could cost a man his life.
Having made his promise firm,
Old Eshen drove the horses away.
The horses were driven to their place,
And had long since arrived.
Taitoru was entered as an omen
By Kanykei, the prince’s wife.
Let us leave this here;
Ismail, the khan of Bukhara,
Kanykei’s brother of the same nest—
Let us begin our tale with him.
That Ismail of whom we speak,
Of the same nest as Kanykei,
Not merely of one nest, but born in succession,
Children of one father,
Camel calves of one mother.
Ill-fated Ismail,
Staying in Bukhara, heard the news:
“Kanykei has gone astray;
Staying in Bukhara, carried away,
She raced her horse,” he heard.
“A widow, yet she grows so excited—
Would that the earth swallowed her!
‘I shall race my horse,’ she raves,
Taking a shortcut by Ichke-Zhol,
Her one horse of sixty mature years,
The unbeliever leads it, saying, ‘I shall race it.’
God has struck that shameless one!
There she stands, saying, ‘I shall race my horse.’
God has struck that fool!
If she does this, the raging woman will bring no good;
If so, she will not leave us unharmed.
The unbeliever, excited though a widow,
Raving, ‘I shall race my horse’—
Can anyone be so shameless?
Running wild while in mourning,
Can an unbeliever be like this?
Eagerly crying, ‘I shall race my horse,’
Making straight for her wayward path,
This raging woman, though in mourning,
Leads her Taitoru.
Whose bones are these that ache?
Whose honor has been broken?
Whose honor has departed?
Whose honor has returned?
The unbeliever, excited though a widow,
Raving, ‘I shall race my horse,’
Dresses herself in men’s clothes.
Can anyone be such a fool?
Rejoicing that her husband died,
Taking a shortcut along her crooked road,
She has caused grievous trouble,
Raving, ‘I shall race him,’
Leading her Taitoru.
Or has this raging bitch
Sought a husband among eight khans
And failed to find one?
With her heart turning toward a husband,
Has the bitch come to ruin?”
