Then Syrgak grew grim,
And Kokchebich, like a spirit horse,
Stretched out like a roe deer.
Ridden on reconnaissance, he never tired;
Left in the rear camp, he never sickened.
He lowered his head to the ground,
And bolting away with Syrgak,
Gave him much trouble.
His cry was, “Warrior Manas!”
Kogala, being led alongside—
When Syrgak caught sight of him,
Against the Chinese waging war
He plunged in, attacking without pause.
Across the blade-smooth plain
He reached them without delay,
And many governors and swift footmen
He speared down as he passed.
Kokcho’s Kogala
He snatched from the Chinese
And led safely away beside him.
Yielding not to six thousand,
Akbaltay’s Chubak
Was wrestling with the host.
When warrior Chubak looked,
Aydarkhan’s Kokcho
They had put to dreadful torment.
Distraught, finding no way out,
He was being dragged about like a game carcass.
His head was a bucketful of blood,
Not a scrap of his body was whole,
He was covered in dark crimson blood.
Seeing this, warrior Chubak said,
“If Muzburchak and Khan Kokcho die,
If all who came here die,
How shall we return to Talas?
What desire shall we attain?”
Saying this, warrior Chubak
Made Kogala prance,
And heading for warrior Kokcho,
He charged among the Chinese,
Sending those he met crawling.
Just as Chubak reached them,
The one-eyed men fled in turmoil,
Striving to save their lives.
Chubak now raised an uproar.
With life hovering between death and survival,
Kokcho of the Kazakhs
He stooped and lifted up,
And laid across his right knee.
Bearing Kokcho swiftly away,
He freed him from their hands.
In the opening and closing of an eye,
Before a word could be spoken,
The falcon, leopard Syrgak
Charged across to them,
And reached warrior Kokcho.
As soon as he reached Kokcho,
He mounted him on Kogala
And took him safely away.
As they carried Kokcho away,
No breath, no vapor came from his mouth;
Not a tick-sized patch was whole.
From warrior Kokcho’s mouth
Blood came out in clots;
Warrior Kokcho’s strength had gone.
Argynkhan’s son Ajybai,
Most skillful of men in finding remedies,
Had in his keeping,
On the swift camel’s back,
A medicine called Sebep,
A medicine called Medet.
From inside the rawhide bag
He now took out a bowl.
Scooping out the preserved travel food,
Six or seven bowlfuls,
He mixed a compound remedy and made him swallow it.
Khan Kokcho of the Kazakhs
Opened his eyes wide and clear;
His aching wounds grew quiet.
Buudayyk Khan’s Muzburchak—
Telkyzyl’s strength was failing,
And from Burchak’s body
Dark crimson blood was flowing,
Seventy spears having struck him at once.
Seven thousand governors swarmed upon him,
Ay Jangjung gave a shout,
Ezkara upon his great bay,
And all the mighty champions
Had taken Buudayyk Khan’s Burchak
Into their midst.
His two eyes darting wildly,
His two feet dangling,
Buudayyk Khan’s Muzburchak,
When the Chinese approached,
With sword and battle-axe,
When they swung their painted spears,
Not knowing what to do,
Faint and bewildered,
Spoke his thoughts in a daze:
“Now I am going to die!”
With no strength to utter a sound,
Bracing himself upon his horse,
Despairing of his entrusted life,
He stood looking on and muttering.
Not revealing his plight to the Chinese,
Not letting them know his anguish,
Then, golden-crested, with a belt like embers,
Azizkhan’s Almambet,
Fearless, born a lion,
Saw Burchak from afar,
And against the Chinese surrounding him
He charged in alone.
“Let me rekindle his extinguished fire,
Bring his dead soul back to life,
And rescue him!”
Saying this, your valiant champion
Came speeding like an arrow.
Racing along on Sarala,
The Manchus who swarmed upon him
He flung aside like fur caps.
He plunged through, breaking their ranks;
The Chinese he met fell in heaps.
The Chinese surrounding him
Almambet drove away;
The Chinese who had encircled him
Almambet drove out.
