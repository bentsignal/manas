The Argyn are left weeping.
Powerless against Kobosh,
Many more go about loathing him.
‘Alas, Manas, do not die!’
Many more keep yearning:
‘While that Manas was alive,
When bored, we would shoot roe deer;
When not bored, we would lie at ease.
Riding horses in their third year, flying hawks,
We played in wild delight.
How could we have known such hell?
What could dear Kobosh do?
He made calves carry loads,
Made us endure such hell,
Had the butcher slaughter goats;
He meant to make us suffer.’
From time to time, growing violent,
A khan like Kobosh arises;
His voice drives the soul out.
Immersed in many pleasures,
As Akylai and that Kobosh
Lay entwined together,
Manas's son, the orphan slave,
Was sorely troubled in his thoughts.
The orphan who had wandered here
Shed tears from his eyes.
As though his father had died today,
His mind was sorely troubled.
At his pompous uncle Kobosh,
He seemed to grow angry:
‘Who pledged allegiance to twelve spiritual guides—
May my holy father never die!
May I never be left fatherless!
Such humiliation as this—
May no mortal be made to suffer it!’
Steeling himself, burning within,
The orphan could not stand watching.
The warrior born of Manas—
Here was where he would spread havoc.
The orphan now made a stir;
Of Manas, gone to the world to come,
He stood demanding the possessions:
‘Uncle Abyke, hear my words.
My father has died.
The countless riches left by my father—
When you plundered and divided them,
When you contrived matters thus,
My poor widowed mother—
When you struck and split her breast,
To Temir Khan's city,
When my mother went wandering,
My dear mother Kanykei,
When she fled toward Karakan,
All the great wealth,
When you ruined and scattered it,
In those days, when you plundered the valuables,
When the boar stirred up trouble,
Ten days after my father died,
Toward my unfortunate mother,
When you became a predator,
Every treasure, wealth and possession,
When you took it as booty,
While I was still in the cradle,
My mother Chiyirdi,
When she pulled me out and fled straight ahead,
My cradle standing empty,
My brave mother Kanykei,
The slave woman's child,
Taking him from his swaddling clothes,
When she bound him in the cradle,
My mother nursing him,
When she put her breast in his mouth,
Your shouting split stones.
Upon my wretched mother,
When you burst in raging,
You found just the right way!
Striking my mother with a battle-axe—
You struck her down, screaming.
The treasury left by Manas,
You plundered it utterly.
Thus you did just as you pleased.
The wealth left by my father,
You took as booty and rested content.
Until I reached twelve years of age,
Because of you, my elder kinsmen,
Because of your foolish deeds,
Into Temir Khan's city
I wandered and made a living;
Into Kara Khan's city
I fled and made a living.
When we wandered to Temir Khan,
As for me, still in swaddling clothes,
“Upon my child while he is young,
Someone will bring suffering,” she thought.
My mother Kanykei
She cut through my twig,
Made me step over a dog's bowl,
Made me step across a sword's edge.
Thus she suffered humiliation;
She gave me to her own brother Ysmayyl.
When holy sages spread their hands,
I knew nothing of blessings;
Of Manas, gone to the other world,
I knew nothing of his being my father.
A wretched soul born to such a fate,
I grew up wandering.
Until I reached twelve,
Temir Khan's little camel,
Ysmayyl's son—
That was what I was, poor soul.
When I reached twelve years of age,
Finding no mountain to hurl myself against,
Finding no foe to fight,
Finding no head to wrench off,
Finding no stone to bite,
I locked up the wandering dervishes,
I beat the mendicants.
If I saw a khoja, I kicked him
And locked him in his mosque.
When they saw my wild behavior,
All the people of Bukhara grew weary.
Temir Khan took fright;
His mind alarmed, he pondered,
And gathered the people of eight khans.
Then the widow Kanykei,
In his sixtieth adult year,
Entered Taitoru in the race.
Kanykei's Taitoru
To the great city of Bukhara
Came far out in front.
The many prizes in Bukhara,
I gathered them all.
Not taking half a coin's worth of goods,
Not taking half a penny's worth of hair,
To all the wrestlers
I gave them as booty.
Those watching crowded round;
The people looked on in wonder.
When the crowd had gathered,
Offering a thousand dark camels,
Offering a thousand black mares with white blazes,
Temir Khan proclaimed:
“Let the wrestlers wrestle on foot;
Let the loser go empty-handed!”
Making it known to the people,
He made his proclamation
And gave his stern command.
From Kokand came Too-Balban;
From Bukhara came Ak-Balban.
The champions wrestled on foot,
Clashing like gray bulls.
Biting like stallions,
Gnashing like hungry male camels,
Gripping one another's trouser legs,
Tripping each other without sparing their lives.
That Ak-Balban of Bukhara
Grew weak;
Too-Balban of Kokand
Lifted him and dashed him down.
When all the many prizes of Bukhara
He drove away,
Wrestling with Too-Balban,
Semetey put him to torment.
A champion such as Too-Balban
He lifted and dashed once to the ground;
When he tore his arm out,
Through this, Too-Balban
Met his death.
“He will surpass Manas,”
All the people began to whisper.
Not content to stop at that,
The mount beneath me
Was Temir Khan's Aktulpar;
What I held on my hand
Was Ysmayyl's falcon.
I went out flying the falcon
And heard the bald charcoal burner's words.
“Your great people are the Kyrgyz,” I heard.
“Your horse has a white blaze,
Your father is someone else.”
Sarytaz said a word;
He put a troubling word into my thoughts.
I heard the words of that damned bald man:
“My people are the Kyrgyz,” I heard.
“Manas is your father,
Talas is your land,
Kanykei is your mother,
Ysmayyl is your mother's brother;
Abyke and Kobosh in Talas are your elder kinsmen;
Akun Khan is your father-in-law,
Aichurok is your wife.”
Sarytaz said a word;
He put a troubling word into my thoughts.
I chose to mount Aktulpar;
From far away I came of my own will,
Saying, “Abyke and Kobosh are my kin.”
You whom I regard in my father's place,
Abyke, on whom I lean,
Hear the words I mean to say.
Kumaiyk, left by my father Manas,
That keen hunter known to the people,
The inheritance left by my father Manas,
From the banks of great Talas
Has strayed into your keeping.
Where is that treasure, the white sighthound?
Tell me what has become of him.
Long ago, the bird a kite hatched and raised,
God Almighty, to my khan father,
Brought it to Khan Manas—
His talons iron, his jess fastener copper,
Two cubits in girth,
Whose striking talons never missed,
Whom no keen hunting bird could match,
Black-eyed, with speckled legs,
Never shrinking from what he saw,
He would not fold his wings and swoop;
He would not let a goose fly from the lake.
He would not gather his tail and swoop;
He would not let a swan fly from the lake.
He was a bird to take hunting;
He was a possession left by my father.
Where is the white falcon, uncle?!
Long ago, into looming Great Beijing,
When he went straight in and thrust his spear,
From a hell of grievous suffering,
When he freed lives.
