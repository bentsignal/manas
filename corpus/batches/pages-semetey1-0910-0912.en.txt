Dozing over their little camp kettles.
They called battle to mind;
The forty companions hurried in alarm
Each to his own horse,
Made ready their weapons of war,
As though the Chinese hook-nosed Konur
Had come upon them shouting.
They took their steel guns in hand,
Against the orphan son of Manas
They took their stand;
The forty wolves made ready.
Even if they cut one another down,
The forty companions were his close kin.
Bending his knees to the ground,
When he saw the forty companions,
The young lord Semetey,
From his camel-like Buurul,
Swiftly dismounted.
The white lead rope of Buurul
Had been braided in the okoro manner
By your formidable mother Kanykei—
A lead rope fitted to Semetey.
He wound it around his neck,
Saying, “My father's legacy,”
Folded his hands against his breast,
And pleaded with the forty companions:
“My dear forty uncles,
Do not dig a pit for me;
Do not shame me by fleeing,
Do not bring my misery to the boil!
You legacies left by my father,
Listen closely to what I say;
Do not break my back,
Do not leave my body cold.
Listen well to my words;
Forty uncles, do not gouge out my eyes.
My Buurul is my gift, my foremost gift;
Weigh my words as you listen,
Forty uncles, do not torment me!
If I have acted childishly,
Gouge out my eyes here on this spot!
The Buurul I have dedicated to you—
Do not let them say I left it starving;
The legacy my father left—
Do not let them call Semetey a fool
Who let it flee away!
My dear forty uncles!
If I have done wrong, forty uncles,
Slaughter me as you go your way;
Say, ‘The son of Manas has died,’
And bury me with your own hands!
I shall make a gift from my horses;
Take my beast, such as Taibuurul.
If that gift is too little,
Take my blood, poured into a leather flask!
When I moved from Bukhara,
I came eating red raisins;
If my forty uncles would support me,
I came intending to overthrow the Chinese!”
As the boy stood speaking thus,
All forty companions—
Was he not your Khizr-blessed lord?—
As though they had seen Manas himself,
Began with gentle words
And laid aside their gear.
Among the forty companions, the capable one,
Their stalwart Baichoro was there.
He strode up with a booming voice;
The warrior Baichoro, beaming,
Spoke these words to the boy:
“Ah, my hoof left by the winged steed,
My orphan left by the white goshawk!
In the days when goshawk Manas lived,
The black steel Zulpukor
Was wielded by the forty companions;
Crushing those who opposed us,
We forty companions bore them down;
The eighteen thousand worlds
Were ruled by us forty companions.
Saying, ‘The orphan who brings us joy has come;
He has taken Manas's place;
God has granted our wish,’
Finding no mountain to hurl ourselves against,
Finding no enemy to fight,
We forty companions had grown restless.
To scout against the emperor,
About the warrior Khan Konurbai
To gather certain news,
We forty companions, grown restless,
To set the world astir,
With the renowned land of Beijing
To come face to face,
The warrior's orphan son
To make our steadfast support,
All forty companions
Came hurrying on a scouting raid,
Setting out early.
The forty companions, forty in number,
When Khizr-blessed Manas was alive,
Without consulting, without discussing,
Were accustomed to go away.
Without telling Khan Manas,
We would scout in this fashion,
Then charge in fighting
When my precious Manas died,
No one heard of the forty companions anymore.
Lying idle for twelve years,
We had grown wild with restlessness.
When the formidable orphan arrived,
Damn it, would the forty companions
Just look on without excitement?”
When Baichoro said this,
The orphan answered:
“Resourceful men who find wise counsel,
My dear forty uncles!
Let us consult Bakai,
Fatten our horses till they are full of strength,
Till every warrior chafes with pent-up strength,
Gather and spread out our host,
The great banner of Khan Manas
Place in Sary Khan's hands,
And then raid Beijing.
For now, forty uncles,
Let us grow stout and recover our vigor!”
The forty companions obeyed him;
Warriors of the finest kind,
The warriors turned back
And set off for Talas.
They traveled across the steppe,
Traveled a long way, no little distance,
And returned to Talas.
He settled the forty companions there:
Kumys by day, girls by night,
He had the autumn festivities held.
Tossing their marten-fur caps,
As though a wolf-maned man like Khan Manas
Were still there, he let them rejoice.
Sixty thousand horses in the hills,
Thoroughbreds and swift steeds together;
He mounted them on horses no missile could catch,
Clothed them in coats no missile could pierce,
Let them crunch sugar like snow,
Let them drink spirits like water.
He spread his wealth before them;
Fattened lambs, tender heads,
He had boiled three or four times a day.
When bored, he sent them to shoot roe deer;
When not bored, in Talas
He let them freely sport and rest.
Making their yurt coverings greasy,
Whenever he met a companion,
He made him a khan in one place or another.
Making their yurt lattices greasy,
Whenever he encountered a companion,
He made him an imposing khan.
From the stream that flowed in bends,
He turned his winged steeds to drink;
A thousand times more than before,
He spread the fame of the forty companions,
He treated the companions like Manas himself.
Then your dear mother Kanykei,
Leopard Bakai, Sary Khan,
Warrior Jamgyrchy, Bolokbai—
All the warriors were there.
Your dear mother Kanykei
Began to speak to this orphan:
“The forty companions your father left
Draw their bows in showers of rain,
Break away and loose their missiles.
Tort-Kul will not become level ground;
The forty companions your father left,
My orphan, will not be your loyal people.
They turn the place of battle into a hollow;
To flee away to the Chinese,
My foal, they will abandon hope in you.
The breeze that rises from the valley
Will not become a face-freezing wind;
The forty ill-intentioned dogs,
My warrior, will not be your loyal people.
Silk with both ends intact—
If I draw it out, it will not part;
The forty companions your father left
Seem unlikely to live without uproar.
The forty companions your father left
Will not settle here in Talas.
My orphan, my comfort, listen:
These dogs will never be your kin!
Bright red crimson cloth—
If I pull its edge, it will not part;
The forty ill-behaved swine,
Not one fool seems likely to look back.
Green-mottled silk—
If I tear it at the side, it will not part;
They seem unlikely to draw near.
Your kumys is in the little leather flask;
If these dogs draw near,
They will do harm in secret!
And now, my child, listen!
Your kumys poured into the wooden tub—
If they can, the forty companions
If you go raiding, the forty companions
Will blow upon the meadowsweet embers.
If you make companions of the dogs,
They will talk when you are hard pressed.
If you plead, they will not heed your words;
They will break your spirit.
If these dogs set their minds to it,
They will take your breath away!”
Kanykei spoke these words:
“The forty companions your father left—
Divide them all into separate groups
And send them down into the depths.
Send one to the emperor,
One to Altai,
One to Khangai,
One to the Irtysh.
If you do not take measures early,
They are no servants who will remain your people;
Harboring wickedness, devising schemes,
The scoundrels will not be your loyal people.
Mice the size of knucklebones—
I gathered ninety; they do not make a coat.
The forty companions, like hell itself,
Will not stay and be your loyal people.”
Beyond his wolf-maned father Manas,
Semetey had been born greater.
However much the elders spoke,
He stayed angry and unmoved;
Those words of the elders,
He counted as worth nothing,
And lay there unconcerned.
