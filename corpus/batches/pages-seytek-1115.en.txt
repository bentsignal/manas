A hero named Kenen, lion-like,
Will indeed be born.
No enemy can breach his steel gate;
To all the peoples of the world, brave Kenen—
You shall indeed bear such a son.
Your camp shall settle upon the terrace;
My dear child Akbermet,
You shall crave the flesh of a lion.
Fierce and raging like a lion,
Breaking mountains to make roads,
Subduing the whole wide world—
Such a son shall indeed be yours.
At his feast we shall slaughter ninety head;
My child, we shall name him Kenen.
This is God’s gift,
The advent of true good fortune.
If he guards Ala-Too as his land,
If he makes his home by the White River,
Then Ala-Too, his homeland,
Shall indeed become his refuge.
If he takes the moon in his right hand,
And, when aroused, takes the sun,
Putting the Sun in the Moon’s place
And the Moon in the Sun’s place,
Then against the infidel rabble
We shall indeed wage battle.
Among all mortal sons,
Brave Seytek shall be a champion,
And we shall indeed join combat.
We shall take fully half
Of the Chinese lands.
For our forefathers who have passed,
My dear child Akbermet,
We shall indeed exact vengeance.
Stirring strife in Chin-Machin,
We shall ride out alongside him.
If all our enemy armies come,
If our resentful challengers come,
We shall extinguish the fires they kindled
And drive every one of them away;
The ranks arrayed against us—
When they clash, mortals will weary—
Of the immeasurable enemy
We shall indeed break the frontier.
My child, those foes who come from afar—
We shall indeed confound their boasts.
This is how I interpret your dream;
May the dream not come true, but good fortune come instead.
My dear child Akbermet,
But after that, our dream
Foretells a grievous trial.
If it settles in the east,
If, leaving not a blade among the gray grass,
It grazes across the face of the earth—
What is here today is gone tomorrow;
This deceitful world is filth.
Your dream is a grave portent:
If two swords are girded,
If they shatter stone at its solid heart,
And do not glance aside from the stone,
If a ravening dragon comes
And, when the dragon is struck,
The sword breaks in two,
Then across the face of the earth
The dragon’s wrath will pour down,
A calamitous affliction will arise
And leave us wretched.
That accursed creature, fit to be struck down,
Will grieve us with broken wings
And indeed bring us ruin!”
So saying, Akjoltoy
Reviled the ending of the dream
And fell silent as though he had drunk poison.
As Joltoy stood there,
His messenger Kadike,
A champion among the herdsmen,
Came rushing in with an uproar,
Bringing news:
