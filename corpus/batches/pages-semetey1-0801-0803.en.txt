Dedicating it to her dear Semetey.
Unable to keep still in her haste,
Terribly afraid of the boy,
Without taking its strap from her neck,
The telescope of unmeasured range
She drew out from her bosom.
Turning its ring,
Bringing it right to her eye,
She looked through the telescope.
It seemed that Allah Almighty, the one God,
Had granted her wish;
Her only son, who had gone to his people,
Seemed to have returned!
When she looked in front of him,
Never flinching from a tiger,
Never turning back from a bear,
Making the one who loosed him laugh,
Destroying whatever he bit,
Tended when he was little,
Kept when he was a puppy,
With a collar put around his neck,
Fed at the treasury's expense,
“Would that he were my dog!”
So all who saw him wished.
That Kumaiyk who had strayed away—
“He was like my firstborn son;
I was like a mother to him.
Allah Almighty, the All-Powerful,
Seems to have granted him to me.
Before Semetey,
Drawing within an arrow's flight,
He seems to have returned!
My reed-like plant growing in the valley,
My only child, won at death's door,
Kumaiyk, left in trust,
He has brought back to me
And shown before my eyes.”
Her poor heart was stirred;
The one running below
Was truly Kumaiyk, it seemed.
The one coming behind him—
“My plump little mottled gray hawk,
My gray goshawk, my falcon,
My spring flowing through the valley,
Comes riding Taibuurul,
My lamp, won at death's door!”
Seeing him, her poor heart was stirred:
“Oh, mother, good tidings!
My orphan seems to be coming!
He has granted what I wished;
My orphan, my support, has come!
God has granted my desire.
Come, mother, rise up;
The orphan we watched for has come!
Your brow will now brighten;
Your sorrow will now ease.
Seeing him, the heart is stirred;
In build and bearing
He looks like Manas.
God has granted my desire;
My orphan left by Manas
Has taken what he sought.
Why speak of the rest?
Left by his father,
The white falcon he has brought.
That dear bird called the wild duck
Flies without turning back, in search of food.
How much I have suffered, poor soul,
For you, who came from my womb!
That wretched bird called the kite
Swoops in flight in search of food.
Your two mothers have suffered so much
For you, who came from the womb!”
Both his mothers wept;
Poor Chiyirdi wept especially hard.
The boy entered Bukhara,
All his gear in readiness.
The trust left by Manas,
All of it was provided.
Long ago, when the widow fled there,
Her father had set it apart for her:
Kanykei's white palace,
Its doors of iron and solid tin.
Though that unfortunate woman was a widow,
Her dignity was very great.
The poor orphan, grown imposing,
Stood grandly like his father Manas.
Seeing him, the heart was stirred;
Your orphan, the wolf-maned warrior's son,
Came fully into view.
A black-spotted leopard,
Springing at his side,
Secretly lent him protection;
Round about the boy
It circled.
Kanykei and Chiyirdi,
Seeing the boy's form,
Offered their very lives
As a sacrifice for him.
When they saw their precious one,
His two mothers rejoiced.
When they saw Kumaiyk,
Kanykei and Chiyirdi
Both grieved together.
He had no tongue to speak with,
Kumaiyk, wiser than other beasts.
The people were there, their khan was there;
As Kumaiyk stood thus,
All the multitude was there.
When he saw the two ladies,
Crouching like a quail,
Kumaiyk came crouching low.
Tears welled in his eyes;
Kumaiyk came shedding them.
When he saw the two ladies,
Kumaiyk stood distraught;
Sobbing more than a human,
Kumaiyk stood howling.
Then Kumaiyk,
When he saw Kanykei,
Laid his head upon her breast;
Like a person weeping,
He lay down and wailed.
As Kumaiyk did this,
Your poor mother wept with him.
Her own father Temir Khan,
Her younger brother Ysmayyl—
The khan of Bukhara wept with them.
When the khan wept, the people wept;
All the people wept together.
Chiyirdi, grown old,
The old woman in her bed, wept.
Ah, that creature Kumaiyk
Had taken my mother
As a mother in his own mother's place;
He laid his head upon her breast.
He sniffed at her right breast,
Then sniffed at her left.
Kanykei wept bitterly;
Kumaiyk growled.
“Kumaiyk, whom we had asked after,
Has come in the flesh,” she said.
“The inheritance I longed for
Has come back to me,” she said.
Like rain from the sky,
They caressed one another.
For twelve years they had not seen each other;
Now they were reunited.
Their lord, Manas the wolf-maned warrior,
Had indeed been their lord.
Long ago, Manas the leopard,
At the Pass of Belendi,
Where he performed his ablutions,
Had found Kumaiyk,
Before his eyes had opened.
When he was near to death,
When he found him and brought him back,
When he gave him to Kanykei,
The steel collar upon his neck
Was fastened by Kanykei.
Becoming his mother then,
Kanykei had raised him.
Before his eyes had opened,
When he was no bigger than a hand,
“If I tend the puppy badly,
Death will take him needlessly,
And my hopes will perish with him,” she said.
She fed him milk from her mouth;
She gave him meat with her hand.
Taking no rest by day,
Getting no sleep by night,
Thus she raised the sighthound.
Never once lying idle,
The sighthound you have seen
She kept as a mother would.
In raising Kumaiyk,
She thought and pondered,
Taking a broad view of the world.
“If this Kumaiyk I am raising
Should happen to die,
Should happen to be maimed,
This whole Bukhara here—
The leopard, in his wrath,
Will lay it all waste,
And thus destroy us utterly,” she said.
She tended and trained him well;
That poor Kumaiyk
She kept as her own child.
Reunited with Kanykei,
When Kumaiyk stood weeping,
The birch wept, the willow wept;
Every soul watching wept.
The white-bearded old man wept;
The people wept, the multitude wept.
All populous Bukhara wept;
Those who held themselves great wept;
Her father Temir Khan wept.
Ysmayyl of Bukhara,
Temir Khan and Koloi Khan,
With Shaatemir, those four,
Had six matching gray mares
Slaughtered in sacrifice,
Quickly pledging an offering to Bahauddin.
All populous Bukhara sobbed,
Crying out, “My orphan!”
Had he not raised him?
Ysmayyl wept on one side.
“My one and only morning star—
Who knows his worth?
If the boy goes about like this,
Someone's evil eye will strike him,” he said.
Raised at the treasury's expense,
Nine black bulls
He had driven to him;
As a votive offering, all of them
He had beheaded.
The khoja in a motley robe,
The mullah in a white turban,
Those nine black bulls
He had them cut apart.
Performing a rite for the boy,
I will scatter him his share.
He had them slaughtered as an offering.
Leaving no rite unperformed,
She consecrated with a rite
Unwilling to accept their rites,
Semetey, whom you have seen,
Would not turn his heart toward Bukhara.
Truly created by God,
Did you see that he was His servant?
Poor Manas the warrior—
Did you see that he was his son?
