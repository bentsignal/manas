As twilight fell and the sun went down,
And the first sleepers had all lain down,
Without letting a single person know,
Without being seen by anyone,
To his people in the broad Altai
He rode and arrived.
The child hidden against his own flesh,
He brought him safely there.
His chief wife, Markatay,
Was a skilled craftswoman among women.
If you ask about her father,
She was the daughter of Khan Kayyp.
The deftest of those with brass thimbles,
The finest among women,
The eldest of sixty wives,
The loveliest of the long-haired women,
Excelling among the brass-thimble wearers,
To wolf-maned Baltay the warrior
She came when she was fourteen.
They had joined when tiny as lice,
Wed when small as thumbs,
Joined when tiny as lice.
To his companion in lawful marriage:
“I have come before you,” he said.
“On reaching forty—
Such is this wondrous world—
We have escaped childlessness.
This is your God's gift!
Put an amulet around his neck;
Cherish him and raise him well!”
So Akbaltay spoke,
Then said, “My chief wife has borne a son!”
Oh, he made meat into a hill,
And broth into a reservoir-like lake.
He had ninety slaughtered for the feast;
With their fangs jutting out,
Their front lips hanging down,
A thousand camels he slaughtered.
He held horse races and a feast,
Gathering the peoples of the world,
Making a splendid, resounding celebration,
Showing the people his distinction.
Your Baltay, now with a child,
Had settled into contentment.
All his countless livestock
He had slaughtered.
Great entertainment and rejoicing
He had provided.
The people gathered and feasted;
He devised a plan none could have thought of.
To those who said, “May your cradle straps hold firm!”
Five sets of nine, each headed by a mare,
He gave as gifts of honor.
Four sets of nine, each headed by a camel,
He gave as rewards for joyful news.
All the people ate their fill,
And great herds were slaughtered.
Calling out to the people,
Summoning the great throng,
Akbaltay in his old age,
When he had reached eighty-four,
Had turned his face toward God.
“My chief wife has borne a son!”
He proclaimed aloud.
“A spring that rises in a valley,
A little one granted at death's door!
My beloved people,
Having been childless before,
I have been granted a child
At exactly eighty-four years of age.
For this child I have seen at death's door,
My beloved people,
Choose a name.
Sixty gifts, headed by a horse—
Whoever names him, take them,
And live in wealth!”
When he spread the news, excitement rose;
All the people
Had gathered there.
White-bearded elders,
Those of many years,
Black beards and broad mustaches—
Every one of them assembled.
Unable to name the child,
The great assembled crowd
Could find no one to name him.
Their white beards gleaming,
The elders looked at the black-bearded men.
All the black-bearded men
Looked at the youths.
Those youths then
Scattered and fled together.
With no one coming forward to name him,
While they stood at a loss,
On this side of the people,
At the northern edge,
Barefoot and bareheaded,
Ninety years of age,
With a holy staff in his hand,
His backside washed for prayer,
And faith upon his face,
A great old man arrived.
He was Khizir himself;
To give the child a name
He had arrived.
His attention was strangely drawn;
To Akbaltay's eyes,
The old man who would name the child
Seemed something extraordinary!
Facing the qibla,
From Markatay's hands
He lifted the child.
The child spread his limbs wide,
As though about to apportion an inheritance.
The holy wanderer named Khizir
Took that child,
Weighing him in both hands:
“Oh, Akbaltay, look!
Your intentions are pure.
As I weigh him and look,
This is your child granted at death's door.
Let us dispel all sorrow;
Let us name the child.
Akbaltay's lion—
Let his name be Chubak the warrior;
May those he spears number ninety thousand,
