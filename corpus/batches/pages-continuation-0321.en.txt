Scattered without counting them worth a hair.
There were great herds of all four kinds,
And strong double-humped camels;
When Ebegey and Kögöy arrived,
Every manner of delight was there.
In the hollow of Jylgynduu,
Along the storied Talas River,
Now behold a wondrous sight:
The feast for Ebegey and Kögöy.
The horses’ tails were knotted for competition,
And the splendid games and festivities
Were proclaimed to all the people.
The heroes competed for the goat,
In games that proved a man,
Their splendor surpassing all the people.
Their weight and worth were known among thousands,
Their fierce heroic nature was revealed.
They maintained a royal court and became great lords;
Ebegey and Kögöy, the two lions,
Became khans seated upon golden thrones.
The matchless hero Kenen,
And Akjoltoy, husbandman of wisdom,
Became men of sacred grace.
His support reaching multitudes of people,
They called Kenen the lion of lions,
Saying his valor, fortune, and prosperity
Showed him to be the true lion’s offspring,
A hero who had indeed been found.
The valiant hero Kenen arose,
His fortunes full and equal,
With an iron core and copper arms,
A steadfast dark-maned lion,
A hero and protector of the people.
Ebegey, Kögöy, and Akjoltoy
Acted as those who came between,
And they settled there in peace.
They had stirred the stars in the heavens:
My forefather, the lion Manas,
And Kökbörü of Urum Khan
Had sworn friendship three times in one day.
As a pledge to the hero,
They hunted and ate a wild ass;
“If any offspring remain after me,
Then, in time, the great-hearted lion’s descendant
Shall take Aykanysh,
My daughter,” he had said.
He had made it known to Uskaluu,
Declared it to the caravaner,
And sent a letter by a traveler.
Such was the heroes’ covenant;
If they failed to honor their promise,
They knew it would be shameful folly.
Akjoltoy, husbandman of wisdom,
With Ebegey and Kögöy—the three of them—
Wore out horses and surveyed the land,
Patrolling with spears brandished,
Wearing their horses’ flanks raw,
And ranging across the whole world.
The valiant men of Uskaluu stirred
And came upon the people of Urum.
She was the tempered edge of blue steel,
The cub of Kökbörü,
A scion of the broad valley,
The offspring of the departed hero;
Aykanysh, fiercer than any maiden,
Had bound up her hair like meadow grass
And donned the garb of a saint.
She slept not by day,
And found no rest when night fell.
She now shook out and donned
The twelve arrow-proof layers from the palace;
Each day she tethered and rode
Telkyzyl, whom no arrow could catch.
She made all her weapons ready,
And once more fastened at her waist
The black steel that spilled blood.
She took up the life-taking iron-headed mace
And prepared every piece of equipment.
She gathered many wolf-like champions,
And noble heroes worthy of her,
Appointing Bolot of Urum as commander,
A thickset man of short stature,
Whose whole body was like black stone.
Such was the hero Bolot of Urum;
Aykanysh was a devastating maiden.
Orphaned of her father,
She watched over the land and governed the people,
And was called the accomplished unfortunate one,
Receiving blessings wherever she went.
At Kara-Kyr of Er-Baspas,
Here lay open grazing land,
Where feather grass twisted above the ridge
And wormwood swayed upon the hills,
Where foxtail and white sedge grew—
These were the names of its grasses.
From the warm slopes curled
Black meadow grass and brocade-like flowers,
And grasses of every kind swayed.
