The lion-prince then
Had come face-to-face with him.
It was time for him to grow wrathful
And stand firm amid upheaval.
It was time for your bloodthirsty hero
And Khan Kojo to fight.
It was time to encounter
The decree laid down by God.
It was time for Kojo and Semetey
To fight to the death.
It was time to tear apart
The folds of one another's ribs,
And spill the dark blood
Within their chests.
The kasaba banner floated in his hand;
The inlaid spear leveled at the enemy
Gleamed in his right hand.
Arbuurul flew like an arrow,
Rearing and bounding in frenzy,
Striding and straining forward.
Tossing his forelock to the sky,
Driving his hooves into the earth,
He moved in eager agitation,
Showing the form of a roe deer.
Semetey settled into his wrath;
Khan Manas's Zulfiqar
Hung fitted at his side.
Coming out over a saddle-backed pass,
Semetey stood before Kojo
And said this:
"Should the prince himself
Go about as a fugitive?
When your khan has fled,
Should he abandon his people?
Should the nobleman himself,
Parted from his renowned people,
Go wandering in flight?
O Chynkojo, khan-uncle,
Do not turn your intentions elsewhere;
Receive your Argyn people in Talas.
You have courage, braggart—
Will you not win a name among the people?
You commoner estranged from your folk.
Can one who wanders apart from his people
Ever come to good?
Is it proper conduct
For your khan to wander?
Can one call a wanderer like you
A good man?
Let me offer you livestock;
If your grievance departs, Chynkojo,
Take my fourteen swift horses,
Take my sword-beaked elephant,
My precious Kojo, heed my words!"
Before he could turn Buurul's mouth,
While he was still saying this,
God now struck Kojo;
His wrathful fury arose.
Calling him "Manas's orphan son,"
Chynkojo, son of Shygai,
Raged like a blue tiger.
As though calamity had struck his wife,
As though the pursuer were close behind,
As though he were close upon his quarry,
Kojo went mad then,
As though his wife had borne a son.
Wrapping himself in his bulletproof coat,
Ruinous Kojo prepared
As though to overturn him.
He bit crosswise through the saddle;
Utterly without public spirit,
Chynkojo, son of Shygai,
Was seized by his bloodthirsty nature:
When roused, his venom was strong;
This Kojo, son of Shygai,
Laid flat whoever challenged him.
He settled a cast-iron club, house-sized,
Across his back,
