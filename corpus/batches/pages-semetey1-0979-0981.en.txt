From godfather Chubak
Highborn Kanchoro was born.
Abyke's Aktelki
Was the mount of Kulchoro;
The mount of Kanchoro
Was Khan Kobosh's Koktelki.
The two raced in earnest,
Riding out in wrath,
From Semetey's own mouth
Having won a solemn sworn promise,
Taking the letter their elder had written.
As the sun wavered toward setting,
Before the people went to bed,
Below Tort-Kul,
To Lord Manas's orchard,
To the dark eye of the spring,
To the very plane tree,
To Kanykei's palace,
To Chachykei's walled enclosure with its gate,
The young men now came in.
The two companions stood abreast;
Unable to find a way, those lads
Whispered together,
Laughed with ringing voices,
Their sister-in-law's temper
Having known for a long time.
They made a solemn promise,
Yet stood there unable to venture in.
The pampered wife Chachykei—
Her pampered ways were something else;
Her scarf was a golden amulet,
A renowned troublemaker.
No one dared approach her;
Those who saw her pampering marveled.
No one without ritual ablutions entered her presence;
Apart from Semetey,
No soul ventured to keep her company.
Her brow was high, her wrath was fierce;
Do not ask about your sister-in-law!
She was one of life's dark misfortunes;
On anyone who truly angered her,
This slave-woman would unleash her wrath.
No one went to visit her;
No mortal came to ask after her;
She was one of life's dark misfortunes.
Beside Chachykei
Were thirty young wives and thirty girls;
Her wealth was no small thing,
Her attendants played and danced.
Hanging at their throats
Were golden earrings and little amulets;
All were shapely, beautiful minxes.
Semetey's tower—
From atop that tower,
Your dear sister-in-law Chachykei
Had been keeping watch.
That Semetey was absent
She learned, then shouted loudly;
God had struck that woman.
Chachykei's forty young wives,
All skilled women,
Stood around her.
Now God struck that woman;
The pampered wife Chachykei
Spoke to the young wives:
“The end of the world has come,” she said.
“The king's falcon
Has surely been carried off
By that husband-seeking infidel!”
Distressed at seeing the young men,
The pampered wife Chachykei
From the top of the tower
Came rushing down.
“That infidel has led him astray,” she said;
“She has taken him at last,” she said.
From her dear quail-like eyes
Tears fell one after another;
Distraught, she wept and sobbed,
Shaking with endless sobs.
The attendants beside her,
Chachykei's forty young wives,
Girls and wives together—
Among those young wives,
What was there, and what was not!
With waists a handspan wide and moonlike faces,
There were serene beauties;
There were those whose speech was sweet;
There were stately ones with young camels' eyes;
There were those with thick, long black braids,
Those with finely drawn black brows.
Your arrogant sister-in-law Chachykei,
How haughtily she carried herself!
The attendants beside her
Surrounded her, keeping her from walking;
Semetey's great carpet
They had spread upon the bare earth,
Keeping her feet from God's own ground.
The arrogant wife Chachykei
Into the gated enclosure
Went with her train;
The young men from the plain could not catch up.
Kulchoro and Kanchoro,
To that woman of great arrogance,
That infidel with an armful of ass,
Could not get an answer and turn back;
They could not speak about the falcon.
That woman, a true affliction—
They could not venture into her heat,
Could not enter her presence and speak,
Nor turn back without entering.
The two companions, like twins,
Stood taking counsel,
Arguing the matter firmly,
Neither listening to the other.
That fiercely wrathful woman's
Palace they could not enter;
They could not offer their greeting,
“We let Akshumkar escape”—
They could not explain what had happened.
They stood at an impasse,
Tormenting themselves thus.
Chubak's son Kanchoro said:
“Hey, my dear Kulchoro,
Listen to what I say, companion;
Go to your sister-in-law, companion;
Say, 'We let Akshumkar escape,'
Tell her plainly, companion!
Does she know about the falcon?
Go ask what she knows.
Broad-jawed, broad-tongued,
You were a man who never faltered;
A ready orator, skilled in reasoning,
You were a lad who mastered speech,
One who knew how to manage words.
Go to your sister-in-law;
Does she know about the falcon?
Speak to her tactfully.
Whether she knows or does not,
You are a brave man, companion—go!
To that devilish woman,
That impetuous infidel,
Oh, my Kulchoro, go!
Tell her the matter of Akshumkar;
By the shores of Ala-Kol,
Tell her the grief of having him taken.
He who licked the sword's edge—
If she tells about the falcon,
Tell her your generous lord's grief!”
As Kanchoro said this,
“Yes, elder brother, yes,” he said.
Bending his knee toward the ground,
From upon Akborchuk,
He sprang down and ran.
Kulchoro flew like an arrow,
Looking to either side,
Licking his lips and nose.
Almambet's only son
Was broad in his right shoulder;
He was a lion sprung from Ogoi,
Thick-armed and stone-hearted,
Surpassingly handsome, and a lion,
With his blue sword girded on,
If you could see Kulchoro,
Bounding like a blue tiger!
At the sight, the heart was stirred;
To that truly pampered wife
He now came into view.
To that excitable infidel,
That exceedingly pampered woman,
Wailing and bracing herself,
Swaying as though struck by a spirit,
That infidel, pampered beyond measure—
Before the lad could face her and speak,
Sidling like a racehorse,
Your grief-stricken, pampered sister-in-law
Addressed the lad and began to speak,
Softly speaking, then forcefully:
“Your rude elder Semetey—
The winged steed beneath him,
Is it safe and sound,
And the falcon on his hand?”
When your sister-in-law spoke thus,
The companion stood and said:
“Noble sister-in-law Chachykei!
Your affliction has passed away;
Your good fortune has increased.
Tell of the sheath that takes a sword,
Sister-in-law visited by Khyzyr,
This time tell the truth!
Of torment and death,
Think of every kind and speak;
If you are knowledgeable, sister-in-law,
Tell it without leaving anything out.
We have left mighty Buurul hungry;
By the shores of Ala-Kol
We let Akshumkar escape!
We heard from a man of counsel:
If it were not for your obstinacy,
We heard that you would know.
My elder Semetey,
If you tell us about the falcon,
Would say neither this nor that,
Would not lash your back over the shoulder,
Would not take a rival wife after you,
He made a solemn promise.
Tell us, sister-in-law, of this matter!”
When the lad said this,
Semetey's impossibly pampered wife,
That infidel, flew into a frenzy.
“Fie, fie upon your face!
You have spoken ugly words.
A man out on the plain
Has business with his hunting bird;
A woman in the home
Has business with her food.
That falcon you let escape—
What business has a woman sitting at home
With it, and for what reason?
Vanish from my sight;
Do not come near me!
You who babble glibly,
You who speak in mockery,
Damn your father's grave, scheming slave!
Vanish from my sight!
I shall slaughter you now,
Damn your father's grave, devilish slave!
I shall gouge out your eyes now!
Understand what I say and go;
You and your bloodthirsty master together,
Go down into the black earth!”
