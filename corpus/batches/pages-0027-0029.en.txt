Against Beijing of Chin-Machin
He waged a mighty war.
He led them away to live in the mountains;
This little remnant of our people
My forefather kept safe.
Now they have heard the news
That Karakan is dead.
To raid and conquer us,
If your Chinese have the power,
To enslave us all together,
In an army swarming like black insects
These Chinese have come,
Setting forth from Kakan.
Their country is the land of Beijing;
They are more numerous than insects,
A deeply rooted Chinese people.
To put an end to us, these unbelievers
Have set forth fully prepared.
A burning coal has fallen on our necks.
If Manchus and Chinese join forces,
If they make ready to seize us,
There is no one who can answer back.
The Chinese are countless, and we are few.
What can I do against God’s decree?
Let us obey the demands
Of the deeply rooted unbeliever.
Without a word of defiance, without a word,
Let us give him what he asks.
We have no khan who can answer back;
Against the roaring unbeliever
We have no strength to wrestle,
No strength to grapple.
He has brought the end of the world upon us;
We have no strength to struggle with that dog.
If we try to match the unbeliever,
The warrior Alöökö Khan,
When his commander gives the order,
They will sweep over us together.
His Torguts will gather, every one;
We are only a little people—
They will trample us into the earth.
The Black Kalmyks and the Solons
Will unleash havoc.
Even children of seven years,
If those unbelievers grow enraged,
They will make slaves for life.
We have no spear to thrust before us,
No means to struggle with this people.
See: what we see today is gone tomorrow.
If calamity falls upon our poor heads,
If we stand up to the unbeliever,
He will scatter us like dust,
Sweep us away without stopping.
The surging unbelievers
Will bring hell down upon our heads.
Children, we have no support to lean upon,
No hero to bind at our side
Who could face them in battle.
We have no strength to fight.
We have been brought down into the depths;
We have always been so few.
If nine wolves attack one sheep,
Will they leave its carcass?
If the countless Chinese strike our little people,
Will they leave even our remains?
The many Chinese, a deeply rooted people—
Can anyone who challenges them prosper?
Let us do as Alöökö
And Molto command us;
Let us give them what they want.
Let us drive seventy red camels,
Load red gold and white silver ingots
Upon those camels,
Saying, “Do not shed blood in vain,”
Let us take them as tribute.
Alöökö, who has come from Beijing,
Has come to take hostages;
Let us raise him up as khan.”
Bay and Baltay flared up:
“At worst, we shall die.
If our poor lives come to an end,
We shall face the Day of Judgment.
When death arrives and our days are done,
We shall suffer the torment of the grave!
If the Chinese gain command of us,
Will they not sound their drums,
Take us all as plunder,
Slaughter all your old people,
And only then turn back?
If the Chinese make us their subjects,
Will they not say, “You have stolen”?
Finding some pretext, will the Chinese
Not devour all the people?
They have a khan called Lungtung.
If these Chinese fall into my hands—
While I still have my own head,
How can I surrender my village?
Alöökö has gone mad;
Let me try grappling with that dog!”
Tekeche Khan and Shygay Khan,
And the resolute Akbaltay—
Before poor Baltay, saying this,
Had even closed his mouth,
Pale mottled standards, crimson banners—
May God never bring such a sight upon us—
Uproar and cries of “Boy, boy!”
Dust whirled into the sky;
Countless troops poured in from every side.
Shouting “Kakan!” as their battle cry,
With brass pipes shrilling,
With brass flutes rattling,
With spearheads swaying,
And soldiers’ heads bobbing,
The axemen drew apart;
The spearmen appeared in front.
Dust whirled into the sky;
The Torguts gathered, every one;
The Kalmyks formed a separate body;
The Manchu army appeared at the front.
Their marksmen who never missed
Came sweeping forward together.
Their skillful bowmen,
Their mighty lasso throwers,
Their spears bound round with bronze,
Towered above the rest.
They rejoiced at the word of battle,
Never opened their guard in fear,
Trampled those who fought them
Into the ground like hares,
Reduced their opponents to helplessness.
Their champions were unlike others:
Bull-necked, with the thighs of male camels,
They ruined those who struggled with them.
Their warriors were many.
Döngö’s army was one ilek,
Sweeping in from the east;
Alöökö’s army was six ilek,
Making ready from the west.
Towering in majesty, fierce in wrath—
Now abandon hope for your borrowed life.
Their khans, such as Akbaltay, were distraught;
Shrieking, they wept,
Karakan’s orphaned children.
They shattered the stone mausoleum,
