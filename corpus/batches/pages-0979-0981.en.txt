Let them not disobey and meet their deaths!”
With six swift horses,
And a letter the size of a palm,
He took the twisted brass spear,
Grasped its end and swung it round.
He mounted him on Zhelmaian,
His felt cap like a yurt's roof covering,
And sent the hero Shuutu.
The messenger went with the news;
Before Khan Koshoy
He laid down the letter.
Once the letter was given to Koshoy,
Koshoy read the letter then,
Read it swiftly without turning aside,
And, reading, shouted aloud:
“You twelve Muslim khans,
Come, all of you!” he called.
Hearing this, the Muslims
All came in excitement.
“We shall go to Beijing!”
They all cried, equally stirred.
“I shall make the journey to Beijing!
While valiant Manas is with us,
A waist like a sheath, a face like the moon—
I shall take a wife from Beijing!”
Many rejoiced with such thoughts.
“To Beijing, beyond even a holy one's reach,
He will drive us and get us killed,
March us on a distant journey,
Bind us, drive us, and get us killed!”
Many more were distressed at this.
Great throughout the whole wide world,
Aykhozho of Andijan
Had come to serve the faith.
Koshoy and the hero Er Toshtuk,
The khan, came together.
The Muslims who had stayed behind
Now all came along.
It was where they counted everyone,
Where the forty companions divided the guests.
All the companions made ready,
Preparing to receive their guests.
At the sight of the forty companions,
Those who arrived were amazed.
Unable to withstand their imposing presence,
Their minds were bewildered.
If you looked at the forty companions,
They wore swords of gold;
The heroes, slender as awns,
Held their spears steady.
Among the horses, the pied-headed racers;
Almambet and Chubak, their mounts a match,
With the hero Bakai in their midst,
All together, lions.
When they counted the people,
The Muslims who had come from Kokand,
When they entered the people in writing,
The army numbered twelve great hosts.
Azizkhan's son Almambet
Had also pitched a thousand yurts.
Akbaltay's son Chubak
Had also pitched a thousand yurts.
Ajybai and Bakai, the two khans,
Pitched a thousand yurts.
The leopard named Kazan,
Their khan, pitched a thousand yurts.
Each of the forty companions,
Every one, pitched a thousand yurts.
After entering the people in writing,
After reckoning the journey,
Almambet and Chubak took the lead
And divided the people among them.
They slaughtered mares to entertain their guests;
Those with the means slaughtered camels.
They crunched sugar like snow,
Sipped their black tea,
And drank spirits to quench their thirst.
The twelve Muslim khans
Had all flocked to Talas.
The great army bound for Beijing
Was fed for eighty days.
The hilltops were broad green meadows;
They had ranged across those green meadows.
Yraman's son Yrchy,
And Baabedin's son Baychoro—
No guests were left for them.
Yraman's son Yrchy,
And Baabedin's son Baychoro,
When no guests were left for them,
“Rather than live in this world,
Let us die!” they thought.
“If there is fairness, let us
Have our guests!” they thought.
“If there is no fairness,
Here in Talas itself,
Without mercy, a great commotion
Let us raise!” they thought.
Finding words in a sudden crisis,
Finding a path in the dark,
The wise leopard Bakai
Saw their mood from afar.
From every camp, a hundred guests
He levied and reassigned.
For those two indignant men,
Khan Bakai of great authority
Now found guests.
The appointed time had come,
For Beijing in Kakanchyn:
It was time to set out.
Your dear sister-in-law Kanykei
Had every piece of equipment
Made ready, had she not?
Impenetrable to bullets when worn in battle,
Every piece of clothing
She had prepared, had she not?
She sent word to her people:
“My little handful of Muslims,
May no stray bullet strike you!
‘Blackening, burning embers,’ they say;
‘Thick-flying bullets in Beijing,’ they say;
‘Pits dug ready,’ they say;
‘Great Beijing, land of unbelievers,
Is a harsh and perilous road,’ they say.
Muslims going to Beijing,
Take the coats I give as gifts;
Take the hand of my generous champion khan!
