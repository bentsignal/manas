The very heavens grieved though they had no horse:
“If only we could go
To Beijing, city of the Chinese khagan,
Against the Black Chinese and Manchus,
To stand as support for the bloodthirsty hero
And fight beside him;
Against the broad-booted, cotton-sashed,
Kongurbai, khan of the Kechil,
That swaggering swine,
To take revenge on him—
If only we could meet him;
Against the vengeful Kechil,
If only we could face him;
Against the Black Chinese and Manchu people,
If only we could encounter them;
Against the teeming Chinese,
To stand beside the gray wolf
And die together!
Without spare horses, we two
Are fated to remain at the pass;
Our grief is great, but there is no remedy—
We are fated to remain at the pass!”
Grieving, the boys said:
“Against Kaldai in his karapjy,
We did not charge and strike with spears;
Against the Manchu in his otogot,
We did not duel him with loud cries,
Nor stand wreaking havoc!
With no tulpars beneath us,
We did not go and see Beijing;
We did not support the gray wolf,
Face the enemy, and die!
With neither khans nor people,
We are fated to remain in the desert!
Parted from the noble heroes,
These glass-bright eyes shedding tears,
We are fated to remain at the pass!
If noble Bakai and Semetey
Return safely, our road is blessed;
If by chance Beijing defeats them,
Our sorrow will never heal!
When one longs for it, is there
A night like the sacred Night of Power?!
Will the day come when my khan and uncle
Returns after battle and plunder?
Will the day come when my warrior uncle
Returns without leaving us to grieve?!
If by chance the two khans perish,
We shall see every misery;
Unable to cross the ridge,
Unable to reach a known people,
We shall die while traveling on foot!
A thick net drawn before us,
Heavy misfortune fixed upon our brows!
Our tulpars were sent against the foe,
And our road became beset with hardship!
Go safely and return safely,
You two great men who sustain our hopes!”
Distraught, the boys said this;
Like children whose mother had gone,
Their spirits were torn,
Many thoughts returning to mind,
Tears pouring from their eyes!
They stood grieving bitterly,
Their lot dark with misery.
Sorrowing and mourning,
The boys remained on the plain,
Parted from the two lions.
The boys now remained with the baggage train;
Bakai now rode out to scout.
Along a winding road,
Beside the winding Kara-Suu,
Curving onward on the way,
Your uncle, lion Bakai,
Rode far without sparing his horse.
The perfect tulpar Kökcholok—
No beast could match his walk,
And no wind could match his gallop,
