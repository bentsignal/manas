Let alone any kinsman, the wretch
Gave no quarter even to
Kakanchin's Great Beijing.
His bearing was formidable, his venom strong;
He laid flat whoever grappled with him.
When angered, he was a black-gray wolf;
This son of the leopard
Was red fire to whoever challenged him.
He summered at Middle Keng-Kol
And tethered his fourteen horses.
If an enemy rode out shooting,
He drove them before him.
When they provoked his anger,
He caught and chewed them in his fangs.
Offspring of a lion-hero,
He was never one of the common folk
To offer resistance.
His courage was high and his venom strong;
He laid flat whoever challenged him.
When Külchoro arrived safely,
When Semetey saw his companion,
He cried out, invoking Babedin;
Saying that the boy had returned safely,
Thirty daughters-in-law and thirty girls,
With peerless Chürök at their head,
Ran about crying out.
Then Aichürök entertained them as guests
And held games and diversions,
Gathering the elegant women.
The outer shell was bead-stone,
With many kinds of sugary foods.
Crying aloud, "Babedin!"
Your formidable sister-in-law Aichürök
Slaughtered a barren nursing mare,
Set out the food and drink,
And served tea in the aptama.*
She had thirty daughters-in-law and thirty girls
Gathered in extraordinary numbers,
Had them play the orompoi game,
And sated their longing for games.
Small-mouthed and camel-eyed,
With dark eyes casting sideward glances,
In crimson dresses and with moonlike waists,
With dark eyes flashing,
She had exactly forty
Of the most precious beauties brought,
And held magnificent games.
In dürüyö* dresses with foxlike waists,
She had the dear women brought
And held games of every kind.
None of the heroes slept;
Their sides did not touch the earth.
They did not prop themselves on elbows to sleep,
Nor rest for even a brief doze.
When all the heroes had risen,
Azhibai came up
To hero Semetey,
Offered greeting before him,
And Semetey returned the greeting.
The formidable orator Azhibai
Raised his voice and began to speak.
Broad-jawed and flat-tongued,
He was a servant who never stumbled in speech;
A swift orator and reasoner,
He was a courageous speaker.
He sharpened his black tongue
And sang out before the khan.
When he spoke, he faced a man's soul;
His words set a man's soul aflame—
Such a servant was he.
His eloquence and wisdom
Were equally matched.
They called him Azhibai the orator,
A resourceful man who found the argument,
A hero who spared nothing.
To hero Semetey,
Azhibai spoke a word
And planted a foul thought in his mind:
