He called the forty monks:
“Because of your ignorance,
Because of you forty monks,
I rode far and wide,
Among the distant Kyrgyz,
Scouting and exploring their roads.
I have looked, forty monks:
There is no ship upon the dark water,
No one to stand against you.
Built and made securely strong,
There is no city besides Beijing.
While I, your champion, live,
No disaster threatens you forty monks!
No city is greater than Beijing.
Unless natural death comes,
Nothing can be done against you forty monks.
Think, forty monks:
I have a ridge securely held,
A place on which I can rely.
Take heed, forty monks:
My fox lies upon its ridge;
My gray wild ram lies there—do you see?—
At its watching post!
My duck lies upon its lake,
Securely entrenched as in a fortress.
Come here, forty monks;
I shall tell you this:
Unless my precious head should perish,
I shall make each of you, in different places,
A lord who never leaves his throne.
Your unending grievance
Is that, guarding the frontier,
You have never taken wives.
If I do not send word to Beijing,
To Karykan in Beijing,
And ask him for forty maidens,
If I do not gather the lamas
And have your marriages solemnized!
If he will not heed my words,
If he will not grant my request,
Unless I die when my time comes,
For your sake, forty monks,
If I do not divide the six khans’ people!
If he will not give them fairly,
I shall break through by force:
If I do not seize their daughters,
For your sake, forty monks,
If I do not stir up real excitement!”
Hearing this, the forty monks
Flung their battle-axes aside.
They all came forward sobbing,
Their knees bent to the ground,
Bowing and worshipping in Chinese fashion.
Before Almambet
They paid homage, crying out.
The forty monks’ khan, Great Malgun,
Stood swaying, without advancing,
Astonished at Almambet.
Coughing in great heavy bursts,
His great axe, as big as a travel tent,
He lifted onto his back.
Great Malgun had grown a giant;
His voice could drive the soul out.
They had fortified his dwelling,
Making a lattice of dark iron.
From the city of giants and spirits
They had brought him and taught him,
Making him learn human speech,
Appointing him to the chief watchpost,
Making him supreme among its guards.
An iron coat no bullet could pierce
Great Malgun stood wearing.
“Cloudy by day, clear by night:
The weather’s character has changed.
Patchy snow and driving sleet—
It looks like Almambet’s weather magic.
The people of forty khans assembled,
Holding a feast with games and entertainment.
They sent word to the people;
The news reached Beijing,
And Kongurbay went away.
To carry his loaded provisions,
There were quite a number of elephants.
Since the fierce one left for Beijing,
It has been exactly twelve days.”
What this man was saying
Was certainly not the truth;
Returning from True Beijing,
Kongurbay was not due to arrive.
Great Malgun made ready.
When Almambet saw Malgun,
He took sharp steel in his hand
And entered battle then,
Attacking the forty monks.
Back and forth Almambet
Sported for a little while.
As he sported, ten men died;
He charged in once again,
And half died where he entered.
He poised himself like a darting snake;
When Almambet poised to strike,
All forty monks were destroyed.
“I have killed all forty,” he said;
How could your champion stop there?
Extending his lacquered spear with a creak,
Drawing and raising his sword,
He headed straight for Malgun.
Great Malgun, born full of cunning,
Hurried, flailing his great limbs.
The one-eyed giant tried to reach
His great mule, lumbering along.
Before he could reach his mule,
That keen edge of dark steel,
Hearing Almambet’s voice,
Charged straight ahead:
Dear young Syrgak.
“Before that wretched blind one mounts,
Let me reach his mule!”
Without looking all around,
The warrior Syrgak reached him,
Sparing not his ocean-vast life.
Beneath him was Kokchebich;
Your winged battle steed
Arrived ahead of an arrow.
With Narkesken in his hand,
Your champion Syrgak reached him,
