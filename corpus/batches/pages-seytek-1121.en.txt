Shameless and honorless,
You have troubled my heart so deeply.
Licking your sword and kissing your gun,
When you left the Kakan lands,
You vowed that you would stand proud as a hero;
That was the promise you gave.
The honor of a nation is a lofty mountain,
And you came for its sake.
With heroic ambition and a valiant heart,
You meant to crush them,
And said you would open
One of China’s great gates.
You meant to ruin my people
And bring them to their end!”
Thus spoke Chongbilgich,
Boiling and raging in fury,
Thunderously hurling harsh abuse,
Until at last he settled the great account.
“No trace of such a man should remain;
Do not let him rave and babble—
Seize him and cut off his head!”
When their khan shouted from the place of honor,
Could the champions beside him
Possibly stand idle and watch?
Once a man is ruler of the people,
Who dares answer him twice?
The executioners who heard the command
Drew their swords from their sheaths,
Rolled up their strong sleeves,
And ran like wild asses.
One seized his arms and another his legs,
And together they pinned him down.
“This swine has acted insolently,” they said,
“He is the very scourge of the nation;
He is no true kinsman at heart.”
Then they struck off his head with a sword.
Chongbilgich condemned Küлүк,
The man who had spoken the truth,
And snuffed out his fly-like life.
“He died for telling the truth;
We too have come to this,”
The people thought with heavy hearts.
“If he destroys one who did his duty,
Whom will his looming wrath strike next?
If he kills his own kinsman,
To whom will he ever remain faithful?”
Until they returned to Beijing,
Chongbilgich sternly poured out his wrath
On anyone who spoke
And on every champion who came riding,
Threatening to kill anyone who uttered a word,
And made them swear to this.
It became a custom among the Chinese
To answer “mөө” when asked, “Did you see a camel?”
Thus they bound the pledge firmly:
“I did not see it” was the one permitted reply.
From generation to generation,
From clan to clan,
The Chinese people thereafter
Abandoned all talk of the quarrel,
Beginning this custom in that way.
Then Chongbilgich,
Reading his magical divinations
And softly reciting a spell,
Said, “If that Burut comes, let him come—
Why should your warrior brother fear?”
The great Kerik who came from Beijing
Saddled his thoroughbred.
He donned immense armor no bullet could pierce,
Mounted a horse no bullet could overtake,
And took a pine-like spear.
