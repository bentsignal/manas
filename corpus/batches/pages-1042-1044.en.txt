He was a hero born a lion.
Poison to the foes who faced him,
He was a hero who had conquered many.
Then Almambet and Chubak, the two lions, said:
“Who has crossed Tal-Choku?
Saying, ‘I shall bring affliction on Beijing,
And the teeming multitudes of Chinese
I shall conquer and subdue,’
The hero Rustam Dastan crossed it.
Who shall cross it after him?
The lion Manas shall cross!”
There was writing on the black stone.
When they read that writing,
There were the names of two men.
Cliffs surrounded it;
Apart from those two men,
No others were in that inscription.
The hero Manas, born a leopard,
Leaning upon his battle-axe,
Looking at the way ahead,
Mounted Aybanboz beneath him
And headed for Tal-Choku.
Above Tal-Choku,
The haze scattered,
And the clouds parted.
Traveling until evening,
Going far without a rest,
He reached the peak at dusk.
As he approached Tal-Choku,
There was snow upon its mountain;
In the hollow at its foot,
Tall willows grew.
Its flat top jutted out in angles.
When one came to Tal-Choku,
A fragrant scent arose.
Spring waters spread in streams.
Between Mecca and Beijing
It stood as a fortress,
Strong on every side.
Considering it, Tal-Choku
Was a place to leave the rear camp.
After they had seen that place,
And tested and learned its true nature,
Almambet, Chubak, and the hero Syrgak—
Kogala and Sarala,
Kokchebich, Kurong, and Aybanboz—
They took off the saddles, leaving the horses bare,
Tied their heads high and tethered their feet.
With their keen steel weapons at their sides,
And white saddles beneath their heads,
Lying with their heads toward the west,
The four champions who had come from afar
Sank into a mighty sleep.
Once they had fallen fast asleep,
A black-spotted leopard
Poised itself at his side;
A black-eared lion
Charged furiously at Manas.
When he saw this dream,
Manas awoke from sleep
And turned onto his right side.
The sky darkened and grew overcast;
Sixty worries crowded into his head:
“If the moon does not rise through the clouds,
What shall become of the night?
If I die in Beijing,
The countless Turks left behind—
What shall become of their lives?
When I am gone,
The many people left in Talas—
What means of deliverance will they have?
Pied-headed horses on the hills;
Behind me, my son is still a child.
Abyke and Kobosh are treacherous enemies.
While I was living in Talas,
There was deceit in their eyes.
If he could, that wretched slave
Was always seeking to trip me up.
There was crookedness in his speech.
Given the chance, that wretched slave
Was always seeking to cut me down.
Common iron will not become keen steel;
Someone raised apart will not become one's people.
Given the chance, that worthless dog—
He is capable of doing harm.
He shares my father,
The son of my mother's rival wife.
When I am gone—
Her forehead sloped back,
Unfortunate Kanykei,
Her share of fortune was scant,
The hair upon her forehead like grass.
When I am gone,
She will bear suffering upon her face;
Her troubles will be many.
Tamarisk-covered Keng-Kol, broad Talas—
Naming the land, he grieved;
The prosperous Kyrgyz, the many Nogoy—
Thinking of the people, he grieved:
“If I should die suddenly in Beijing,
Not listening to one another,
My hapless people in confusion,
Unable to bear one another,
May fall into discord.
The many enemies lying in wait
My little handful of people—
May utterly destroy them...”
Saying this, Khan Manas,
Without taking the strap from his neck,
With a sudden tug, his spyglass
He drew from inside his robe.
Last summer,
Because the spyglass had broken,
He had the face of the earth searched,
From the peoples of far-flung lands,
The master craftsman Bolokbai
Had specially had it brought.
Bolokbai, who had come from Crimea,
Set things stirring when he worked the bellows;
He was a friend of David.
The accomplished craftsman Bolokbai,
From his youth, Bolokbai
Had lived by his craft.
Gazing intently day and night,
Fitting it precisely together,
He had made the great spyglass.
