Collapsed and lost consciousness,
And despaired of his precious life.
Had the Kalmyk not seen it himself,
Back when his beard was blue-gray,
When he faced many young warriors?
Thinking, “I may suddenly die,”
The warrior Kalmyk pondered the disgrace.
He wound the gold-cheeked battle-axe
Around his wrist,
And the cast-iron mace with a head like a camel’s,
He took that along as well.
He was about to begin the upheaval;
Kongurbay had saddled
The swift horse Algara.
“Without taking the vast black army,
I will ride patrol and watch,
Skirting Mount Kaspan,
And divide the road into four.
They say he was born after his father,
The gray-maned Manas;
His figure and bearing,
His form astride a horse,
I will observe carefully and see.
Without seeing the young hero’s courage,
Why drive a host of ten thousand into turmoil,
Driving not a few but a multitude,
Driving them over forty days’ journey,
Driving them into a desert where they would perish,
Soring all the thoroughbred horses,
Paining the champions’ hearts,
Making some of them weep,
Making others ache,
Chilling the people’s spirits?
What inheritance would I gain?
If he is indeed a hero born of Manas,
I will meet him in the peopleless desert
And clash with him.
While my towering head still stands,
Why should I fear for nothing
A boy who has come without companions?
Without bringing ruin upon Beijing,
Without laying waste to my people,
I will get news from afar.
If he truly is Manas’s son,
I will reach him and put him to the test.
Without troubling my people,
I alone will go.
While my mighty head still stands,
How could I surrender my swift horse?
If Semetey is mighty,
I will clash with him and see.
While my strength is in its heroic prime,
How could I surrender my livestock?
The pale boy seems to have gone mad;
I will grapple with him and see!”
Thus Kongurbay declared.
He put on his blue coat,
Coiled like a blue tiger—
Such was Kongurbay’s nature.
His eyeglasses gleamed,
And a pendant-stone like a leather flask
Flashed on the crown
Of the Manchu doomed to burn and die.
From the palace with gates of steel,
The Kalmyk departed in turmoil,
Clad in armor no bullet pierced.
He slung the great six-grooved gun,
Ochogor, over his shoulder.
As though their Kalmyk would never return,
As though he would never see his Chinese again,
Karagul stood grieving.
With a cotton sash and broad boots,
The hulking great Kalmyk,
With a leather saddle-cloth and twisted waist,
The rank-smelling warrior Kalmyk—
At the sight of him the heart was divided;
Among all sons of men ever created,
He appeared the greatest.
