The two maidens, born exceptional,
Fought with spears for eighteen days,
Battling in single combat.
There was not one Muslim beside them;
They suffered their torment alone.
The Chinese were a multitude, she was alone—
What had she done against God?
When Alooke’s Kongurbay
Came in to seize them,
Enduring hardship,
The two maidens stood alone
And exchanged shots for forty-eight days.
When their strength failed,
When the moment came that they might be taken,
They fled this way from Zhyldyz,
Fighting like dogs,
Their strength failing.
Oguz-Ashuu and Tai-Ashuu,
They crossed both passes together.
Kunos, Tekes, Uch-Aral—
They hurried away in flight.
They lay on opposite sides,
Unable to reach with spears,
And shot at one another with bows.
(Kongurbay was skilled,
That swine who knew the tricks of war!)
When the two luckless maidens suffered,
Kardygach’s Telkyzyl—
Alooke’s Kongurbay,
The skilled, mighty Kalcha,
When he fired from the opposite side,
The two maidens were at their wits’ end.
The shot struck across the breast;
Telkyzyl lay dying.
When Kardygach’s certain death
Was about to arrive,
When at the hands of the Chinese
She was about to perish,
Khan Saikal, daughter of Karacha,
Was one of the mighty warriors.
Upon Kardygach’s back
She put a coat no shot could pierce.
For Kardygach,
From Kaip’s daughter Kuyaly,
Surkyzyl, freely bestowed—
She seated her upon Surkyzyl.
A star was eclipsed in the sky;
In plain sight they fled,
And escaped in this way.
Fighting with spears by turns,
They remained among the living.
They traveled upward out of the fire,
Traveled far without sparing themselves.
Kalcha had hemmed them into a narrow place;
Thus the two maidens were worn out.
The two poor maidens, conserving their strength,
Toward a pass that stood there
Made their way for shelter.
Loading their baggage on the piebald stallion,
Enduring terrible hardship,
They traveled by night, never by day.
There is writing on the black stone;
From that day to this,
It bears the names Ala-Aigyr and Saikal.
The Chinese are a costly foe to us;
Such trouble do they bring.
Just as they were about to seize them,
To bring torment upon their heads,
The black Kalmyks and the Manchu people,
Just as they were about to take them as wives,
With golden forelock and tasseled belt,
Almambet, son of Khan Azyz,
The saintly hero Bakai,
Azhybai, son of the Argyn khan,
The finest of the sixty,
The formidable one of the forty companions,
Syrgak, son of Khan Ulak,
Traveled along the lower road,
They traveled far, sparing no horses,
Past cleft, enormous crags,
Past strange, dark mountains.
Bakai followed along their foothills;
Almambet did not travel by day,
But made his way through the night.
To see the maiden on watch,
The grievous plight of Kakan’s people,
To bring back news of it,
Across moonlike Sary-Talaa,
When they crossed and descended,
With their teeth jutting out,
Dead horses lay everywhere.
With their mustaches bristling,
Dead warriors lay in one place.
When the clear dawn broke,
When light fell upon the earth,
When stars departed from the sky,
And light appeared upon the earth,
When leopard-like Bakai looked,
The crust of the black earth
Had been worn away toward the west.
The tracks the coursers had left
Were hollowed like hearth pits.
An army had gone toward the west,
Had gone far, wearing out its horses.
Then your elder Bakai,
My saintly one, stood thinking:
“A mighty army has come from Kakan,
Has come in great numbers against our lives.
It seems they have overrun the mountains,
Fighting along the mountaintops.
The two maidens who went on watch—
Black night has fallen upon their heads.
Meaning to capture both,
They seem to have closely hemmed them in;
They seem to have overwhelmed us,
Scattered all four kinds of livestock!
Hot upon their trail,
Oh, Almambet, my master of hidden arts,
Let us pursue them relentlessly.
Upon the countless Chinese,
Let us bring slaughter in a narrow place.
The two maidens who went on watch—
Bringing slaughter in the narrow place,
Let us rescue them.”
Having spoken these words,
Prince Bakai, the leopard,
And Almambet, son of Khan Azyz,
Grappled with the Chinese,
My saintly master of skill.
Wind-driven drizzle turning into snow—
Your Almambet worked his weather magic.
Gray haze, low-lying fog,
Cloudy by day, clear by night,
The weather’s temper was upset.
Fleeing across to the far side,
Alooke’s Kongurbay,
Sixty Chinese, countless governors—
When these men pursued them,
He fled across and escaped.
Sixty Chinese and a great governor
Were captured there.
A thousand wrestlers, led by the governor,
Bakai took prisoner.
Leaving not one alive,
Almambet, son of Khan Azyz,
Your hero born surpassing others,
Offered them up as sacrifices.
The two distraught maidens,
The mighty ones on watch,
These men rescued them.
Khan Saikal, daughter of Karacha,
They returned to her father.
Manas’s sister Kardygach,
At the world’s end, Zhelpinish,
To Zharmanas of the ill-famed people,
To Zharmanas they gave her.
“She bore one son, a lion champion;
My mother who lies here
Bore one daughter, a warrior.”
Having spoken these words,
Telling of the lion champion who had passed away,
Calling her own son little brother,
“Whom have I but you?” she said.
“For a widow like me,
Apart from you, little brother,
Who is there to look after me?” she said.
Kanykei turned and walked.
On this side of the lattice wall,
On the far side of the curtain,
Upon the bed,
The old woman lay hunched,
The saintly one upheld by her patron,
The venerable old Chyiyrdy,
The elder who had borne Manas.
God had granted her wish;
She had reached ninety-five.
Bent over like a quail,
She had huddled into herself.
When her royal son had died,
“My king!” she had wept and wept.
With no strength to rise,
The old woman had sunk down.
Her eyes had filled with pus;
Her chest had begun to rattle.
All twelve bones of her frame
Trembled without cease.
Call her dead—she was not in the grave;
Call her alive—she was not among their number.
Seen today, gone tomorrow:
Such is this world of shit.
The elder was ninety-five,
Without the strength to stand.
Your dear mother Kanykei,
The old woman lying there in sorrow,
Took her firmly by the forearm.
That same dear mother Kanykei
Spoke to her loudly:
“Venerable old woman, my sacred mother,
My darling mother!
Seeing you, my heart is deeply content;
Mother, I have no sacred refuge but you.
God has granted your wish;
Lift your head and rise, Mother:
Your young in-law has come.
Rise and greet him, Mother!
Your lamenting split the stones;
My darling mother,
You were like a crying snowcock.
‘If only I could see that in-law,’ you said;
Mother, you had longed for him.”
Then Chyiyrdy,
When she heard this news,
Coughing in deep, repeated bursts,
Raised her head from the bed.
Coughing in sharp, repeated bursts,
Raised her head from the pillow.
Taking a golden staff in her hand,
She barely managed to straighten up,
Turning toward her only one, Semetey.
Her dry eyes, so full of dignity,
Your mother turned and wiped.
For the time it takes meat to cook,
She gazed at the boy without tiring.
Watching the boy, full of longing,
Chyiyrdy began,
Pleading then with Kanykei:
“Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
Who would not tire if ridden for six months,
Even upon reaching sixty mature years,
Would not wear down her teeth and grow old—
Oh, Kanykei, my foal,
My tireless black steed with the white blaze,
Let me speak what lies within my belly,
