Covered himself with Akolpok,
Prayed to the spirit
Of his father Manas, the gray wolf,
And fell into a hero’s sleep.
They removed the saddles
From Kökcholok and Taibuurul,
Leaving them bare-backed,
And tethered the ancient coursers high.
They lay upon their sides and rested,
Lay down and slept their fill,
Rested propped upon an elbow,
And took a brief doze.
When the time of truthful dawn arrived,
When daybreak turned yellow,
The stars separated above them,
And dawn appeared, swelling bright.
The stars scattered across the sky,
Trembling toward the qibla;
The cold wind of dawn roared,
And the reed-tops shivered.
Every living creature awoke,
And fledgling larks twittered;
Insects began to stir,
And the sun’s countenance prepared to meet the earth.
Your uncle, old Bakai—
That hero Bakai—
Hero Bakai started awake,
Prayed to the Almighty,
And prepared to give fodder
To the rangy courser.
One tuft of fescue among eighty grasses—
He had the prancing courser
Graze it in great mouthfuls,
And evenly quenched his thirst
At the bubbling spring.
He made Buurul prance,
Rolled him upon the green turf,
And your uncle Bakai, the gray-wolf lion,
Tethered Buurul high.
Scraping him with a currycomb,
They made Buurul a splendid sight
And cleaned away his heavy sweat.
When Bakai looked from the ridge,
The strands of his coat separated one by one;
When he examined him on every side,
The marks of a courser were revealed.
He could race a bird in flight;
The flank-flesh that would yield rump-fat
Had drawn back and lengthened,
Clinging close to the ribs.
His forelegs were short, his body great;
The marks of a thoroughbred appeared:
Mattock hooves and a broad rump,
A courser bred upon spacious land.
Born in sand and raised among rocks,
He had grown apart without seeing a living soul;
He had raced with wild asses,
With Kylkara of Maiden Saikal
And Kongurbai’s Algara—
He had contended with those steeds.
As a three-year-old he was guarded with great care,
With an owl plume fastened upon his head;
Learned Lady Kanykei,
Fearing that someone’s evil eye would strike him,
Had him raised in a dark place,
Without letting anyone know,
Without letting any person perceive it.
Saying his legs would become bronze,
She trained him in ravined ground;
So that he would never shy from a saddle,
She had him drag a lead-rope from a deer.
His back was high and his temper subdued;
He never tired when raced over ridges.
His withers were high and his temper subdued;
He never tired when sent uphill.
Iron hooves and copper forelegs,
A camel-stallion forelock and owl-like feet—
A true kara-baiyr thoroughbred,
