Straining and clenching their teeth,
Fire issuing from their eyes,
Hair bristling upon their faces,
They charged at Madykan.
Thrusting with their spears,
Fighting with their battle-axes,
They could not overcome him.
Then your champion Manas
Flew into a rage.
“From the very beginning I
Have been a lion who slew his enemies.
I cannot master a single Chinese—
Why have I become so weak?
I became the renowned warrior Manas,
Who made the palm-sized Burut people
An equal of the whole wide world.
Many and many a lion,
Whenever he fought me,
I shattered in the end
And leveled with the dark earth.
Unable to overcome one man,
I have become a dug, dark grave!”
Thus spoke the warrior Manas,
Biting his lip till it bled,
His eyes like glowing red embers,
Wind pouring from his mouth,
Flame issuing from his eyes.
Your champion raged;
Your leopard, champion Manas,
Took his lacquered spear in hand.
Speeding like a falling stone,
Thundering like a fired shot,
The blood draining from his flesh,
He charged at Mady.
Who would never shrink from fire,
Nor flee in fear before a foe,
The Chinese giant Madykan,
Thundering like the sky,
Making all who heard him tremble,
His whole body bristling,
Charged at Manas.
Seeing him charge,
Your precious warrior Bakai
Reached him from the flank.
That champion Madykan—
He reached him before Manas
And thrust at him as he passed.
Behind Khan Bakai,
Azizkhan’s Almambet
Also speared him as he passed.
Almambet and Bakai, the two warriors—
When both had speared him in passing,
His hand slipped from the reins.
Chubak and the warrior Shuutu,
The two reached him side by side
And thrust together as they passed.
When those two had struck and passed,
Madykan, giant of the Chinese,
Lost his footing in the stirrups.
Your bloodthirsty leopard Manas,
Racing upon Akkula,
Reached Madykan
From the gray bull’s back
He struck him down as he passed.
The warrior Chubak, standing ready,
The bull beneath him—
He cut off its head as he passed.
When Madykan fell from his horse,
Without looking about him,
Alooke’s Kongurbay,
Sparing not his fragile life, said:
“If Beijing’s khan dies,
What will become of me?
Better that I spare no effort
And reach Madykan,
From among the massed Burut
Rescuing him and taking him away.”
Thus spoke Kongurbay,
Crying out with all his life.
This was the Chinese battle cry:
“Come, come, come!” he shouted,
Calling the battle cry, “Come!”
As he was about to reach Madykan,
As he was about to rescue him,
The warrior Syrgak blocked him again.
At that moment the warrior Bakai
Took his ravenous steel in hand,
Reached him first with a shout,
And as soon as he reached Mady,
Cut off his head as he passed.
When the warrior Madykan died,
A thousand times greater than before
Was the force that came.
Shouting their battle cries,
All the Chinese multitude
Had come together.
Skilled on horseback, swift on foot,
Who came forth when the Chinese faltered,
The great marksman of the treasury,
Their sorcerer had arrived.
In the Chinese emperor’s realm,
He was the one they relied upon;
When they fought an enemy,
He was the one who gave them strength.
His marksmanship was most formidable:
In a dark and moonless night,
The tip of an outstretched spear
He could shoot without missing.
They called him the Great Sorcerer Marksman.
When the Chinese of forty khans
Had found themselves in straits,
When Karykan had given orders,
When the children had been gathered,
With Almambet at their head,
When six thousand children went,
When the six thousand perished
And six children remained,
He was one of those six.
Of Almambet’s skill,
He fell short by three months:
That was his deficiency.
Had he studied three months to complete it,
The knowledge that he possessed
Would have equaled Almambet’s.
That the great marksman had arrived,
The warrior Almambet now saw.
