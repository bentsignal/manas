The gray war belt inherited from his forefathers
Blazed brightly, fastened around his waist.
Like a prince ruling a nation,
He rejoiced when he saw the champion,
Like a man beholding at her wedding feast
His maiden beloved, the beautiful Bermet.
His mighty teeth, broad as a gate,
Showed within his mouth,
While blue smoke thick as an arm
Streamed from the corners of his lips.
Charging downhill, the hero speared
The ninety-four who met him;
Among the Chinese who pressed upon him,
Each bore the fury of a thousand,
Each was a black demon incarnate,
Yet he skewered them two at a time.
Of the forefathers who had gone before,
Which one could have struck more fiercely?
He made Kulansur prance,
And laid low all who faced him.
Those he grappled with he never released;
Some of those champions
Could never be filled though feasts were cooked,
And once they fought, they spared no life.
Their prowess surpassed all others;
They stood foremost before the khan,
Champions of deadly venom.
Swearing to devour the Burut whole,
They had made their pledge firm
And readied every piece of armor.
Of the warriors who came from Beijing,
No fewer than fourteen giants lay dead;
The Chinese advance was cut off.
As the battle raged on,
Chongbilgich roared aloud,
Thundering and surging into fury.
He was said to be the son of Kün-Jangjung,
And they called him Küpü the Wrestler.
He was immensely strong in a grapple;
When he seized a man in combat,
He would twist and break his neck.
He could overturn a mountain in his path
And uproot anyone he encountered;
If a man fled, he never escaped.
He confronted all whose death had come.
His prowess was renowned:
“He is the son of Ai-Jangjung,
This is the warrior from Beijing
Destined for the khan’s golden throne,”
So it was entered in his record.
“He is the greatest of forty tribes;
The misery of every enemy
Who meets him will boil without end.”
His valor was well known;
Clairvoyants in Beijing
And judges of men had tested him.
His nose was like a mountain spur;
He drew breath like a dragon,
As though he could devour the entire world.
His eyes were blue as steel;
When you looked upon his nose,
It resembled a ridge where a rich lord camped.
The black brows above his eyes
Were like a mastiff barking at the fold.
None dared advance against him;
His nature was vicious, his presence terrible,
And no man dared provoke him.
On foot he was a wrestler of surpassing might,
With iron hands trained to perfection.
When he looked upon a human face,
Anyone who confronted him
Seemed to die before death itself.
He was the hero who tore apart nine giants.
There was also a man called Jangyzköz,
Who, they said, paid neither tribute nor tax
And yielded to no word spoken.
He ravaged and destroyed foreign peoples,
A hero who swallowed every giant he fought.
Roaming ceaselessly and ranging over the land,
He was a warrior who smelled of dust.
