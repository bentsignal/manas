Zhelzhetpes spoke on in this way.
Wrapped in a mottled blue robe,
The fearsome Chyngtengir, behold,
Raged like a blue-gray tiger,
And girded on his blue steel blade;
His eyes blazed like a boar's:
— Damn you, Zhelzhetpes!
Who should listen to such words of yours?
Let the dark night listen,
Let beast and living soul listen;
These words that you have spoken—
Let four-footed cattle hear them,
Let the dug black grave hear them,
Let the broad black earth hear them!
When I sent you to survey the land,
You were one of my capable men,
Yet you have always gone about
Feeling pity for the Kyrgyz.
You have become a hindrance to my work,
A wrecker among my men;
It seems you mean to carry this through,
To the stammering Kyrgyz themselves
It seems you intend to go!
You are no envoy, but a land surveyor,
A goldsmith who deceives this nation!
You yourself would ruin the people,
You are the very eye of treachery.
Every word you say points elsewhere;
If I leave you alive,
You will spoil every little undertaking.
Without once looking back toward China,
You hasten toward the Kyrgyz!
If you keep this up, Zhelzhetpes,
You will set fire to my heart
And bring strife upon Beijing.
From time to time, in every age,
An enemy like you has risen within,
Destroyed my people,
And plunged into darkest night
The lofty honor I possessed.
So saying, Chyngtengir
Thundered like a lightning bolt.
When he saw Chyngtengir's wrath,
His brow twitched and his teeth ground;
How could he answer back?
His cunning tongue fell silent.
Though there was no rope or cord,
His hands and feet seemed helplessly bound.
Then, speaking softly but firmly,
Zhelzhetpes began his reply:
You are a warrior, mighty Chyngtengir;
You may cut off this wretched head.
But a people, its treasure, its homes—
You will gain nothing by acting so.
A khan's ambition is a maddened camel;
If it tramples you, it will finish you.
Yesterday, black-jowled Zulpukor—
You drew Zulpukor and brandished it;
Making men lick the sword's edge,
You scattered a thousand of us
And sent us against the white-capped people.
We watched them from without and judged them,
We ranged their land and examined it closely.
They too are sons of men.
Kenen is indeed a mighty hero,
And the army he has mustered stands ready.
Ala-Too is no land cheaply won;
Without spilling blood and waging strife,
Without splitting lungs in single combat,
He is not one to surrender his people.
His courage is no less than yours.
One young lad stands in his support,
The forty chiltens and holy Khizr are his true aid,
Ever present before his face.
He is one of the proven warriors,
A man endowed with sacred grace.
Two twin lions, two champions,
Alym and Kulansaryk,
Are lion-hearted sons he has.
However I look at him from every side,
Kenen is no son to be cheaply taken.
He is no foe you can seize as spoil
And make into your plaything;
His is no people of forty scattered tribes
That you may slaughter and butcher,
Nor am I there in his hands.
If you face them and level spears,
Blood will surely be spilled;
If you meet them man to man,
They will test the limits of your strength.
Use force, and they will fight.
The Kyrgyz warrior's father was a lion;
They strike fiercely and raise battle.
Those pass-ridden mountains are a hell;
A foe that splits lungs and severs heads—
Measured rightly, that is the Kyrgyz!
I have told you truly what I saw:
If you provoke war, you will slaughter them like sheep,
