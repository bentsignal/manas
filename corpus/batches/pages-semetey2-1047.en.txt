Then, slave companion who came between us,
May I fail to kill you before I go!
Will I not find a way to master you?
Will I not stain my spear with your blood,
Cut short your years,
And sever your head, you accursed slave?”
So raging, Ümötöy
Took a blue-steel spear
With a steel shaft and sharpened point,
And struck Kulager once.
Fire boiled beyond his eyes,
And he bit hard enough to split his lip.
He shouted torrents of words,
Raising Köksö’s battle cry.
His shout could split a head,
The force of his voice could shatter stone.
Pouring out his fury, he lunged,
Refusing to turn from bloodshed;
He armed himself with his heavy musket,
Prepared himself, crying, “Stand and do not flee!”
He confronted
The noble hero in his wrath.
Ümötöy entered the fray;
Ümötöy entered a tumult
From which no one could find escape.
His brass spear was edged with steel,
Fit for the broad ravine no bay horse could cross,
A spear from which no living soul escaped.
Ümötöy had truly committed himself;
Stiff with fury,
The madman reached his foe.
“The face of the gray coat of mail,” he thought,
“The lower edge of the heart,
The very point I measured,
The fatal spot to strike;
If I thrust a little farther,
That is the heart itself.”
He attacked Külchoro.
The many Kazakhs behind him
Burst forward in a wild charge.
The surpassing wrestler Ümötöy
Made his companion’s armor ring
With a mighty spear thrust.
Külchoro, lion of the wilds,
Did not so much as grunt when the spear struck.
If struck by a battle-axe, the fierce one
Would never budge from his place.
He stood firm and paid it no heed,
Counting Ümötöy’s thrust
As nothing beside his boot-heel.
The Lord Most High had granted the companion
Valor and lionheartedness
Beyond all measure.
His fortune was complete;
That Külchoro was a true warrior.
He took no account of high or low;
Such was his heroism
That he gave no thought to dying.
He turned the mouth of Surkoën,
The broad-rumped, mattock-hoofed steed
He rode into battle.
“How many more tricks have you, hero?
If your own strength is not enough,
Call all your Kazakhs,” he said,
And stood laughing boldly.
Ümötöy of the Kazakhs
Thrust not once but ten times,
Reeling there like a slave.
Though he struck twenty times, Külchoro paid no heed,
Counting it as nothing beside his boot-heel.
The lion Külchoro stood and said:
“By your father’s grave, Ümötöy,
It seems you have no sense at all.
Put aside this frenzy.
Your strength alone cannot prevail;
Bring on your strongest men—
Your excited Kazakhs, your nation of ten thousand.
Fire your heavy muskets and draw your bows;
