There was neither hail nor rain;
A freezing wind appeared.
All the lesser streams,
Troubled and splashing,
Separated and flowed in every direction.
Not one of a thousand running waters remained;
Only the Great Urgench was left,
Its stones visible in the riverbed.
Aichurok, the daughter of Akun,
That clever-born wretched woman,
Revealed her form as a weather-worker.
Not a spoonful of water ran from the springs;
It lay frozen outside,
As though the depths of winter had come.
So powerful was her incantation
That the heads of the glaciers froze.
Through her spell, the Great Urgench
Lay without flowing,
Only a waterskin's little measure of water stirring.
If you would behold Semetey,
He pleaded with his Allah
And strained like a white tiger.
Driving fourteen horses from the Argyn,
He drove all those horses
So hard they seemed about to die;
Their white foam streamed.
He drove them swiftly without stopping.
Then Semetey grew furious;
As though Kalmyks had raided
The populous Kyrgyz nation,
He had his trumpet drawn out
And advanced in dread majesty.
He urged the fourteen horses onward;
No person could approach his anger,
And no one wished to see his frowning brow.
Of the extravagantly born gray-maned hero,
Which deed can one recount?
He who had pastured at Middle Keng-Kol
And tethered his fourteen horses—
If enemies came out shooting,
He drove them before him,
And, lion that he was,
Caught them in his fangs and chewed them.
Watching him, Choro's
Heart was deeply troubled.
Driving fourteen swift horses,
The doomed man seemed to have come
To cross to the farther bank.
Kulchoro started in alarm.
He ran toward Buurul,
Despairing of his entrusted life;
Distraught, he caught Buurul.
Kulchoro laughed openly
And pleaded with Aichurok:
"My dear sister-in-law!
The enraged lion
May kill me for nothing;
Go smiling openly
To that bloodthirsty man who loves his wife.
My dear sister-in-law!
Beg him to spare my blood.
If you do not ask, it will not end well;
My sultan will not leave me unharmed!"
Having said this, Kulchoro
Stood holding the steed.
Seeing her lord, Aichurok
Stood watching from the riverbank;
Thirty brides and thirty maidens
Watched together in wonder.
Beating the fourteen horses,
Your swaggering unfortunate man
Made ready to enter the Urgench.
To keep his lord from entering the water,
Speaking gently in his distress,
Kanchoro came to his lord
And pleaded, calling him "dear uncle":
