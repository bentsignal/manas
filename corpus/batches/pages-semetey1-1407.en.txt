My champion, gained at death’s door!
Oh, my pale-gray, solemn darling,
My gray hawk, my morning star!
Before now you had not reconnoitered the road to Beijing,
Gone out on the hunt and shot a wild ram,
Nor gained practice with wild game!
You had not judged the quarry’s course,
Nor, standing forth in triumph,
Had you shot down Akkulja!
My beloved foal,
Do not stare too high by accident,
Or loose your aim awry;
Do not let your bullet fly too far,
Lest Kuukulja escape alive
And bring about our ruin!
Let it not dig my deep grave,
Carry this news to its khan,
And set my sorrow boiling!
Let it not give news to the army,
Give the news to its khan,
So that all the Chinese converge,
Drawing their bows like rain,
Firing bullets like hail,
And amid the dense dust,
Standing their ground to fight,
Leave us caught in blackest night.
Give this orphaned soul your support;
The knack of Akkelte was mine,
My breast was wise and my hand skilled,
I had the needed mastery;
When I went hunting I was fortunate,
And had the finest skill!
You who once knew no failure
Had a champion’s prowess;
Do not overshoot,
You careless, headstrong child!
While Bakai spoke in this way,
Fierce anger seized him;
At his uncle, old Bakai,
The blood-drinking one raged
As though he would swallow him whole:
“You have a chest, but no eyes;
Buried old uncle, what are you saying?!
Your beard is white as a swan,
And when the journey is close at hand,
You rush about saying, ‘I shall shoot’—
Time-stricken uncle, what are you saying?!
Long ago, when you were young,
You grew used to shooting wild rams;
You do not recognize your own decrepitude,
You worn-out champion, old man,
And will not stop contesting the road!”
Semetey grew furious,
Gripped Akkelte and pulled it to him;
When Bakai grappled for it,
He swept him aside with his left hand.
Ah, withered world, be damned—
Your aged uncle Bakai
He shoved away, leaving him standing gaunt.
Today the death of your uncle Bakai
At last became plain.
“Bakai was once a great khan;
Take me now without further torment,”
Bakai said, sitting hunched.
“I was once a great man;
God, take me before I suffer this,”
Bakai said, sitting hunched.
Your uncle, once mountain-like,
Stooped like a camel sunk to its knees.
Kuukulja, watching from its lookout,
Set off at a rolling run,
Struck a black rock as large as a house
With a resounding crash;
First butting against a stone
Was the wild ram’s habit,
Then it would tear off grass.
Kuukulja made straight ahead,
Parting like blue mist,
