Deer dwell in its rough country;
Those who have not seen it have cause for sorrow.
Foxes dwell upon its plains;
To gladden human hearts,
It holds the riches of every living creature.
Golden, rolling slopes they were;
May I be their sacrifice!
A place for hunting expeditions.
Was it not the choicest of lands?
Would not those dwelling there flourish?
When they reached the level ground,
Foxes fled in a streaming line.
When they saw those foxes,
The forty mighty men, delighted,
Burst into laughter and rejoiced.
A great, gently rounded, uneven height,
They called it Belendi Ridge.
For Zhanaaly and Karach,
Running their hounds, seeking game,
It was a place they came in season.
Frenziedly pursuing wild asses,
The two khans, Zhanaaly and Karach,
Made their sport in this place.
And so that place
They called Belendi Ridge.
To a saddle-shaped rise
The noble men climbed.
If one sought the people's well-being,
There was no crow to caw,
There was no raven to croak.
Looking across the empty plain,
Driving mountain sheep from the hills,
With five hundred men beside them,
They had come to hunt.
A terrible, unhealing affliction,
They had come into the dust of battle.
That they were Zhanaaly and Karach,
Resourceful Bakay now knew.
“God has delivered the slave,” he said;
“He has come onto the empty plain,” he said,
And smiled beneath his moustache.
Your elder Bakay, the exceptional lion,
“Manas! Manas!” he shouted,
Calling the mighty men:
“What was lost has been found;
Our honor is now preserved.
Did you see what I saw?
Did you perceive what I perceived?
For the servant he wishes to favor,
He lays things ready upon the road.
Your expedition has found good fortune;
Zhanaaly and Karach, the two khans,
Have appeared upon your road.
Drawing bows to rain arrows,
Firing shots to hail bullets,
When Bakay was alone,
They brought him terrible suffering.
He swept down and beset me.
Unable to inflict suffering alone,
Unable to take Körpöyan,
The humiliation Zhanaaly inflicted,
Let us show before his eyes
The violence he himself committed.
Let us bring hell in full;
Striking him into a heap,
Let us seize Körpöyan.
Without giving a joint of meat with its knuckle,
Without giving an orphaned bay-pied yearling,
Let us seize Körpöyan,
Let us place her behind the saddle.
Taking Körpöyan by force,
“Here is the daughter you cherished,” we shall say.
Let us make her a wife taken as booty;
“How do you like it?” we shall say and return.
“We remain without a marriage rite,” we shall say;
Let us say exactly this.
Drawing bows to rain arrows,
That doomed Zhanaaly,
When he attacked, made me suffer.
Firing shots to hail bullets,
When they split up and swept down,
Your Bakay lost his strength.
From his whole body then
Black blood flowed in great drops.
A resourceful khan died for nothing.
While battle cries were ringing,
All the spearmen died.
Concerning those past events,
On account of Körpöyan,
I fought a mighty struggle.
Curse being alone!
I left unable to know my own fate.
I left then unable to take
Körpöyan, my noble wife.”
Your elder, old Bakay,
All those actions,
The saint recounted.
At that time the companions'
Number had reached forty.

All forty companions,
Their elders headed by Kyrgyl,
Were accustomed to grind him like roasted flour.
Standards fluttered in their hands;
All their weapons
Glittered at their sides.
In their midst was mighty Manas,
Appearing like a towering mountain.
The mighty ones of forty companions,
The skilled among spearmen,
The finest among sixty,
Ajybay and Bakay, the twin khans,
Yrchy and Shuutu, equal warriors,
Chalybay and Küldür, the twins,

If caught in red fire,
Would shout and thrust their spears.
Hearing the battle cry,
Khan Manas's forty madmen,
Could they hold back from slaughter?
The forty split in forty directions.
If one beheld their wrath,
They seemed ready to slaughter, leaving none;
Their aspect was that of blood-drinkers.
Manas had gathered them,
Collected from blood-drinkers,
A people called the forty companions.
No mortal could defeat them in combat.
All who attacked them,
They had the custom of drinking their blood.
Thrusting spears and shouting,
Do you think these companions would not try?
The enemy they faced, in every direction,
Do you think these companions would not scatter?
To signal, “Enter battle,”
Tiger Bakay the leopard,
The great drum of elephant hide,
Now beat it, boom upon boom.
All forty companions,
Their elders Kyrgyl and Bakay,
As though slaughter had struck,
Now shouted and thrust their spears.
The forty companions, stirred to frenzy,
Zhanaaly and Karach, the two khans,
They reached those two khans.
Their men numbered five hundred.
Khan Manas's forty mighty men
Plunged among them.
They mingled in battle;
Their resourcefulness was beyond measure.
In the clash, each one,
Of Zhanaaly and Karach's men,
Speared six as he passed.
Making their winged steeds prance,
Their agility beyond measure,
Zhanaaly's five hundred troops,
Before a word could be spoken,
In the opening and closing of an eye,
Zhanaaly's five hundred troops
They slaughtered utterly, laying them low.
Looming like a towering mountain,
Devastating Manas approached.
When they saw that devastating man,
Zhanaaly and Karach, the two khans,
The strength left their wrists,
The coverings left their hearts,
Their chests began to rattle,
Their beards began to tremble,
Their knees bent,
They hurried down from their horses.
The great lead ropes knotted short
They cast about their necks.
Their swift thoroughbred steeds,
They offered them all alike.
Folding their hands against their breasts,
Before blood-stained Manas
They bowed in submission:
“If you are an unbeliever, my blood-stained lord,
You will slaughter us, leaving none.
If you are a Muslim, my warrior,
You will save us.
We too are of the faith of Islam;
In origin we are kin.
Heed our words;
We have submitted to you.
Impose your tribute.
Without ravaging or scattering us, my warrior,
If you are truly a warrior,
Take us as your subjects.
You are the Muslims' lion;
We have agreed to what you say.
Two reins and one lead rope,
We now give to you.
Throughout the year's twelve months,
Impose your tribute.
Without killing or destroying us,
If you are truly a lion,
Take us as your subjects!
You were a warrior, you were a lion;
Let us agree to your commands!
Sixty otter pelts, a thousand silver ingots,
Let us give these each year!
To maintain a khan's treasury,
You need sacks full of such wealth.
If you clash with those of another faith,
If you fight with unbelievers,
Subjects such as ourselves,
Even if poor, you need a people.
Formerly, there was your saintly elder Bakay,
And all the champions were there.
We were left where we had not died.
Contending with Bakay over a girl,
We made a splendid battle.
Since you yourself have come, blessed by Khizir,
Let us agree to what you say.
The finest girl, Körpöyan,
Let us give her as a gift.
A khan's heart is a lake, a river;
Forgive the grievance.
Blood-stained Manas, leopard,
Do you not need supporters?
Zhanaaly and Karach,
Leave us behind alive.
If you roar in terrible might,
Though we are many, we will die.
You are a lion, we are mice;
If you strike, what will become of us?
Be willing to heed our words.
If your heart remains unwilling,
Overturn and slaughter us all.”
Zhanaaly and Karach's numerous troops
Dismounted and gathered round.
To the magnanimous lion himself
They turned and submitted utterly.
Even in death there was no regret;
Against leopard lord Manas,
None remained who could turn a lasso pole!
Taking Khans Zhanaaly and Karach,
Such was the magnanimous man's exploit.
If the wolf-man lived,
His havoc would reach the nations.
He struck stone and made a road,
Laid many lands low.
Making a costly sheath for his sword,
Raising strife in many lands,
Large fur caps and long coats,
Spreading boundless deeds of valor,
Subduing the people of Chambyl,
Long leather shoes and leather trousers,
Raiding the Kalcha and taking girls,
Your blood-stained warrior had set out,
Working his craft upon the earth.

Could any land remain whole?

Could any warrior remain whole?
Unstruck by the gray falcon,
Could any swan remain whole?
Untouched by that Manas,
Could any people remain whole?

Before this or that could be said,
In the opening and closing of an eye,
Körpöyan, of whom Bakay spoke,
Was the finest of women,
The daughter of Khan Karach.
They dressed her in exquisite silk.

Your elder, wolf-man Bakay,
Received Körpöyan as a gift.

They stood spreading gossip, they say.

There is a striking tale to tell:
Against the magnanimous man and Bakay,
Zhanaaly and Karach bore a grudge.
Zhanaaly's Narkyzyl,
A true winged steed with no brains,
A pacing gait on the road, gentle to its rider,
A great broad-chested winged steed,
Owl-footed for long journeys,
No such horse could be found.
Upon the earth, for a warrior,
No such swift horse could be encountered.
Your elder Bakay pondered this.
The finest of forty companions,
More vigorous than many,
Open-browed, broad-chested,
To capable Boz Uul,
Your elder Bakay the leopard,
Biting his lip and winking,
Without the forty companions seeing,
Was making a gesture.
His mighty man understood a wink,
One who made the distant near,
A close kinsman of Manas,
Toward Khan Bakay
He leaped like a roe deer.
When he came to Khan Bakay,
Such was Bakay's way,
Known among warriors for his wisdom;
He spoke to Boz Uul:
“Chambyl-Bel is no land of ours.
Zhanaaly and Karach, the two giants,
At bottom are no people of ours.
Of Zhanaaly and Karach, the two,
Deceit lies in their words,
Mischief lies in their eyes.
When they grow fat and recover,
They will chew through the lead rope.
If we become a little weaker,
They stand ready to quarrel.
When we leave this place,
He will throw his leg over the tethering rail;
Zhanaaly, whose words are cold,
Will sound the trumpet's tune.
Before he returns to this land,
Narkyzyl, whom he will not give if asked,
Go cast a threatening lasso upon him.
Narkyzyl, whom he will not give openly,
Ask unseen and bring him back.
One fit to take as booty,
For Manas's wife Akylay,
He is a fitting mount.”
When Bakay said this,
Boz Uul flew like an arrow,
To Zhanaaly and Karach, the two,
And now reached them again.
Narkyzyl beneath him
Has a whole armful of tail,
He shows the marks of a winged steed,
No ordinary tail and mane,
No mount for ordinary men.
I came desiring the Red Steed;
At the sight of this Red Steed
My heart was stirred anew.
With a bloody spear in hand,
Swaying this way and that,
He is a mount for the bloodstained warrior;

He is a mount to ride to battle.

He will not falter on the hills.
He shows the marks of a winged steed,

And of him who came from Altay and became a giant,
Who became lord of the Noygut people,
The blessed Khan Akbaltay—
Let us begin the tale of him.
A just khan to his people,
Noble Baltay at that time
Was a blessed man.
Akbaltay's confidant,
His companion into the next world,
Was the hero Kutubiy.
He took Alay as his homeland,
Maintained the Noygut as his people;
His yaks numbered ninety thousand,
His wealth was renowned among the people;
His horses numbered seventy thousand.
He was a fully accomplished, formidable khan;
His livestock countless, but he had no child.
Akbaltay was wretched,
With no way to beget a child.
To hear a single child's voice,
To attain that one desire,
All his countless livestock
He spent and squandered,
Begging God for a child;
Even then he had no child.
Days and nights passed;
Many years passed between.
Having no child, Akbaltay
Had become a wretched man.
For the sake of a single child,
Begging God, Akbaltay
Took sixty wives.
Not one of the sixty gave birth;
He remained without an heir.
What divine power had ordained,
Wretched Baltay experienced.
“These are livestock with no head to lead them;
What use is keeping them?” he said,
And set apart half his livestock.
“What use are herds with no heir?” he said,
Suffering for want of a child.
Half his livestock Akbaltay
Among orphans and widows
Distributed and gave away.
Though he spent his abundant herds,
Though he slept at sacred shrines,
Though he begged God for a child,
Still he had no child.
He scattered his abundant livestock
And was left full of regret.
Calling his Kutubiy,
Akbaltay was stricken:
“Rather than languish like this,
I shall wander away to Mecca,”
Akbaltay declared.
With sixty retainers, Kutubiy
To scout sixty hills
Had set out at that time
On a hunting expedition.
When the people moved toward higher ground,
In early summer, when horses fill on green grass,
When he came out onto the ridge,
With many people as companions,
Akbaltay lamented:
Longing for a child to carry on his line,
His eyes had grown inflamed,
His chest had begun to rattle.
Longing for a child, Akbaltay's
Beard had become white;
His time to die was drawing near.
“I shall remain childless,” he said,
“When this wretched childless man dies,
Like wheat growing on dry ground,
I shall leave no offspring.”
Caring not a hair for his bare life,
Baltay suffered and languished.
For the sake of a child, damn it,
Noble Baltay languished.
Looming in mist at the corners,
Ending in haze at the edges,
On the face of the great plain,
Of the Mediyen desert,
At its nearer edge,
Folded into a saddlebag,
Loaded upon a pale she-camel,
Laid in white felt,
Tied upon the camel's hump,
God Most High had fittingly
Placed the child there at that time.
Oysul Ata, with the pale she-camel,
Upon that she-camel's hump
Had tied him there at that time.
Before Akbaltay,
Oysul Ata's pale she-camel—
An animal bred in Akbaltay's own herd—
Came straight toward him.
When the she-camel approached,
From her very hump
Came crying: “Wah, wah, wah, wah!”
The child wailed and cried loudly;
As the child wailed and cried,
He cried piercing ears and nose.
When he heard the child's voice,
What did poor Baltay do?
The grief in his breast was released,
Tears streamed from his eyes;
A child was found through unseen powers,
And sorrowful Baltay's
Honor was thus preserved.
The child in the saddlebag
Wretched Baltay now took.
Against the bare flesh of his body
He laid that child.
A cry rang out before him;
That Baltay then
Called to Kutubiy:
“Oh, Kutubiy, my companion,
My confidant who shares even death!
My honor is now preserved,
What I sought has been found,
What was severed is joined,
What was scattered is gathered,
My extinguished fire is lit,
My dead soul has come alive.
My dear Kutubiy,
I have a child—know this!
My Kutubiy, a reward for glad tidings!”
Thus spoke Akbaltay.
His many thoughts overflowed his breast,
His tears streamed in ten directions;
His white beard was thinning,
And tears flowing from both eyes
Poured down past his collarbones.
When he saw Khan Baltay,
Kutu too wept at the sight.
Seeing the aged old man,
Everyone who looked on wept.
Then Akbaltay
Said to all who had come:
“My companions, my friends,
You, my only eyes beneath my brow,
These are the words I speak:
That I have found a child,
Do not let a single person know;
Do not let a single person hear!
Understand well what I say;
Chew white wheat flour.
Swear yourselves to silence,
And take the holy Quran in your hands!
See how exceptional my generosity is:
My countless herds of livestock—
Divide them all, leaving none!”
Thus spoke Akbaltay,
