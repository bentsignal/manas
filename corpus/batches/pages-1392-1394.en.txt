He was a true warrior beyond measure,
The youngest of nine sons—
A true lordly lion.
The battle steeds he had captured,
Before mighty Manas,
He now presented as a gift.
Together with this offering,
The missing Toshtuk had been found;
The great army had good fortune,
And its honor was thereby saved.
All the twelve khans,
When they brought the matter before them,
Found counsel and wisdom.
Lifting his head and turning aside,
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan,
His wide eyes like the morning star,
His moonlike ears like shields—
If words were difficult beyond measure,
The warrior Koshoi untied their knot;
If a lion rose against him,
The warrior Koshoi humbled him.
Calling like a gray-mottled bird,
To the people of the twelve khans,
To them all he spoke thus:
“Iron armor that no shot can pierce—
You who wear it, go to Beijing!
Who can untie the knot of words—
You who know how, go to Beijing!
When evil days fall upon your head,
Who will neither grieve nor sorrow—
You champions, go to Beijing!
Who have sharpened your dark tongue,
Who speak out without faltering—
You warriors, go to Beijing!
Broad jaw and wide tongue,
Who speak without a slip—
You orators, go to Beijing!
Whichever of you will go, then go;
Go and speak without holding back!
If he gives Burulcha as a gift,
Bring the hapless girl back.
If he will not give his daughter as a gift,
‘There is no death for an envoy,’ they say:
Return without much delay.
The evil you have seen in Beijing—
Return and tell what you have seen!”
Thus spoke Khan Koshoi;
While he was speaking,
The great army of the twelve khans
Sought each other out and met,
Bringing the matter before them.
They were a people of twelve tribes;
They went through them clan by clan.
With no one coming forth as envoy,
Yellowing as if with jaundice,
They were struck down by God.
As though they were about to die,
They hid behind one another.
The reason for their hiding was this:
In the Chinese emperor’s Beijing
There were pits dug ready,
There was brine kept boiling.
There were fortified mountain passes,
There were places they relied upon.
Those who went there never turned back;
No living person returned—
Such were the places there.
Of sending an envoy to Beijing,
The twelve khans had spoken.
To give an answer to the people,
Jamgyrchy of the Eshtek—
Such was the lord they had.
Jamgyrchy rose and spoke:
“Who joins what has been torn,
Who gathers what has been scattered—
Let great Bakai go!
Who wields the mottled gray shield—
Let wolf-man Bakai go!
To these Chinese before us,
On a dark and moonless night he will go.
If it is not the lion Bakai,
Who else will go?”
Thus spoke Jamgyrchy.
While he was saying this,
As if pierced by a spear,
Leaping up from his place
And taking his sword in hand,
Khan Manas stood shouting:
“When Bakai goes as envoy,
Will not the many Chinese gather
And put him in their cage?
Will they not take Bakai
In exchange for Ezkara?
Will my people not then fall into misery?
When Bakai is held captive,
Think of it, you great people:
Will the mist ever leave your heads?
If gold and golden coins
We gather and give to the Chinese,
Will the whole world suffice to ransom Bakai?
When Bakai falls into their hands,
Will they not put their feet up on the wall,
Will they not play the Chinese tune of swagger,
Will they not say, ‘Come if you dare, Burut’?
Will they not speak like this?”
When Manas had spoken thus,
The people thought and said, “That is right.”
These words of Khan Manas
All the people found fitting.
“Jamgyrchy of the Eshtek,
If this bastard carries on like this,
Will bring us much misery,” they said,
And all the people loathed him.
Ajybai, son of Argynkhan,
Born exceptional as a goshawk,
Who found a way in darkness,
Who found words when taken unawares—
That lord was Khan Ajybai.
When hardship fell upon their heads,
He was the warrior who found a way.
Knowing the tongues of seventy tribes,
He was the son of perfect eloquence.
They dressed him in a coat no shot could pierce,
They mounted him upon Kartkurong,
A double-edged, stone-cutting blade—
