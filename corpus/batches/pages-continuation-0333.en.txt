To go among the people.
Then Zhelzhetpes said:
“We shall keep going on patrol;
Sooner or later we shall learn
How great his fury is.
Let us survey the land,
Let us scout the far side of the ridge.
We shall speak misleading words,
Avoiding battle with the white-capped people,
Binding the army and leading it astray,
Guiding the host by another road,
Gathering up its surging ranks;
We shall drive this hellish infidel
Against the mountains and the rocks,
Turn his army into a flock of blind sheep,
Lead them the wrong way,
And set them on a false road.
Somehow we must save
The offspring of the brave Kyrgyz,
The descendants of the valiant wolves.
Let us pray for the people’s safety;
Say there is escape in flight,
And, if we can, shepherd the people away.
If we do not carry out this ruse,
No pleading will save us
From this cruel, mighty swine.
He destroys anyone who stirs;
He makes wretched women and young children weep,
With no fear of the sin.”
Having said this, Zhelzhetpes
Told it to those who would go,
And firmly instructed the envoys.
Clad in a loose, drooping coat,
Mounted on a long-striding mule,
He set out on a journey of many days,
Crossing passes and roaming deserts,
Enduring great hardship;
Thus Zhelzhetpes went on his way.
He crossed rough, humped passes,
And followed many winding roads,
Saw rivers and deserts,
Saw the lands of many tribes;
Day waned and night passed,
His poor life nearly spent,
Yet where would a hero ever shrink back?
He reached the Ala-Too.
When he looked upon the Ala-Too,
It held every kind of adorned treasure,
And foxes in countless hosts;
There were deer in its sheltered places,
Ibex on its crags,
Snow upon its mountains,
Game within its forests;
There were lions and wild boars,
Bears and tigers as well;
It had sorrel grass for fermenting mare’s milk,
And an abundance of every wealth.
It was a land inhabited by heroes,
A people living free and at ease.
Zhelzhetpes saw all this.
“If that furious swine attacks,
Where will their villages settle?
What recourse will they have?” he thought.
Seeing their blue-green pastures and rocky cliffs,
Their thoroughbreds and swift steeds mingled together,
He grieved at the sight of their herds.
All radiant and crimson,
He grieved at the sight of their lives.
Before he could send word to the people,
The wise Ebegei and Kogoi,
With their twin sons in their train,
Had ranged an immense distance.
Taking Alymsaryk and Kulansaryk,
The two heroes, as companions,
They had gone on a great hunt,
Hunting lions and pursuing tigers.
The two giants, Ebegei and Kogoi,
Were both beside themselves.
Earlier they had dreamed a dream,
Foretelling calamity upon their heads;
They had sensed a threat to their tiny lives,
And dreamed on behalf of the true Kyrgyz.
Ebegei and Kogoi summoned
The twin tigers, born of one lair,
The two lion-like champions.
When the two arrived,
Ebegei spoke softly and began his tale,
Laying great weight upon its ending.
He came forth into the daylight:
“My sons, interpret the dream I saw.”
Then brave Kogoi began to speak:
“My two tigers, born of one lair,
In my sleep I saw a grievous dream,
And saw calamity upon calamity:
Blood covered the Ala-Too,
Dust covered the plain below;
The people cried out, the nation was in turmoil,
And all was steeped in black blood.”
