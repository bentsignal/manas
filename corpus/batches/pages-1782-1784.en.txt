Perform your service for me;
For Manas, my father’s friend,
Stand at his rite of absolution.
Come, forty holy men, go to him;
Heed my words.
I will set your hearts at ease;
Forty holy men, I will make you rich.
Like starling-speckled Sarala,
A thousand piebald horses I will pledge;
With ring-marked legs, never ridden,
A thousand fat horses I will pledge.
Purebred Karabayyr steeds—
A thousand of these I will pledge.
If for Manas, my father’s friend,
You go and read the Quran,
I will give a thousand dark camels,
A thousand black mares with white blazes.”
Their noses bulging like leather sacks,
The forty holy men blustered and refused:
“Your Manas was a scoundrel;
Your Manas was a wretch;
Your Manas used to oppress Kokand.
Your Manas beat us
And plundered our turbans.
We will not go to your Manas;
We will not heed your words!
We have work, we have money;
We have goods to earn.
When we are idle, we lie about;
We spend our time pruning tobacco.”
Then, saying these words,
The forty holy men refused.
“If you suffered oppression from Manas,
You shall see my might!
I will dig your deep graves.
Damn your fathers’ graves, forty holy men!
I will boil your bitter lot this instant.
I will draw stripes of every kind;
Forty holy men, I will give you
Such a treat as no man ever gave!”
Then Koyonaaly, that impish slave,
The white whip with its black handle
He seized at once and swung;
The forty holy men before him
He lashed with resounding cracks.
The forty holy men scampered off,
Blustering, “Let us go!”
All hiding among the tobacco.
Every one of the forty holy men
Fled on foot, scurrying,
With blood running down their necks.
No beast could match their pace;
Every one of the forty holy men—
No wind could match their trot!
The men to read the Quran
He had now made ready;
Wearing them down, beating and marching them under guard,
He had driven them here.
Setting up a dwelling in Talas,
From those mighty men who came to see him,
Hiding Sultan Manas,
That formidable woman Kanykei,
Her splendid hair as thick as a girth,
Had bound tight upon her crown.
Your dear sister-in-law Kanykei,
With her son left young behind him
And all his brothers hostile,
Was governing the people then.
Her many sorrows overflowed her heart;
Your fierce sister-in-law Kanykei,
Her tears streaming in ten directions,
Her thoughts cruelly torn apart,
Her tears pouring down:
“God will take the generous one,” she thought.
“That dear lion, greater than the world,
May be left without a memorial,” she thought.
She had six hundred pack camels saddled,
Had their ropes set firmly right;
Beyond Andijan,
Across from Namangan,
Within the reach of the khan of Kokand,
The earth of fire-resistant clay
She had brought by hired transport.
She had it mixed in a great vat,
Had goat hair sprinkled in,
Had cattle hair added;
Aksakal’s son,
The mighty Sart Aralbay,
And sixty strong men wielding hoes
She had tread that clay.
“Give six thousand wether goats,” she said,
Laying a levy on the Argyn.
Giving not a stone, let alone a wether goat,
The Argyn moved away.
“Give seven thousand wether goats,” she said,
Laying a levy on the Kyrgyz.
Giving not shit, let alone a wether goat,
The Kyrgyz moved away.
When the Kyrgyz would not submit,
It was as though, when their khan died, they too had died—
Those bastards had been left thus,
All that great people dead!
Or were they a people short of wether goats?
Your noble Manas, the leopard—
Was he a lion for whom they should begrudge them?
Unable to find a way, crying out,
Your noble sister-in-law Kanykei
Stood there weeping bitterly.
(What is seen today is gone tomorrow—
Alas, such is this wayward world.)
Making their knees bend to the earth,
Your champion Koyonaaly
Drove them into Talas,
The forty holy men running.
Dust rose where he went;
That impish Koyonaaly—
When they heard his fame from afar,
The Argyn people were scared out of their wits.
They yielded to what was demanded, they say;
The wether goats levied upon them
The Argyn drove in, they say.
They accepted the levy, they say;
Through fear of that young man,
The Kyrgyz drove theirs in, they say.
