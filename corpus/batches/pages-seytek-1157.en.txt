Rejoicing and paying him honor.
“He is scarcely less than Heaven itself,
This Chyngteñir,” they said,
“The great khan’s beloved child,
The very apple of his watchful eye.”
They laid him upon precious carpets,
And held a feast for sixty days,
Scattering riches throughout the celebration.
Condemned men awaiting execution
Were spared from losing their heads.
Conceiving splendors no man had conceived,
All the people of Beijing assembled
And began a vast celebration.
Every kind of worldly wealth—
Red gold and white silver—
Was scattered from the treasury.
Young boys who snatched it up
Ran out into the streets
And traded it for walnuts.
The children plunged into their games;
“It is the hero Chyng’s feast,” they cried,
And pelted one another with walnuts in the streets.
The people increased its splendor,
Calling fortune down upon his head,
And easing their burdened hearts.
“The lord of Beijing has come,” they said,
And had it written in the chronicles.
High and low, old and young,
Women in white kerchiefs mingled with men.
In radiant Beijing
Countless festivities abounded.
By the Black Khan’s throne,
In the garden where he took his pleasure,
There was a narrow place no man could tread.
Beneath a dragon
Was a well called Zuu,
With twin clefts like a split palate,
Filled with countless portents.
The Zuu Well to which they took him
Had a dragon at its bottom,
Venomous toward every mighty foe.
It was a well of divination,
And terrible was its wrath.
At the bottom of that well
Was a sixty-headed dragon,
Whose breath drew things in from six fathoms away;
Exactly sixty heads it had,
The dragon in the Zuu Well.
Infants
And men by the tens of thousands
Were tested by the sorcerers
And taken to that Zuu Well.
Behold a wonder such as this:
The black dragon sporting in those waters—
Those destined to become loyal subjects
It left safe and sound,
But those destined to oppose the khagan
It sprayed with venom and sucked dry.
When they lowered him into the well,
The Zungtung attendants brought necklaces,
Plumes, and precious coral stones.
From the dragon’s very stench,
Many people fainted.
Yet great Teñir neither died nor vanished,
But emerged unharmed.
Fearing someone might harm him,
They enclosed him behind ninety layers of iron chambers,
Not letting even a breath of wind touch him.
No one with corrupt speech,
No one with an evil eye,
Not a single soul was allowed near;
Thus they reared him thereafter.
They wrapped and covered
His radiant good fortune with ritual care,
And had him raised without seeing sunlight.
“If he survives, he will become great,” they said;
“When angered, he will drive the world
Before him and suck it dry.
For the khagan whom none can withstand,
The towering Mount Kap has arisen.
