Finding no enemy to fight,
Finding no mountain to charge,
He would vanish six months, seven months,
Going off on a long hunting expedition.
When he returned from the hunt,
When my Almam turned back toward Sary-Arka,
Foolish Kokcho gathered troops,
Gathered an immeasurable multitude,
Having heard gossip from the people,
That Akerkech, his friend's wife,
Was his lover, so he heard;
Of skilled wielders of the spear
He gathered all he could.
Intending to exchange fire,
While that Kokcho waited,
Akerkech, Kokcho's wife,
Wiser than other people,
Came out to meet him, sobbing,
Akerkech, his friend's wife,
Weeping, crying out:
“My banner on a red spear,
Dear son, my gift come from China!
There is no shame in weighty words;
Turn your horse's head awhile,
Dear son, stand a little way ahead!
‘Along the ruddy shelduck's waters,’ they say,
‘Akerkech, his friend's wife,
Is this man's lover,’ they say.
‘White wheat flour makes dough,’ they say,
‘The two of us are intimates,’ they say.
The pit he dug became deep;
The Kazakhs gathered a mighty army.
The day clouded over, and spring came;
That bastard Kokcho, Aidarkan's son,
Became an enemy to his own good fortune.
If it is plentiful, it tastes like honey,
They say the gazelle’s flesh is finest;
With the accomplished elder Tölök,
Toward that damned Kokcho, Aidarkan's son.
These Kazakhs standing here
Are no men to stop your laughter,
No lions to break your strength;
Laughing and playing among the Kazakhs,
They are no people to settle beside.
You have lost heart in Kokcho;
Move away to Talas,
Go and make your camp beside
Manas, Zhakyp's only son.”
Akerkech, his friend's wife,
Had spoken these words;
Akerkech wept and spoke,
Brooding on the filthy slander.
Over what had happened last month,
Offended bitterly, to death,
Because of Akerkech,
Because of the troublemaker,
Over this Erkech's affair,
Kokcho, Aidarkan's son,
Deep among the reeds,
He penned him in and left, I heard.
Making the Kazakhs resound with blows,
He beat them and left, I heard.
It was that Almam himself;
It was time for him to enter Mecca
And return from his pilgrimage.
Your eagle and sword in the dream,
Were they Almambet himself?
Your Alake, the one God
Is about to grant you.”
I, your unfortunate mother,
Having heard the truth of this news,
Having heard King Bakai's words,
With sixty wives and forty girls
As attendants by my side,
Set out from Talas.
I traveled across the face of the earth,
A saint well versed in mortal ways,
That uncle Bakai of mine,
I took as my companion.
Almambet, son of King Azyz,
Was due to turn back, I thought,
And I went to meet him in Bukhara.
Having welcomed him in Bukhara,
I gathered khojas and mullahs,
And honored him as my guest for nine days.
Aruuke, Agynai's daughter,
Whose father and mine were brothers,
My younger cousin, I married to him.
A star moved in the sky;
Your father and your Alake,
Friends even in the other world,
He came and settled in Talas.
As soon as he arrived, he befriended
Manas, now gone to Judgment.
Your father and your Alake
Chewed white wheat flour together,
To enter battle side by side,
Taking hold of the Holy Quran.
When Almambet settled in Talas,
When he became your father's companion,
It was then your royal father said:
“Ah, Kanykei, my senior wife,
When paired alaba coats* and sable caps
This is the time they sway;
Joining Argyn and Kyrgyz together,
This is the time we rejoice.
Argyn and Kyrgyz mingled,
Today they dwell together;
Kumis by day, girls by night,
That is the life today.
To this head blessed by Kydyr,
Have all the mighty now come?
With my precious Almam found,
Has the Creator truly granted him?
My sworn friend, my confidant,
For that hungry lion, that noble falcon,
If we do not allot him a coat,
If we show him no respect,
Will God not strike us down?
Wife, will this warrior's spirit not be broken?
Will God not strike us down from the outset?
On Almam's arm,
If we do not hang black steel,
Not growing thin after six months' riding,
For that noble soul himself,
If we do not allot a horse,
Let us die unworthy of the human name;
Rather than lumbering about,
Let the hour of our birth be cursed!
Making his weapons secure,
Then the lion
Let us send forth and see his strength.
I have Chinese on my flank,
I have Franks before me,
Many enemies stand ready to oppose me.
Go, my wife, go,
Your father's Aktulpar,
Find a way to bring him here;
Use your privilege as his darling.
When you go humbly to ask for him,
He will consent to your words;
If he is truly a king,
He will give you Aktulpar,
He will agree to what you say.
With golden forelock and tasselled belt,
To King Azyz's only son,
He will surely allot him.
The youngest of twelve daughters,
If you insist, he will heed your words;
If he thinks you his true child,
He will give you Aktulpar.”
Your father spoke, and I listened;
I agreed to his words.
Entering the palace,
Lined inside with silk,
I, your mother Kanykei,
Had worked its patterns with my teeth for seven unceasing years;
Between Orenburg and Margilan,
I had the intervening country searched,
The freight-carrier's cotton
I had taken by force.
The filings of hard iron,
Its filings carefully fitted,
I had them placed within;
It filled anyone who saw it with delight.
Thin iron wire pressed into it,
It wearied Kelte's bullets.
No musket ball could enter,
Arkesken's point could not pierce it,
No spear could pass through when thrust;
Its collar gold, its sleeves brass,
Double mail, with lark-eye rings,
A broad collar, a roomy vest,
The white padded coat your mother carefully made;
“Wear it when you go to battle,
Remember your sister-in-law,” I said,
And dressed your Alake in it.
When drawn from its sheath with a cry,
When turned toward the eager enemy,
It never failed to strike when swung;
Unless it had drunk its fill of blood,
It would not enter its sheath when put away.
With all his skill devoted to the work,
Your godfather Bolokbai
Had barely made it in six days.
Arkesken from the palace,
Black steel, a piercingly sharp blade,
On fierce King Almambet's
Arm I hung it.
Visiting my father's royal home,
I went to ask for Aktulpar.
Your father's treasure,
His treasury I opened.
I had eighty gelded camels saddled,
Loaded all eighty with gold coins,
Your father's wealth
I lavished on Aktulpar.
Wide Andijan and Margilan,
I traversed the country between.
Your graceful mother, then a young wife,
Before my father,
Visiting my distant birth-home,
I went to ask for Aktulpar.
Refusing to heed my words,
My father, miserly with wealth,
The gold of eighty camels
He put into his treasury.
What ruin he brought upon me!
God had utterly struck him down.
Though I sobbed, he would not heed me,
Would not give me Aktulpar;
Making me howl like a dog,
Me, your furious mother,
He sent away empty-handed and forlorn.
“She will not heed my words;
A single yearling of her father's
She will not bring from her birth-home;
She will not camp along the river;
The youngest of twelve daughters
Is worth less than one of her father's yearlings.”
Because of what had happened,
Because of Aktulpar,
Your father would not lie or stay with me;
For Aktulpar, wretched soul,
I was a neglected wife for six months.
The ten commands the saints had given,
I ceased to call it a blessing.
Hook-nosed black Sart,
Incorrigible King Temir,
Then I ceased to call him father.
