The bald black vultures will fly over him.
Crushing his sturdy bones,
Wolves will tear the gray wolf apart.
Sniffing his blood and gore and whining,
Accursed foxes will defile him.
Summoning the beasts of prey,
Croaking ravens will fly above him.”
Peerless in his swiftness,
The valiant-born Külchoro cried:
“My doomed uncle Sary Khan,
Your face has faded pale!
Death has taken the kinsman
Who carved the meat and gave me honey.
Death came from the vile infidel,
Unworthy of salt enough for a cooking pot.
May he never put forth shoots—
Death came from that wretch.
Not from some distant stranger, but from kin;
Death came from one close to us.”
Saying this, Külchoro
Grasped him at the waist and folded him.
Tuuchunak had been taken as spoil,
So he loaded him upon some other beast.
Unable to cease circling him and weeping,
Külchoro, son of Almambet,
Could find no peace.
He sobbed, unable to die,
Unable to take his own life.
The poor man had no kinsman beside him,
No forest into which he could flee,
No Nogai people loyal to him,
No companion in his grievous trial,
None of the blessed Kyrgyz there.
He paced about, restraining himself,
Leaning upon
His golden-bladed battle-axe.
Unable to find any peace,
He stood bewildered.
Then mist arose from the ravines,
And over the mountaintops
Great clouds gathered.
Winds blew from every side,
The grass trembled and stirred,
Hail swept all the foothills,
And from the sky a sixfold hailstorm
Poured down in driving sheets.
Külchoro, his father’s only son,
Stood muttering as the sky convulsed
And the earth seemed to shift.
With no Argyn or Kyrgyz people there,
The lionlike Choro
Stood helpless.
Then, when the man looked toward the qibla,
There among the horses was Bozaiman,
A creature wiser than other beasts.
Heaving deep sighs and drawing in his breath,
He broke away from the herd,
And from among the horses
The creature came galloping up.
He appeared before Külchoro,
His tail and mane streaming apart,
Flicking his ears playfully,
Pounding and scattering the earth,
Twisting his neck like a wild ass,
Neighing and pawing the ground,
Striking and battering the earth,
His tail and mane flowing.
In that sacred creature
He plainly beheld
Kambar-Ata, patron of horses.
When he saw Bozaiman,
His heart broke once more.
In the loudly neighing Bozaiman
He seemed to see his forefather Manas.
Neighing and whinnying,
The stallion lowered his head to the ground,
And circled around
Your honored Sary Khan.
