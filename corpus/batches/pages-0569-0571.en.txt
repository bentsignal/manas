He let it graze, tethered by one leg.
Pillowing his head on his white saddle,
With a leather sweatcloth and blue saddle pad
Spread beneath his hip,
From his playful childhood days,
The bulletproof garment from Altai
He drew over himself entirely.
He kept his keen steel at his side.
Without opening his closed eyes,
Without drawing back his outstretched legs,
The lion-hero, unlike other men,
Fell into a giant's sleep.
That lion among men, poor fellow,
Slept there, snoring.
When dawn began to break,
When light touched the earth,
Taking the short way along a narrow path,
Destitute, searching for grain,
Karacha came along,
Leading ninety camels.
Wretched, unable to find relief,
Leading his ninety camels,
Searching in bewilderment, finding no grain,
Pale-faced, traveling through the night,
His people destitute and starving,
His mind utterly distraught,
Riding far and wide,
Through all the world
He wandered in search of grain.
The stars thinned one by one in the sky;
Dawn's cold breeze shivered;
A chill wind blew.
Opposite the shrine of Baabedin,
Built there on the nearer side,
A sight caught the eye:
Red and white wheat piled like a mountain
Appeared in the open steppe.
When Karacha saw it,
“That will be much food for us,” he thought,
And his whole mind turned toward it.
Five sets of nine, headed by a mare,
He offered in sacrifice to Baabedin.
The people called out in a clamor
And drew near to the wheat.
That wheat had an owner:
With an ogre's overhanging brow,
The giant Manas lay beside it.
He was a being unlike other men.
Looking at his back,
They saw he had a lion's mane.
His appearance was strange, his menace fierce;
He would flatten anyone who attacked him.
If this hero wakes and rises,
Forget the wheat, Karacha:
Give up hope of your precious life!
He had heard from those who came
That the people of Andijan
Were exceptional farmers.
“Are these peaceful people or enemies?
If this well-fed wretch grows angry,
Will he cruelly gouge out our eyes?
People and camels, stay back here!
If we happen to draw near,
Will he slaughter us without mercy?
Let him not take our camels as booty;
If he should grow angry,
Let him not kill every one of us.
His bulk is equal to a mountain.
If he should roll over,
He looks ready to devour all he sees.
His appearance is unlike other men's.
Could he be a lion out on patrol?
Having thoroughly stocked his provisions,
Could he be a hero lying here, snorting?
He is a champion and a pillar of strength,
A renowned and formidable hero.
He is a man of immense dignity.
On his right, as his companion,
Growling and licking its chops,
There is a hungry lion.
If that lion attacks, it will spare nothing;
If this man rises with a roar,
He will leave none of us unharmed.
He is a precious one, a lion,
A hero worth a whole age.
His right shoulder is broad;
He is a lion who could rout the Oirats.
His eyes are an ogre's eyes.
He is the very lion of the unseen.
The hardship of hunger—
We have come to experience it.
Hungry, searching for grain,
We have come here to die.
If he rages and attacks us,
He is no fellow who will let us fare well.
This is no hero whom
An ordinary mortal can easily withstand.
Searching for crops, looking for grain,
We shall die in vain.
If he is a formidable foe,
If he is a man of evil intent,
We shall die a dreadful death!
Our dream has turned ill.
If we are slaughtered in the steppe,
Nothing like it will happen in an age.
Could he be a merciful soul?
If his fury truly seizes him,
Will he slaughter us?
Before he rises from sleep,
Before he turns his head toward us,
Before he takes up his gear,
Let us say, ‘Spare our lives, hero; here!’
The foal of a young mare,
The peerless steed among horses,
Akkula, approaching its fifth year—
Before this lion
Let us present it as a gift.
Instead of standing idly watching,
Let us act with sound judgment.
Let us plead for our lives.
If we do not do this, lads,
How can we survive?”
Karacha of the Noygut
Spoke in many different ways.
The hero Bolot, their elder,
As though slaughter had struck—
