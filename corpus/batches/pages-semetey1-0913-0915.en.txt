On the day of Thursday,
On Friday's Night of Power,
Talas was full of shouting,
Calling out, “Semetey!”
Then came the forty companions who had fled.
Along the broad Talas,
At Kanykei's Kotormo,
They crossed over Kotormo,
Passed through Suusamyr,
Swept over Kyzart,
And roamed the broad Kochkor.
Then the forty companions
Gave themselves over to merriment.
Their winged steeds that had borne the journey,
They unsaddled, leaving their backs bare,
Tethered them with their heads drawn up,
And let them cool thoroughly.
They made tethering places of stone,
And stood ready.
They spread out their food,
Made ready their little camp kettles,
The archers drew together;
Their tent with reed supporting poles
They pitched on level ground.
They slaughtered yearling fillies,
Posted a strong watch,
And lay there, the forty companions.
They made ready their spears,
Prepared their guns,
And lay there, the forty companions.
Descending from Kyzart,
When Semetey looked through his spyglass,
A blue-mottled tent appeared,
Blue smoke rose apart.
There were tumultuous cries;
When he looked at the forty companions,
Their hostility was plain.
Smoke rose against the sky,
The tent showed blue,
With much shouting and fierce cheering.
Damn it, the forty companions
Looked stubbornly defiant!
Manas's son, the warrior orphan,
Rode Buurul, urging him to surge forward.
His heart could not contain his many cares;
When he saw the forty companions,
His tears streamed in all directions.
The gray belt left by his father Manas,
He unfastened and hung around his neck.
Leading Taibuurul,
He pleaded with the forty companions:
“Since I came to Talas,
My dear forty companions,
Your number has become sixty,
My wings for circling flight.
Listen attentively to my words,
My dear forty uncles;
Do not utterly break my back.
‘He went to Talas—the forty companions,
His iron-strong support—
He scattered those companions!’
Do not let them call me Manas's worthless son.
Do not kill my words,
My dear forty uncles;
Do not make me the subject of gossip!
Do not leave me alive; slaughter me
With your own hands, forty uncles!
Bury me in a known place;
If you are angry, forty uncles,
Slaughter me, the orphan!”
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Chyibyt and Kochkor, two scoundrels,
Adybai and Kolbai, making four scoundrels,
All stood up together.
Firing guns and drawing bows,
They fought with resounding crashes.
Khan Manas's forty companions,
At the orphan who offered his head,
Drew their bows in showers of rain,
Fired their shots in showers of hail.
The forty skilled companions,
Who had thrown the whole world into confusion,
Both mighty wrestlers and lions,
Upon Semetey's head
Brought trouble there.
With their jazayyl guns all together,
All forty companions,
Their wise men and their elders,
At the orphan standing there pleading,
All rushed together.
The Ochogor with its steel muzzle—
They stood in a row and fired a thousand times.
All forty companions
He drew his bow, raining arrows,
Wide-mouthed lacquered arrows—
They snatched them up and shot a thousand.
The orphan who stood his ground,
They meant to surround and seize;
Their true intentions had turned evil:
They meant to slaughter him as a sacrifice.
If they had the power,
They would drag him onto the road
For full-grown dogs to tear apart.
Chyibyt and Kochkor, two scoundrels,
Adybai and Kolbai, making four scoundrels,
Sons of a junior wife,
Their thoughts had turned apart.
The forty companions left by Manas
Pressed fiercely, drawing their bows.
Look at the haze and sparks,
Look at the shots that strike without swerving;
Thrusting spears from every corner,
Look at the throng that swarmed upon him.
Piercing cries and fierce cheering—
Those forty companions of Manas:
A wolf's teeth could not pierce them in a fight,
The world's strength could not overcome them.
Lacquered spears with deep sockets,
With steel joined to their tips,
That felled whoever came their way—
The forty expert spearmen
Shouted and thrust their spears,
Pressing in by tens and fives,
Striking with spears from every side.
Manas's son, the warrior orphan,
Stood his ground unconcerned;
All those spears
He counted as nothing.
The orphan stood motionless.
They threw aside their spears
And struck with axes.
They stormed and clamored,
Swinging battle-axes, thrusting spears;
The fighting grew costly.
They took their swords in hand,
Without sparing themselves, at the orphan,
Shouted and struck with swords.
The wolf-maned one's son, the warrior orphan—
His breast could not contain his many cares.
The wolf-maned one's orphan son,
His tears streamed in all directions.
The dear lion, proud as a cock,
Though they inflicted such humiliation,
The dear warrior did not move.
A helmet of blue iron
Was on the wolf-maned orphan's head,
Fitted down over it;
The armor of his ribs—
Impervious to missiles, upon the orphan's
Shoulder blades it lay:
His mother Kanykei
Had fitted that iron securely.
The Akolpok left by Manas—
Forged its metal from hard steel,
It wearied the bullets of Kelte;
One skilled artisan after another fashioned it,
And submitted to the wise for testing.
Gold and silk intermingled,
I shot sixty warriors.
In difficult, grievous straits,
The Akolpok—not merely against forty companions—
In battle against the Chinese emperor,
When he brought terrible hell upon them,
When he fired amid tumult,
No striking missile passed through,
No steel shot passed through.
From his wolf-maned father Manas,
The Akolpok remained as a gift.
Trouble-laden and unfortunate,
Your tiger mother Kanykei,
Of the companions left by Manas,
Foretold that they would become enemies.

“Suddenly my orphan
May meet a needless death,” she said.
The noble woman Kanykei
Had clothed him in the Akolpok,
And at his throat its fastenings
Had tied across one another.
At the sight, the heart was stirred;
The wolf-maned lord Manas—
He looked just like him.
Having assailed the Khizr-blessed lion,
The forty companions had no good fortune;
There was no hole in the Akolpok.
What is seen today is gone tomorrow;
His own kin had become enemies:
Such is this shitty world!
His insides burned, his liver was an ember.
“A wandering orphan,” they called him;
No kinsman understood his secret grief.
“I went on calling you exceptional kin;
Only a spoonful of my blood remains,
My flesh is left blue and lean.
I rode Buurul, calling him a swift steed;
Damn this empty world!
I turned to you as my only kin.
Either my sorrow does not reach God,
Or I cannot turn to my kin.
That my saddle straps had grown stiff,
My horse knew as it staggered;
That my dear soul had burned away,
Strangers knew, my own kin did not.
Now, to streams in valleys and broad waters,
I have no livestock to drive upstream.
My own kin have become my enemies;
Now I have no life to spare from death.
Hilly Ken-Kol, broad Talas—
I have no livestock to drive there.
My own kin fired upon me;
Now I have no life to spare from death.
Since I came to Talas
To see you, my kin,
Did I hijack your field?
You have utterly exhausted my strength.
Damn you, worthless men,
Did I carry off your treasure?!
Foolish kin, may you never prosper!
Even when I offered my mother, you refused;
Saying, ‘I'll yawn, then slaughter him,’
You did not give up your wickedness.
You have now exhausted my life;
Though I took on your burdens,
I offered my head as a gift.
