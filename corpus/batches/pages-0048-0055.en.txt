Those who answered back,
They dragged back and did not spare,
But slaughtered them then.
The teeming Chinese, the kakan's people,
Were sated now;
They had crushed all the Kyrgyz
And taken them as spoils.
So much gold and silver they took,
They could not load it all.
Noble young wives and beautiful girls
They took in numbers as booty,
Unable to gather them all away.
Do not ask what the Chinese did:
Taking the red-tailed single-humped camels,
Red ingots, diamond stones,
Taking red gold and ingots,
The hero Döngö of the Sibe,
Girded with a sword without a sheath,
Like a cloud about to rain again,
His fury gathering on his face,
His heroism extraordinary,
His strength beyond measure,
A smooth spear with a thickened iron socket
He held jutting high above him.
He brought torment on the world;
When an enemy rose to strike,
He never opened his guard in fear.
When they came to exchange blows,
He stamped them to the ground like hares.
No one who faced him survived,
No mortal dared approach him head-on.
Gathering fury like a gray wild boar,
To Molto and Alöökö,
To those two, he spoke:
“Damn your fathers' graves, you two khans!
Listen to my words now.
I am a great sorcerer;
Understand what I tell you!
Upon these Burut standing here,
This people called Burut,
Let us unleash slaughter.
Not only the Burut men,
Let us slaughter their women too.
Their very tongue is Burut;
These fucking Burut
Are our enemies in their essence.
This is the Burut omen:
Three or four can be seen,
The girth straps fastened to their horses.
Dwelling in the Zanggel mountains,
Whom no mortal has faced and overcome,
Whose paths no scout has reconnoitered,
Whom no one has brought low,
They are a people weighed down by hardship.
If by chance we leave their old folk alive,
If we do not kill every one,
In the kakan's Beijing,
Let them never pitch their village,
Learn the secrets of that people,
Survey the terrain of this land,
And grow fat and regain their vigor.
Let the fierce Kyrgyz never run wild,
Move into your land,
Settle villages among your people,
First drawing out songs,
Then drawing out secrets,
And grow fat and regain their vigor.
Then let them never against us,
Generation after generation,
Rise and wage war.
Upon Beijing of Chin-Machin,
Upon the people of the true kakan,
Let your Burut, flaring into flame,
Never take their revenge in earnest!”
Döngö stood and said this.
At that time Alöökö,
Thick of forearm, stone of heart,
A brute with an elephant's build, said:
“Damn your father's grave, hero Döngö!
You are a dog, Döngö.
My name is the champion Alöökö;
When my wrath takes hold,
I have a rhinoceros for my mount.
Never mind your Burut! I myself,
The eighteen thousand worlds,
I have the strength to grind them to meal!
Look at me now:
My tigress has borne fifteen cubs;
At this very time
No person dares come near me.
I have sixty dragons;
Beyond them
I have sword-tusked elephants.
You whimpering Döngö,
Understand this strength of mine!
Dust rises from my passage;
Souls leave bodies at my voice.
As long as this head is on me,
No one can cast an evil eye on me;
No mortal can bring me low.
I have my khan in the kakan's realm,
I have cities in many places,
And dwelling in those cities,
I have people in many places.
Over the people of this world
I have the strength to prevail.
I have soldiers, I have an army,
I have a waist to gird with resolve,
I have a place where I stand secure,
I have a cap with coral-beaded plumes.
Not merely your Burut, but the whole world
I have the power to command.
We need land to sustain life;
Khans like us
Need people to be their subjects.
These few Burut,
If we bring them into submission,
This people called Burut,
How can we unleash our wrath,
Their women and their children,
For what reason slaughter them?”
Alöökö spoke in various ways,
And went on to say:
“They have no strength to fight with us,
Nor do the Burut have a khan to devise a plan.
Their women and their children,
If we slaughter every one,
That would be madness on your part.
Of these helpless Burut,
You stand fearing what will follow.
Stand firmly in the foremost place!
Turn your head toward your superior;
Döngö, this would disgrace you!
The common folk who fall into your hands,
How could you have the face to slaughter them?”
As Alöökö closed his mouth,
As he was saying this,
Bawling like a young goose,
That hero of the Chinese,
The same Molto, clattered out his words,
With coral beads, plumes and coral stones
Glittering upon his head:
“Damn your father's grave, hero Döngö,
Do not keep talking like that,” he said.
“These Burut standing here,
This people you call Burut,
Are of one seed like white wheat,
Of one lineage, sharing one battle cry.
Instead, this people,
Let us extinguish their fires,
Move every one of them away.
Our twisted copper spears,
Grasping them at the end and swinging them round,
Let us send them to Eren-Kabyrga.
Drive one to the Russians;
Do not give even three households
Of Burut a place to settle.
Drive one of them to Iyren;
Cast them down into the depths.
Drive one of them to the Kazakhs;
Plunge the Burut
Into torment from which there is no return.
Move one to the broad ridge of Orkon;
Deliver them
Into Torgut hands in Orkon.
Move one of them to Kanggay;
Moving them to Kanggay's ridge,
Press them into an allotted share for those below,
Into the hands of the Manchu and Sibe.
Send one to Altay,
Where no human would dream of going;
Send them among the Black Kalmyks.
Let us see how brave they are!
Without killing or destroying them,
These fucking Burut,
Let us measure out and divide with the whip!”
Once their khan had given orders,
Would the mass of people stand and watch?
Would they make the khan repeat his word?
The honored Kyrgyz, the dear people,
Karakan's eight sons,
Their throng was scattered to dust;
Without delay they bound them, they say.
Their throng was scattered to dust,
Hell was brought upon their heads, they say.
Karakan's eight sons:
The eldest were Bay and Üsön.
They bound Üsön's hands
And drove him toward Orkon.
They bound Bay's hands
And drove him down toward Jelpinish.
They bound Ulak Khan's hands
And drove him toward Iyren;
Their leaders, Baltay and Jakyp,
They drove toward Altay.
Among the few Kyrgyz,
No one able to fight remained.
What was dug became a deep pit,
What boiled became bitter misery.
Souls fled at the sound of his voice;
Alöökö became a ruling khan.
At Margalang, marvelous things
Alöökö had built.
From Alty-Shaar and Kokon,
Lashing the Sarts month by month,
He took tribute from them.
That ruler named Alöökö,
God had cursed him beyond measure;
Month by month, whipping the Sarts,
Six thousand jamby ingots, a thousand otter pelts,
He kept taking as tribute.
Unable to find that tribute,
The people thrashed about in distress.
Molto Khan arose among the Chinese;
Dust rose from his passage,
Souls fled at the sound of his voice,
And the great people's numbers dwindled away.
Let us leave that aside;
Of the heroes taken in bonds,
The poor ones driven into exile,
Let us begin to tell.
The leaders, Baltay and Jakyp,
Had both their hands in bonds,
Both their eyes still shining.
Six thousand Chinese, a san of kalday,
Surrounded them and drove them on.
Six oxen and four mules
They gave for hauling as they drove them;
From six goats and three cows
They gave them milk as they drove them.
They were forty households of Kyrgyz;
Not a shred of strength remained in them.
Their flesh wasted away to nothing,
Only a spoonful of blood remained.
Driving them conspicuously across the earth,
They allowed them no place to stay;
For two days in one spot
They would not let them lie down, but drove them on.
There was no bank where they could hide,
No food for them to eat properly.
The children cried, unable to walk,
And grew thinner as they went.
Driven on, unable to find a way out,
Their hands tied behind their backs,
They went on in misery.
Without rest by day,
Thus they were driven onward now.
Without sleep at night,
Not resting through the days,
Not granted a day's respite,
Not resting through the months,
Not granted a month's respite,
Until the tender kuray stalks ripened,
Until the autumn gossamer floated,
They were driven for six full months.
They crossed many a looming mountain,
Forded many a surging river,
Passed over rough and hilly ridges,
Tramped across the mist-veiled desert,
Descended from Echki-Ölbös,
And Kordoy and Mangkan
They crossed together now.
Üch-Aral at the head of the Ile,
They had no choice but to ford it now.
Ögüz-Ashuu, Tay-Ashuu,
Kichi-Jyldyz, Chong-Jyldyz,
They traversed them all now.
Beyond lay Ak-Talaa;
They reached Ak-Talaa now.
Ah, dear Ak-Talaa,
No land their forefathers had trodden!
Its people were Torgut, many Kalmyks,
A people seven forefathers had never seen.
They reached Ak-Talaa and camped;
That day they stayed the night in Ak-Talaa.
When the dawn broke clear,
When light reached and touched the earth,
When the true dawn broke,
When the dawn swelled forth,
Stars trembled overhead,
One by one turning toward the qibla;
Stars parted from the sky,
Dimmed and veiled in haze.
The glow of dawn appeared,
Dawn's cold wind whistled,
The reed tops rustled sharply,
Young larks twittered,
The surface of its sand was scored,
The tops of its willows bent;
Thus lay Ak-Talaa.
It was a land of rolling ridges,
An exceedingly beautiful place.
It had pools and ponds and marshes,
Ducks and geese in separate flocks.
Its apples ripened and fell to mulch,
Its ripe walnuts filled the ravines,
Its poppies swayed,
Its sorrel and wild rhubarb
All ripened and swayed luxuriantly:
Thus lay Ak-Talaa.
In its midst were gushing spring waters;
Its wormwood glistened,
Its season was full of delight.
Its river valley lay along the water;
No human foot had come to this place,
Its lands lay open and free.
Its shoots were like plane trees,
Its plane trees like towers;
If you looked at its little birds,
They were like snowcocks crying in the mountains.
Its lizards were like snakes;
If you looked at its snakes,
They were like ropes nine armspans long.
Almighty God Most High
Had created living things of every kind;
Such was the land of Ak-Talaa.
Its plain lay open and free.
There was a creature called börsö;
From its belly, wobbling out,
Its young would emerge.
Animals of every kind,
Every creature no human had seen was there.
Wild human creatures and wild camels
Were there upon its hills.
It was a land lying open and free;
Its far end, they called Altay.
Inhabiting that Altay,
The Black Kalmyk and Manchu peoples
Were a teeming multitude.
With Baltay and Jakyp at their head,
Their hands bound in hardship,
Forty households out of the Kyrgyz
Were driven away to Altay.
The wretched ones driven there
Found the excellent land of Altay,
Found the Black Kalmyk people.
They wandered away from the Kyrgyz and were lost;
All the many mourned,
Parted from their people in suffering.
They were called Black Kalmyks;
They were a worthy, admirable people,
Dwelling in broad Altay,
Before their doorways
Tethering barren mares by the thousand.
Beyond were Torgut and many Kalmyks;
They had horses grown old without a bullet striking them,
Horses frisking freely.
Vast herds of all four kinds of livestock
Your mighty Kalmyks kept.
The forty households driven there,
With Jakyp and Akbaltay at their head,
Forty Kyrgyz households, the wretched people,
Hired themselves as milkers and drank milk;
Thus they sustained their lives now.
They grazed horses and rode working mounts;
That way they sustained their lives now.
From the Black Kalmyks and Manchus,
Eating congealed goat fat,
Finding a way to eat a young barren filly,
Thus they sustained their lives now.
Since their arrival among these Kalmyks,
Twelve months had now passed.
Among the forty Kyrgyz households,
The wise man Akbaltay,
The gray bull brought from the Kyrgyz—
They slaughtered that gray bull;
The many sorrows filling their hearts,
They banished those many sorrows.
