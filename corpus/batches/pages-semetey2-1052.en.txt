Many Kazakhs dismounted:
“Keep the common people in your thoughts;
Seize and slay the troublemaker!
Is our blood the blood of swine?
Spare those who remain!
He was the one who began the quarrel;
What have all the people done to you?
Ah, hero, we beg your mercy!
You were a lion of mighty presence;
We submit ourselves to you!”
So spoke the teeming host,
And the people broke into lamentation.
Külchoro wisely perceived
How the multitude stood before him;
Whether they spoke or not, could it be denied?
Seeing the dense crowd clamoring,
Külchoro accepted their plea.
Without touching the common people,
The startled lion-warrior
Rode farther on.
Clad all in blue steel,
He reached Khan Ümötöy.
“From atop the bay horse,
At the face of his right shoulder,
By the edge of his spine,
There lies the measured spot.
If I guide my thrust without driving it through,
That trailing-robed Kazakh slave
Will be hurled from the saddle.
I shall stab close beside
The maddened fool
And take him bound for judgment
Before my lord and sworn brother.
Without killing or destroying him,
Without bringing ruin on my own head,
I shall take him bound to the council.
I shall bring this incurable great quarrel
Before the public assembly;
I shall bring this deafening uproar
Before the council.
I shall bind to public renown
The honor for which a hero dies;
I shall bring this merciless great mischief
To trial for its wrongdoing.
If Semetey approves Ümötöy’s words,
Let him strike and hew me limb from limb;
Let me cease to live
And renounce my uncle Semetey.
Though Ümötöy has fought me,
He is still my blood cousin;
I shall bind my kinsman
And take him to my uncle.
I shall tell my uncle,
‘Your friend has become your enemy.’
If the people see Ümötöy
And suddenly take fright,
Or unexpectedly call him an outsider,
Why should I treat him as kin?
If we meet, I shall cut off his head!
If he will not obey at all,
I shall renounce my uncle the khan!”
So saying, Külchoro—
A broad-shouldered master of the spear,
A deft man surpassing the world,
Who never withdrew once battle began,
Who never turned from a foe he engaged,
A grasping leopard of a man,
A sage with the wisdom of a saint—
Wore trousers of yellow hide
And rode the handsome gray horse.
Though countless hosts came against him,
He charged without thinking he might die,
A lion-warrior born to command,
A surpassing sultan among heroes.
At the face of Ümötöy’s belt,
At the edge of his bulletproof coat,
At the eye of his great mail,
