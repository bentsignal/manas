Go and settle in Talas.
He is the hero who will welcome you,
The lion who will ease your anxiety.
He greased his blue steel.
The one who sorely provoked him—
Khan Kobosh—he slew.
When they stirred his stubborn anger,
He destroyed his own father.
When they provoked his wrath,
He destroyed the forty companions.
He devoured Khan Zhakyp.
No mortal remained
Who could withstand the boy.
The reason for our coming to you—
Against the unbeliever like a venomous spider,
The hero Khan Kongurbai,
To wage a holy war in earnest,
Placing the standard in your hands,
The countless Muslim troops
To have you alone lead,
To gird our waists firmly,
To take you as our aid,
To face Kongurbai in Beijing
And pursue the blood-price,
To inflict grievous torment,
My champion has sent us to you,
Over the Argyn and Kyrgyz
To raise you as their lord.
With a rosy countenance, a radiant face,
A glowing red ember was he.
In this fleeting world
There was none like Kulchoro.
The fulfillment of Semetey's wish,
Girded with a silver belt,
His sunlike face shining with light,
This smiling boy
Was Almambet's son.
Of middling height, broad-shouldered,
At their feast ninety beasts had been slaughtered;
He had been named Kanchoro.
He was dear Chubak's son.
You were their honored kinsman;
My Kozho, know their worth.
We raised peerless steeds, calling them noble horses;
Unable to stay away, we came, calling you kin.
I have spoken at length—heed my words.
My gray-maned Kozho, understand.
You were my exceptional Kozho;
I was Bakai, bringer of good fortune.”
When he spoke in this way,
Kozho began to rage:
“Valiant uncle Bakai!
I will not accept these words of yours;
I will not go to Talas.
His bloodthirsty father Manas—
This hand deceived him six times.
Talas amid the mountains—
How could I go and make it my homeland?
To Temir Khan's city
He wandered and grew up,
That orphan rotten at the root—
How could I go and make him a hero!
Do not grieve, Bakai; hear my words.
In hilly Ken-Kol,
How could I pitch my village?
Manas's foolish son Semetey—
Taking that one as my support,
How could I go there and prosper?
Set your words right.
Uncle Bakai, before I topple you yourself!
Do not turn your eyes askance;
Do not twist your words!
Is your Talas such a superior land?
Is Semetey a greater hero than I?
To Manas's foolish orphan son
I will not pitch my village as a subject!”
While Kozho was raging,
Almambet's Kulchoro,
Who had not yet charged shouting into battle,
Between an enemy and a friend
Had not yet learned to distinguish.
Kulchoro slipped away,
And caused mischief there.
Chynkozho's Black Horse—
He cut a notch then,
And put Ogoy's earmark upon it.
Pulling free of its tether,
The sly one's Black Horse
Hurried away with nimble steps.
That accursed one's Black Horse
Fled toward Kebez-Too.
The mount beneath him
Was Abyke's Aktelki.
The mighty orphan Kulchoro,
Lashing his whip to right and left,
Spurring this way and that,
Pursued the Black Horse.
Chasing and chasing, unable to catch it,
The companion came onto the main road.
Keeping beyond his reach as he pursued,
The Black Horse gained a ridge.
He let the Black Horse escape.
Cutting its ear, Kulchoro
Had left his mark upon the Black Horse.
While they were raging,
Kulchoro came back.
Arguing and pressing his case,
Chynkozho was carrying on.
Roaring like a raging stream,
Raging like a hungry camel bull,
Bellowing like a bull,
Summoning his old fury,
He rumbled and stormed,
Speaking against Khan Bakai.
He had all things in abundance;
His right shoulder was broad.
Were it not for his raging madness,
He was a hero who could shatter the Oirat.
He was a surging lion.
However mountainlike he was,
In wisdom he was lacking.
If that Kozho had been resourceful,
All the world
He could have made submit.
Your dear uncle Kozho,
When he raged like a lion,
Had a black fur cap like a cauldron.
If Chynkozho were wise
And joined with Semetey,
All the Chinese and their khagan
He had the power to put to trial.
All his twelve bodily members were complete;
He was born an imposing hero.
If you looked upon Chynkozho,
Kozho was capable in all he did.
Tall and broad-shouldered,
More imposing than other men, Kozho—
Which of his qualities shall we tell?
Both a hero and a lion,
If you looked upon Chynkozho,
He was an exceptional man of distinctive splendor.
Clad in a blue flower-patterned coat,
If you looked upon Chynkozho,
He raged like a gray tiger,
All his anger rose,
His scalp drew tight by a span,
And he quarreled with Bakai.
Your uncle Bakai, an exceptional lion,
The dear hero could not gather himself.
With his beard grown white,
While away upon his journey,
He had no means to contend
In his old age.
Caps of gray lynx and marten—
Who had not worn them swaying?
Not Kozho alone: before Bakai
Even Beijing's khan had trembled.
Your leopard uncle Bakai's
Heart burned fiercely with grief.
Wishing to die, he could not die;
He could not take his own life.
God would not take Bakai.
Stern Bakai, because of Kozho—
If he would die, life was too sweet;
If he would sink into the earth, the ground was hard.
Before even that much time had passed,
Before a word could be spoken,
In the blink of an eye,
His father, lion and goshawk—
Kulchoro was born surpassing him.
While Kozho stood raging,
While he committed this outrage,
Felt fury spread throughout his body.
Ormonbek and Kanchoro—
He looked at those two.
Unable to withstand his wrath,
Ormonbek and Kanchoro
Their hearts leaped within them;
The weapon grips slipped from their hands.
Shygai's son Chynkozho
Seemed about to seize them both,
And lay them across his knee.
Unable to rise, they trembled,
And scraped the earth, quaking.
At the sight of those two,
The adept Chynkozho
Burst into renewed fury,
Changing his expression in many ways,
Worked himself into a rage,
And muttered to himself.
While Kozho carried on thus,
His father, Lord Alma—
Kulchoro was born surpassing him.
Did not look about him.
While Kozho stood spurring himself on,
He spared his life not at all.
Without glancing all around,
While Kozho stood shouting,
Not sparing his life in the least, he said:
“I greeted you before your face,
Yet you shout without cause.
Now I shall call you simply Kozho!
You bellow, Kozho,
As though a demon had struck your wife.
You have flown into a madness,
As though your quarry had just escaped.
Do not rage without cause, Kozho.
You have no Oigut people in Sary-Kol.
As though stricken with the yellow sickness,
Do not contend without cause!
You are getting carried away, Kozho.
These people whom you brought to their knees,
To Manas, who went to the world beyond,
You handed over as subjects.
Do not rumble, do not rage.
Do not thunder and mutter, Kozho.
If you mean trouble, Kozho,
Name the support on which you rely;
Name the place where you will flee and hide.
Name the pit we shall dig for you.
Give your answer without evasion!
If you would fight, Kozho,
Name the army you have made ready!
Zulfiqar, left by Manas,
I have come to grease.
With my chilling might, Chynkozho,
I have come to lay you low.
If you call me a blood-drinker, I drink blood;
I am that bloodthirsty lion himself.
Of your leopard Semetey
I am the dark eye that keeps watch.
If you call me a hero, I am a hero.
Of blustering men like you,
If my precious life does not perish,
I am worth eighty!
Agree to what I say.
The tribute you paid my father Manas,
Counting twelve years,
Pay it in full today!
If you will not accept these words,
If you will not give that tribute,
I shall trample your very crown!
I shall scatter your four kinds of livestock!
I shall bind your champions.
If you keep blustering like this,
I shall offer you as a sacrifice!
If your words go crooked,
If you keep shouting,
I shall destroy you yourself.
You have sorely provoked my wrath.
Watch, Chynkozho:
An orphan who has grown to strength,
I have now come into my own!”
So Kulchoro spoke.
“Let the slave go!”
Kozho charged forward.
Shygai's son Kozho,
Struggling like a woman whose husband had died,
Bakai seized and held.
Almambet's son Choro,
Struggling like a lion,
Ormonbek seized and held.
Raging and wrenching about,
Struggling without restraint,
While Kozho thrashed in his frenzy,
The immeasurably brave Kulchoro
His dark-cheeked whip—
He seized and swung it round.
Making Kozho's head ring,
He struck him upon the crown.
Your Kozho, who thought himself a lord,
When the whip struck his crown,
Sat down, retching.
The hero Kozho, who thought himself great,
Having taken a whipping from a child,
Grew cold and stared blankly.
For as long as it takes meat to cook,
The hero Kozho lost consciousness.
Before Kozho regained his senses,
Kulchoro mounted.
His father was a gray-maned hero;
He moved without haste.
Abyke's Aktelki—
Raiding and shattering his settlement,
He attacked the hero Kozho.
Kulchoro grew wrathful.
What the champion carried upon his back
Was his appointed ammunition.
Valiant, gray-maned Kulchoro—
The horse he rode beneath him
Was Abyke's peerless steed.
What he held in his hand
Was Almambet's Oikuma;
What he slung upon his broad shoulder
Was Almambet's Syrbaran.
Choro broke away in a frenzy,
And turned toward Chynkozho,
As though to bring him utterly to his end.
If they let him go, Kozho
He seemed ready to destroy completely.
When the boy came near,
Bakai caught and held him.
Keeping him from reaching the hero Kozho,
Though he struggled like a bereaved wife,
He intervened and restrained him:
“My darling Kulchoro!
After six months of riding, untiring—
Is such a noble steed not needed?
To share counsel and confidences,
Is a kinsman not needed?
Shygai's son Chynkozho,
Kozho is capable in all he does.
Be patient, my Choro, be patient.
Can a man who fights prosper?
Can those who battle fare well?
Every stain will cling to you.
My gray-maned Choro, hear my words:
Where else will elder and younger brothers be found?”
Traveling toward Talas,
Around the spur of Kebez-Too,
Past its jutting headland,
On the rise covered with fescue,
With smoke spreading in the sky,
Yellow-mottled like starlings,
Chynkozho's great horse herd
Appeared along the road.
Kulchoro, born a gray-maned hero,
Felt his heart stirred.
Kulchoro and Kanchoro,
Complete in every way,
Truly took after their fathers.
Except for the weight of his sacred might,
They were greater heroes than Semetey.
Turning toward the great horse herd,
Raising shrill cries and clamor,
Beating the war drum,
With the painted musket and blue musket
The two fired in turn.
Their thunder deafened the ears.
Chynkozho's horses
All stampeded with a roar.
Through the center Ormonbek
Carved a way, driving them in a scattering rush.
From the right the companions
Raised “Manas!” as their battle cry.
The weak, stunted foals and yearlings,
Beyond broad Kashgar,
They slaughtered on the bare ground.
Making the mares toss their heads,
The slow-moving yearlings and foals
They slaughtered upon the steppe.
The sleek mares
They drove away without their young.
Shygai's son Kozho's
Horses they took away,
And drove sleep from his eyes.
Then Shygai's son Chynkozho—
What could dear Kozho do?
Wishing to die, he could not die;
Kozho could not take his own life.
When he came out in fury,
The tethered Black Horse—
The raging slave Kulchoro
Had cut its ear,
And put Ogoy's notched earmark upon it.
He came to the pasture: there were no horses.
“He has brought utter ruin now;
He has raided them all.
He has inflicted terrible suffering.
The Chinese killed his father,
And he has taken revenge upon me.
My mares like white gulls
He has let Khan Bakai raid.
The Black Horse no lasso could touch
He has let slaves ride bareback.
I have suffered such humiliation!
I shall bring utter ruin upon him.
The Zhediger's hero Toltoy—
I shall go and join him.
His bloodthirsty father Manas—
This hand of mine deceived him six times.
Fighting and shooting in earnest,
I shall bring utter ruin upon him.
The Zhediger's Toltoy,
From the countless troops that bend the earth,
I shall go and gather to my side.
To Temir Khan's city
The orphan wandered away—
I shall sweep across all the Afghan land,
And seize his wife.
Until the swaying stalks ripen,
Until the autumn gossamer flies,
Fighting firmly for six months,
Thus I shall bring him to his end.
That fool, the son of Manas—
Akun Khan was his father-in-law;
Aichurok was his wife.
I shall sweep into the Afghan land,
And bring a day of misery.
The bloodthirsty hero's orphan son—
I shall seize his wife.
To Toltoy of the Zhediger
I shall go and pitch my camp.
Fighting for seven years without pause,
I shall slaughter Semetey!
Bagysh's son Toltoy
I shall fasten at my waist as support.
The orphan who thinks himself a champion
I shall slaughter with his slaves.
Shattering his stone fortress,
I shall raid it utterly.
I shall bring devastation,
Shatter his great multitude,
And sweep down in a raid.
Pitching my camp beside Toltoy,
Stirring the stars in heaven,
Becoming friends unto the next world,
Tying our horses' tails for battle,
Driving our foes this way and that,
Until the blazing heat retreats,
Until the people move to lower lands,
I shall fight without tiring.
From Manas's fugitive son
Thus I shall take revenge!
I have heard news from those who came.
When he returned this way,
When he moved from Bukhara toward us,
She was the hero Toltoy's wife:
Shah Temir's Chachykei,
I heard Semetey took her.
Would God strike Toltoy down?
If I stirred him into revolt,
Unless Toltoy were utterly wretched,
Would he refrain from striking back!”
Kozho moved his camp in fury,
From his land at Kebez-Too.
An extraordinary strongman, a mighty hero,
Chynkozho broke away
From the familiar Oigut people.
What did dear Kozho do?
He castrated his rams and made them wethers.
He made all his sheep large and fat.
He castrated his stallions and made them geldings.
Kozho swiftly brought suffering.
He castrated his bulls,
And made them oxen, as you know.
All the subjects in his hands
He made submit, leaving none out.
He castrated his camel bulls and made them gelded camels.
He left his warriors on foot.
He sold his possessions and turned them into sable pelts.
Taking offense at his kinsman,
Dear Kozho raged,
And did things no human would do.
At Kebez-Too were his subjects;
He had so many people in his hands,
And all his strongmen were there.
Kozho too was no ordinary hero;
He had mighty men of many kinds.
Driving them all, Chynkozho said:
“If Toltoy of the Zhediger
Will not heed me when I arrive,
Watch, you great multitude:
I shall flood his gardens,
Throw all he has into turmoil,
And raid it all!
Think, you great multitude!
His Talas is no superior land;
Manas's fugitive is no greater hero than I.
Shygai's son Chynkozho
Is inferior to none in the world.
If I pitch my camp beside Toltoy
And do not gather his army,
If I do not inflict grievous humiliation,
Better that I had never been born;
May I die instead of living!”
Kozho shouted these words,
Spoke of unheard-of horrors,
And tormented the peaceful people.
The poor people in his hands—
He extinguished their burning hearths,
Made every one of them migrate,
Drove their beasts into a frenzy,
And made their girls and women weep.
Chynkozho moved his camp along.
Oppressing them, Chynkozho
All the great multitude
Drove before him, towering over them.
He inflicted humiliation upon those people.
Driving them all under guard,
He migrated for months and years.
To Toltoy of the Zhediger—
What did dear Kozho do?
He reached him at last.
Your wrathful Kozho
Had a black fur cap like a cauldron.
The eighteen thousand worlds
He had the power to suck dry at once.
Your elder brother, Lord Kozho—
Look at these deeds of his.
To the Zhediger people,
A mighty man came, a pillar came.
Of an appearance unlike other mortals,
The renowned lion Kozho came.
Upon the hero Toltoy's head
A standard as great as Mount Opol came.
Upon the people's heads
An unending tumult came.
The great mass of common people
Spoke these words in sorrow,
Their white-bearded elders,
All the Zhediger:
“He has come as a subject.
The gray-maned hero Kozho
We have seen with our own eyes,
That unbeliever full of devils.
The hero Toltoy was mild;
Kozho will take power,
And afflict us with incurable sorrow.
Chynkozho has pitched his camp.
If you live, you will see:
He will devise many reckonings,
And shake much dust out of us.
We were a thoroughly peaceful people.
That accursed Chynkozho,
Bullying and driving us onward,
Will slaughter us to the last.
May he sink into the earth! Will he not
Bear the guilt for these people?
Have you seen Kozho?
His words are jagged and rough.
When hardship fell upon his thimble-sized head,
It seems he came here in this state.
He speaks with great boasting;
Having grappled with some hero,
It seems he arrived worn out.
Our khan Toltoy, the leopard,
Will heed this Kozho's words,
And subject these people to oppression,
Slaughtering them to the last.
The stooping old and the tender young—
He will bear the guilt for them!”
The people spoke thus and fell silent,
Cursing one another.
Chynkozho came to Toltoy,
And became a renowned khan,
And became an influential man.
Than the hero Toltoy himself,
He became a mightier khan.
Kozho and the hero Toltoy,
Tolok and Tolobai,
Kalcha and Kadyrbai—
Of the Zhediger people,
These six were the foremost,
Their shamans who struck with lungs,
Their noblemen who raised them up,
Their heroes among spearmen,
Their champions who swung battle-axes.
These six joined together.
Like flour of white wheat,
They were kneaded into one.
Like hairs of a white saiga,
They swore an oath unto death,
And made their promise binding:
To pursue claims together,
To fight enemies together,
To wage battle together,
And if their appointed time came, their days ended,
To die together in one place.
Calling upon God,
They made their promise binding,
And swore an oath upon their lives,
Kissing the mouths of guns,
Licking the edges of swords.
Nine thousand horses upon the mountains,
Of the Zhediger's Toltoy,
Which sorrow shall you tell?
He had ninety fat mares brought,
Turned toward the qibla,
And had them offered as horse sacrifices.
“Those who turn back and flee the enemy,
May they die in this fashion,” he said,
And dipped his hand in blood.
Chynkozho arrived and settled
Beside Toltoy of the Zhediger.
At Zheti-Ozon, the Zhediger
He gathered completely.
Then Chynkozho,
Of what he had seen and known,
Told a lengthy tale.
Their sheep shared one fold;
Their food they shared as comrades.
His heart was at ease, like a flower.
Winter passed and summer came.
As the people were about to move uphill,
When summer had come,
Shygai's son Chynkozho,
Kozho, capable in all he did,
Roared in anger,
And summoned the great mass of Zhediger,
Leaving not one uncalled.
“An order has come from the khan,” they said.
All the Zhediger
Came there then.
Tolok and Toltoy were their elders.
Without turning to look at Toltoy,
As though struck by a massacre,
They all crumbled before him.
“Zhediger people of every clan,
I shall give you all courage.
If you turn back and flee,
Then you have eaten shit.
When I first arrived,
Swaying about in pairs,
What was it you said!
Come, your elders with your chiefs at their head.
All you people of mine, come!
Let me tell you much:
We were the Argyn and Kyrgyz people.
Born among those folk,
A khan named Manas arose.
The enemies who heard of him
Had the life driven from their breasts.
He was born and grew up in Altai;
The foes who grappled with him were brought low.
Upon Boshogo, the dark Kalmyk,
He brought devastation.
He brought devastation once,
Destroyed Kangai and took its wealth.
The Chinese people's great khan,
Alooke, he sent into the earth,
And showed himself a lion.
Clashing with the unbelievers,
Fighting with the unbelievers,
That Manas died.
The countless riches Manas left,
Abyke, Kobosh—the six villains—
Those men lived upon them.
His fierce mother Kanykei,
With Semetey left an orphan,
Her mind distressed by humiliation,
Fled away with her child.
He went to Bukhara and grew up,
Returned to Talas,
And killed his own kinsmen.
That boy turned out a reckless desperado.
When he moved toward Talas,
Temir Khan flattered him,
