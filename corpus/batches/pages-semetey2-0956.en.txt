May he not contrive to cut off your head.
May he not sweep away your quiver;
If your Kyrgyz learns your secrets,
May he not overturn your baggage.
May his foal not eat from your tether;
If that madman begins to prance,
May he not threaten to slaughter your village!
May the sly Burut not surround and deceive you
And choose your stallion for himself;
May his Burut not drive everything away
And pursue us all the way to the Khakan’s realm.
May he not shoot down the stronghold’s defenders
And call it vengeance for his father.
May he not spear and slaughter the mighty,
Boasting until he is sated with blood.
May he not crush and rout your army
And somehow settle his account.
May he not start a senseless war,
Drive off your people, and slaughter them.
May your Algara, which you would never surrender if asked,
Never feel this Chinese people’s grim lasso!
There are six thousand Chinese san-kaldai;
If these Chinese grow angry,
May they not take your head with their swords.
His eyes are red as burning embers,
His cartridge pouch is filled with shots.
Think carefully, Kongurbay,
About this restless boy of yours:
My prince, he is no kin of yours at all.”
Thus the seer Ayar
Spoke in anguish.
The Kalcha stiffened in fury,
As though struck by God.
“I will make a slaughter now!” he cried,
Drawing his sword,
And summoned his forty princes.
At the cunning seer who had spoken,
The diviner esteemed by the people,
Who kept babbling on,
He bristled with rage,
Seized by violent fury.
The hair atop his head stood on end,
And the prince’s hair bristled at his nape;
Like a fierce black eagle,
He hunched in wrath.
His fury began terribly:
He slaughtered and cast down
Not the seers, but the khans.
“When my brother, lost among the Burut,
Had turned back and newly returned,
Had only just fallen into our hands,
Declaring that Burkan was God,
When he had only just arrived,
You raged while worshipping stones,
And stood there howling;
You sought to turn my brother
Into an enemy and corrupt him.
In the Kalcha’s old age,
It seems you wished to see my ruin;
You wished to separate
Younger brother from elder.
He became khan over the whole world,
And it seems you wished to behold
The white-bearded Manchu.
It seems you wanted, if you could,
To foster wicked arts;
You have roused, my seer,
The fury I have borne since ancient days.
You stand ready to make an enemy of my brother
And destroy him;
In my old age you stand ready
To make war upon my faith—
Upon my newly returned brother.
Damn you, seers!
From time immemorial
You have never ceased your trickery;
You speak evil words and never prosper.
