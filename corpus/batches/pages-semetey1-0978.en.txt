It was a wondrous slave-woman—
Tell my wretched mother, the fierce one!
Tell Sarykan of the bowl-shaped eyes,
Tell my wise elder, the hero!
Tell Jamgyrchy of the Esheks,
Tell the sage who has seen so much!
Tell the broad-backed woman
Who once tangled herself in my reins
And remained screaming and weeping!
If she knows news of the falcon,
Let her tell it swiftly, without delay;
Let her speak at once, without grieving a soul.
If she knows where the falcon went,
And tells its secret plainly,
Then I shall not raise my whip
Against your sister-in-law again;
I shall say neither this nor that,
And shall not take another wife beneath her.
If she says the falcon went by this road,
And afterward I deny my word,
May the open sky strike my crown!
May the hairy earth strike my breast!
May God Almighty strike me!
May Akkelte’s bullet strike me!
If that woman makes an empty boast,
May the oath I have spoken strike her!”
Speaking words of greeting and prayer,
And voicing his bitter lament,
Semetey, the son of Manas,
As though his father, the lion Manas,
Had died that very day,
Removed the saddle from camel-like Buurul
And left him bare,
Hobbled and tethered him,
Set the leather saddle cloth and blue pad
Against his own hip,
Appealed to the Creator,
And made the Akkangky his pillow.
The noble man’s brave son
Lay with his head toward the qibla,
Wrapped himself in the Akolpok,
And if you had seen Semetey,
He raged like a lion,
Flame sputtering from his mouth,
Sobbing, “My falcon!”
He scattered as though seeing darkest night,
Having lost Akshunkar.
The dear lion, huge as a fighting cock,
Lay motionless upon the plain.
He grasped the bulungur brass flute
By its corner and drew it close.
The sorrowing woman Chachykey
Had sent her two younger brothers;
Semetey lay in grief,
As though Kanykey herself had died.
His outstretched leg would not draw in,
His closed eyes would not open,
And his sighing would not cease;
Your blue-maned champion lay sprawled.
He tied Buurul at the grazing line,
And your magnanimous hero lay swollen with grief.
The two choros were warrior choros,
As though born as twins;
The two were equal as a single hair,
Choros like burning red embers,
Choros like arrows shot from a bow,
Calamities loosed upon the world.
When they advanced, they were his supports,
Setting the grassy land aflame,
The pillars of Tügöngür.
If your magnanimous Semetey remained safe,
These boys were the wings
With which he circled in flight;
If seven thousand foes appeared,
It was their custom to strike without wavering.
Kulchoro and Kanchoro—
From noble Almambet
Kulchoro was born afterward,
