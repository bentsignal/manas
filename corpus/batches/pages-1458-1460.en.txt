He struck it once upon the thigh,
Without turning back or stopping,
Took the road with a cry of “Go!”
Flashing out of the city,
To the mine where gold was dug,
Where fierce Kongurbay hid—
There he was lying then.
The mendicant drew near;
In the blinking of an eye
He reached Kongurbay.
The flatbread he had brought,
He handed over and went on.
When the fierce one broke it and looked,
In the middle of the flatbread
Was the letter Shuykuchu had sent.
When he read the letter,
It bore the name Shuykuchu.
This was written in the letter:
“Against Manas, who rules Beijing,
Your strength cannot prevail in a struggle.
He is one of the mighty fighters;
What Manas seeks
Is the Muslim faith.
He is truly a lion.
When I look within him,
He is broader than a river;
His fortune is complete.
If he rages, Beijing,
The countless Chinese and Manchus,
All the Chinese empire,
Its young and its old,
He is a warrior who could slaughter alone.
For the Muslim people
He is a fortress with iron walls.
Were it not that he is Burut,
For the Chinese emperor’s Beijing
He would be a fitting khan.
In combat, strength will not suffice;
In wrestling, might will not suffice.
Even if you fire, no shot will pierce him;
Neither spear nor sword will pierce him.
If you come to fight him,
He will bend your back,
He will ruin your people,
Set rivers of blood flowing
And flood your land.
If Manas catches sight of you,
You will bring trouble down;
For the Chinese of forty khans
You will bear the guilt of their suffering.
They have champion Manas as their khan,
Who count forty thousand as less than a hair—
He has his forty companions too.
Among those companions
Are the mighty Almambet and Chubak.
Why should I tell of the others?
There is that stubborn wolf Syrgak.
Why should I speak but briefly?
There are masters of the spear,
There are men skilled to wreck the world.
Unless you take him by a stratagem,
When we fight with Manas,
There is no day of escape for us!
Let me tell you Khan Manas’s secret:
He is a khan endowed with sacred power.
On every Friday
He has a certain habit.
As dawn breaks clear,
As light enters the earth,
First he washes his backside,
Then he washes his face,
Cleanses his whole body,
And last of all washes his feet.
He puts no boots upon his feet,
Has no weapons upon his body,
And wears only a robe.
He fears no one.
Beside his throne there is a tower;
All around are plane trees.
Before light reaches the earth,
Before dawn breaks bright,
Striding proudly with a sway,
The forty sentries guarding him
Keep watch all around.
Walking slowly and coughing,
The warrior Manas goes that way,
Climbs the tower himself,
Turns his face toward the west,
And loudly calls to prayer.
Crying ‘Allah!’ and raising his hands,
When he is calling to prayer,
When he performs that rite,
He looks neither to right nor left.
Even if you come before his eyes,
To ask, ‘Who are you?’—
Even if you cut off his head,
Manas will never turn to look.
Unless you find a stratagem then,
He will surely gouge out your eyes,
He will destroy you utterly.
He will dig your dark pit,
He will boil your brine of misery.
He will break your back;
Unless you use that stratagem,
He will destroy your people,
Slaughter the Chinese and wipe them out,
And make a dry riverbed of your land.
Unless you find such a stratagem,
He will plunder all your goods.
These Burut khans who have come
Will bring misery upon your head.”
Reading this, Kongurbay
Was struck speechless and wandered dazed;
He yellowed with jaundice.
Unable to think of a stratagem,
Kongurbay lost his wits.
“Unless we devise a cunning plan,
What Shuykuchu says is true:
No beast can match Akkula;
Without that stratagem,
No strength can master the warrior Manas.
Dust rises wherever he walks;
That unbelieving Burut—
His very voice drives out the soul.
Without this stratagem,
In combat we cannot match him,
