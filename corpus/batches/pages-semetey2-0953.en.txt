Their khans worshipped stones,
Their people thronged in multitudes,
And made Choro their very heart.
Everyone who went over, he won to his side,
And among the forty tribes of China
He became a formidable power:
“He has been living among the Burut,
Yet Choro knew well
That Burkan is God.
To call him a Burut Kyrgyz
Is idle slander.
He declares Burkan to be God;
His faith is loftier than ours.
He is a champion and a lion,
A hero beyond all measure,
A warrior fit to guard
This Beijing of the Khakan.
By cruel fate he was born
And lived among the Burut,
Yet he knew our god Burkan
Better than we ourselves.
Unable to learn the truth of Burkan,
He barely endured among the Burut.
Now he has returned and found his kin,
And this accursed boy has recognized
That Burkan is divine.
He is the true descendant
Of khans who gird the royal belt.
Of the khans who wear fine robes,
He is the mightiest himself;
He is the watchful dark eye
Of the kaldai in the karapchy coat;
Among the Manchu in coats of mail,
He is the very champion.
He is the offspring of true China,
The mainstay of Chin-Machin.
Without wearing out the racers
Of these forty tribes of China,
Without burdening their hearts—
He is a man fit to defend it.
For a khan’s descendant to wander homeless
Is, as we see, a grievous, senseless thing.
With cotton sash and broad boots,
Kongurbai, khan of the shaven-headed monks,
Stands one spear-cast above the rest;
His fortune is complete.
The powerful Chinese, the realm of forty khans,
Have a son fit to defend them.
He is both a strongman and a giant,
And master of our Great Beijing.
Fastening on his armor,
Riding around the Five Beijings,
He stands guard over Beijing.
He is the favored warrior who guards Beijing.
Donning his black armor,
Raging like a blue tiger,
He is the warrior who guards the kaldais.
Clad in armor and girded for battle,
Swaying as though striking this way,
He is the warrior who guards Beijing’s people.
From Kakanchin at its foot
To Keng-Tung and Bakburchun above,
He circles all these cities,
A sheathless sword at his side,
And guards the Chinese;
His fortune is complete.
If Kongurbai were to disappear,
He is the servant who would become master
Of the Chinese realm of forty khans.
Tall, broad-shouldered,
With high brows and fierce wrath,
He straightens out all who challenge him.
He is a champion and a bulwark,
A man surpassing all in the age.
He would be entrusted with Great Beijing,
Raised onto the golden throne,
And in Kakanchin’s Beijing
Proclaimed khan.
