Chewing white wheat flour together,
Licking the edge of a sword,
Making a solemn promise,
Taking hold of a twig
And cutting it in the middle,
To Bukhara with its twelve gates
The mighty warriors went and camped.
With that fairy-born soul
They firmly bound a marriage alliance.
Eighty girls stood at the gate;
Esteemed Kanykey,
From the red-robed, moon-waisted,
Gracefully moving beauties,
Leaving not so much as a hair,
Had gathered the finest together.
The people assembled, the crowd swelled;
When the people stood at the gate,
Silver-eyed and sun-faced,
The roaring leopard
Prepared like a hawk.
To the leopard herself
Counsel came from the unseen.
Glowing in her coat of mail,
Temirkan's daughter Kanykey,
Her moonlike face shining—
When they saw Kanykey,
The people crowded round in wonder;
The people stood gazing.
Beside the greathearted lion
Were Ajybay and Khan Bakay,
And his forty companions too.
From the ridge of Kum-Bulak
The gray puppy they had found earlier,
His Kumayyk, was there too.
Kumayyk, found while still young,
Was at an age when his eyes had not opened;
If Kumayyk were poorly tended,
He was in danger of dying.
Ajybay and Bakay carried him
Toward the dusky-complexioned beauty,
The radiant one among Muslims,
And approached Kanykey:
From the ridge of Kum-Bulak,
From the place of ritual washing,
Kumayyk had been found:
“Put a collar round his neck,
And tend him very carefully.
It would be shameful to let him die;
Kumayyk is entrusted to you!
Appearing from the unseen,
Through God's favor, for you
One of the peerless creatures was found.
He ordained a gray vulture to bear a puppy
And sent him to you alone.
If a man rears him, he will not stay;
He will vanish, I am told.
Unless the finest of the wide-robed,
The sovereign among women,
A woman tends him,
He will come to harm, I am told.
Kumayyk, whose origin is the unseen,
Unless a treasure of a woman rears him,
One day his desire
Will be fulfilled, I am told.
He will not stay despite being reared;
In the end Kumayyk will flee
And depart, I am told.
Understand what I have said;
Lion-hearted soul, heed my words!
Do not soil a single spot on him,
Nor break even half a hair!
Saint-born, I hear you are;
If you show your sacred power,
For a span of six months,
And after six months Kumayyk
You place safely in my hands,
I shall not strike you with a raised whip,
Nor take a co-wife behind your back.
If you tend the puppy well
And show your lion's nature,
If you place him safely in my hands,
Should I fail to bow before you,
May the shaggy-breasted earth strike me,
May the open-topped sky strike me!
Do not grow heedlessly conceited
And accidentally bring him to the grave.
Around Kumayyk's neck
Is a collar strap of gold.
Should he happen to be lost,
Not merely Bukhara—
The livestock of all nations could not meet
The enormous claim for him.
It would be shameful to let him die;
Kumayyk is entrusted to you!
If you rear Kumayyk well,
I shall slaughter ten thousand horses,
And give you the title 'My Lady.'
Like my cherished night,
Like the sun granted to my brow,
If Kumayyk's living presence is lost,
I shall turn my blessing into a curse
And utterly destroy your father.
I shall allow no earth to be opened with a hoe;
If my Kumayyk is lost,
I shall allow no handful of seed to be sown.
Here is the remarkable thing I say:
Beyond the means of the whole world
Is the compensation due for Kumayyk.
Sixty young wives and eighty girls,
With Kanykey at their head,
Agreed to what he said.
The gray puppy, small as a fist,
Your wolf-maned one called a lion
Now handed over as a trust.
Taking him in her hands, she looked:
The gray puppy, small as a fist,
Without letting anyone know,
Without letting a single person notice,
She took into her own hands.
His eyes had not yet opened;
Unless she watched and tended him closely,
That wretched Kumayyk
Was in danger of dying.
Kanykey took the gray puppy;
God's sun in the sky grew clouded,
And sixty worries came upon her at once:
“If Kumayyk should die,
Will he destroy us all together?
Bukhara, small as a palm,
Will he utterly sack?
Making Kumayyk his excuse,
Will he bring punishment upon us?
On my father, khan of Bukhara,
Will he bring every trouble at once?
Slaughtering Bukhara,
Taking its girls and women as booty,
Will he carry them to his people?”
So thought Kanykey.
She had a young mare slaughtered
Without letting anyone see.
The young mare's rib fat
She had him suck with his tongue;
She fed him milk from her mouth.
Without reclining on an elbow to rest,
Without snatching a moment's sleep,
Thus she reared the hunting hound.
She had a steel collar fitted to his neck,
Had a tiger-skin covering laid upon his back,
Dug a pit forty rope-lengths deep,
And had him tethered and reared at the bottom.
Spring came with moonless darkness;
Fanged creatures became his enemies.
'Predators will steal him,' she thought,
'And bring him to his end.'
Giving a horse as a month's wages,
Paying promptly without stint,
She prepared and stationed lookouts,
Had strong guards posted,
And made them endure great hardship.
Of the fanged creatures lying in wait
She had quite a number shot.
When six months had passed,
The noble puppy Kumayyk
Reached the age to prowl.
He proved a burning ember;
Among four-footed predators
None was so formidable.
The steel collar upon his neck
He chewed apart.
Eighty young wives and eighty girls
Were resting at their leisure;
Into the midst of the many girls,
As though about to slaughter them,
He came prowling.
Before they could raise a shout,
Before they could seize his collar,
Into the embers of saxaul wood
He swept his tail.
His tail touched the embers;
Three finger-widths of it burned.
'He will inflict terrible suffering,' they thought,
'On Kumayyk's account
He will start a quarrel.'
Before they could rub it with fat,
Remove the burned part,
And wipe his fur clean,
Because of what had happened,
On Kumayyk's account,
God struck them in an extraordinary way:
Looking as huge as a mountain,
Troubling the hearts of those who saw him,
On Kumayyk's account
He arrived on his bridegroom's visit.
Kumayyk, his tail burned,
Before the fiercely wrathful lord
Came prowling.
When Manas saw the puppy,
His blood-drinking fury seized him.
His white teeth, broad as a door,
Teeth unlike those of other people,
He ground again and again.
“God, who strikes, has struck her!
The puppy I told her to rear
She has made a scrawny pup of him!
Without giving him her full attention,
God has struck the wretched girl!
Unequaled among mortals,
My champion she has reduced to misery!
She herself is still a little girl,
A conceited young noblewoman;
I see that God has struck her!
The puppy I said to rear
She has made a scrawny pup of him!
He took her by the hand and swung her round,
And kicked her under the arm.
Before enjoying their happiness,
Before lying in her embrace,
Before gathering the learned men,
He seized her left hand and swung her round,
Kicked her under the arm,
And two of her ribs,
When the warrior's foot struck,
Were terribly shattered.
The twelve pairs of ribs
Nearly slipped out of place.
Then Kanykey,
Her heart broken,
Without speaking in protest,
Her tears pouring down,
'The blame is mine alone;
My own deed has overtaken me,' she thought.
She made no retort,
Nor complained of brow or eyebrow.
Because of that wrongful deed,
He laid punishment upon her
And left without lying in her embrace.
Kozubek, khan of Kokand,
Malabek of Margalan,
Fork-bearded Synchybek of Andijan,
The box-bearded Uzbek—
With these, the four khans' people were Sarts,
No strength to bind about one's waist,
But an affliction like an aching back.
As though that were not enough,
Dust rose from between,
And they came from lower India too.
Dwelling in Sary-Arka,
Called a weighty, formidable people,
With yurt lattices of wood,
Their forefather being Alach,
Hero Kökchö, Aydarkan's son,
Came to that feast.
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshtek,
And here Ormonbek of Khan Tekeche,
Summering in the Two Kemins,
Tethering a pair of black horses,
Sowing autumn rice,
Feeding the tethered horses its weeds,
Sharpening his dark tongue,
Speaking eloquently before the khan,
Gathering mixed folk into a people—
Hero Ürbü of Khan Eybit
Came to that feast as well.
From the people of lower Urum,
Muzburchak, Buudaykan's son,
He too came to the feast.
His wide eyes like the morning star,
His moonlike ears like shields,
Whose blessing enriched the people,
Whose care brought them light,
Khan Koshoy of the Katagan
Was there to speak impartially.
The twelve Muslim khans
At the marsh of Üyrülmö
Gathered round in council;
At the marsh of Chogulma
They assembled in council.
Laying their words before each other,
Gathering together,
Seeking and finding schemes,
The twelve Muslim khans—
What would they not gather?
To Manas, resting there,
What would they not do?
Upon Manas from above,
Why should they not descend?
That poor man's four kinds of livestock,
Why should they not scatter?
“Let us fall upon his head,
Let us scatter his four kinds of livestock.
Raiding and taking his mares,
Let us take revenge on him!
If Manas comes near,
Let us punch his brains out,
Let us take revenge on him!
Have you noticed Manas?
Have you seen his grand bearing,
Like a wineskin soaked in water,
Have you seen him swell with pride?
Chasing white deer,
Have you seen him hunt?
Openly among all the people,
Have you seen him stir up turmoil?
The dark forest and ravine,
Have you seen him make his enemy?
The poor man who fled to him,
Have you seen him make powerful?
Talas where he dwells,
Have you seen him make his homeland?
The companion who came from Khangay,
Have you seen him make a khan?
The troublemaker who fled to him,
Have you seen him make his equal?
Almambet and Chubak,
Have you seen him make them lions?
Twelve nations had not struck;
Against Manas, with all this,
None remains to turn a lasso pole.
If we do not attack this Manas,
If we do not find a sure way to deal with him,
He will plunder the spoils;
Manas will stir up disorder!
Now Manas has
A dark fur cap as big as a cauldron.
If we let that poor man have his way,
Over all the people
He has the will to rule.”
Talking this nonsense,
The twelve Muslim khans
Concluded their consultation.
Then Koshoy spoke these words:
“He had fortune in full measure;
He was a warrior supported by the saint,
For all the Muslims standing here,
He was a warrior who could provide.
Even now Manas's
Strength is formidable.
You handful of Muslims,
Beware lest he deal with you all!
Do not remind Manas
And have him knock out your ancient madness.
Beware lest all the people standing here
He gather together,
And at Kakanchyn's Beijing
Have them slaughtered together.
He lives at ease in Talas;
If you remind him of Beijing,
You handful of Muslims,
He will drive you in under guard.
I know the story of Beijing.
In the time of the prophet Noah,
The Flood swept through here.
When the Flood surged and flowed,
When it covered the whole earth,
After that it became a land,
And the firmly rooted Chinese became a people.
When the holy prophet Muhammad
Came out to his palace,
Radiance poured upon Beijing.
His Holiness Ali the Lion—
Great Beijing has come down through the ages.
Beware of stirring Manas up,
Lest he gather you as a levy,
Drive you to Beijing,
And surely have you slaughtered.”
Though Koshoy gave counsel,
They did not understand his counsel.
The Muslims of the twelve khans
Would not heed his words at all.
Though Koshoy spoke,
Not one obeyed him.
With that, the Muslims
Did not hold themselves back.
Their finest spear fighters,
Did you see them lead them out?
Those skilled with the battle-axe,
Did you see them equip them?
Broad-jawed and broad-tongued,
They set their orators apart;
Those who shot without missing,
They set their marksmen apart.
Those skilled with the sword,
The finest who knew the enemy's ways,
Gathering all of these,
Did you see them lead them out?
A red banner with a golden crescent
They raised and carried at the front.
Twelve thousand marksmen
Set their faces toward Talas.
Riding their horses forward,
Swearing an oath unto death,
Chewing white wheat flour,
'Let whoever flees be a woman!' they said,
Swearing upon the holy Quran.
They headed for the bounds of broad Talas,
Making their promise firm.
The skilled among spear fighters,
The finest of them all,
Displaying his ample blue overshoes,
Making his thoroughbred hold its head high,
Kozubek, khan of Kokand—
Did you see the khan enter?
Surging along behind him,
Did you see them all enter?
Some of them rode
Karabayyr warhorses;
Some of them rode
Sieve-lunged, copper-limbed steeds.
The twelve Muslim khans,
Looking neither up nor down,
Not thinking they might die,
Their swords swaying,
Their spears quivering,
Rode down into Talas
To pick a fight with Manas.
Of Khan Manas's forty companions,
The finest of the forty,
Wearing a gray lynx-fur cap,
Playing his blue-mottled shield,
Sheltering his head in abundant gold,
Supporting his feet with a thousand silver pieces,
Who found an eye in darkness,
Found a word at an unexpected moment,
If he led the way, the road was blessed;
If he journeyed far, he was like countless troops.
Khan Bakay, the rich man's son,
Who rinsed his mouth with honey,
The sovereign of the companions,
A saint born of humankind—
Wolf-maned hero Bakay's
Wisdom was a broad river.
Wise Bakay, the saint,
When he saw this conduct of the twelve khans,
All the companions,
Did you see him gather them?
Syrgak of Khan Ulak,
Did you see him restrain him?
Calling out for consultation,
Hero Bakay then
Ajybay, Argyn's son,
He summoned, his wise counselor:
“Your fathers' graves, forty companions!
We forty were warriors from forty places;
All of us alike were lions!
Some came without horses.
To the companion who came horseless,
An argali-footed, copper-limbed,
Swift horse he gave to ride.
To the companion who came without a coat,
Double mail with lark-eye rings,
A netted outer coat, a broad jacket,
Gold-collared and copper-sleeved,
He gave the finest coat to wear.
To the companion who came without a wife,
A thimble-mouthed, camel-eyed,
Broad-hipped woman with jeweled braid ornaments,
Mighty Manas gave as a wife.
To the companion who came without a faith,
Aykhojo in a mottled robe
Mighty Manas gave as a mullah.
To the companion who came homeless,
As usual, without hesitation,
Mighty Manas gave a royal dwelling.
Ajybay, son of Khan Argyn,
Knows sixty different tongues.
If this is an enemy from China,
Lest trouble befall us,
Find out the news of them.
If they are people of the Islamic faith,
Ascertain it and bring word.
To this Talas here,
No mortal has ridden in deliberately;
No person has come without ritual washing.
If we sit and consider,
A dug pit is coming toward us,
A great peril to our precious lives.”
The broad, screw-fitted telescope
He grasped by its edge and turned.
To ascertain this news,
Ajybay of Khan Argyn
He expressly sent.
Ajybay went to gather news.
Yraman's son Yrchy Uul,
With a tasselled drawstring of forty joints,
Old Kyrgyl, leader of the forty,
The mad elder who grew conceited at forty,
Shuutu the servant, his cap as big as a roof-felt,
Dark steel, broad-shouldered—
Have you heard of him?—
Chubak of Khan Baltay;
Golden-crested, with a tasselled sash,
Almambet of Azizkan;
Baychoro of Khan Baabedin,
Skilled at fattening horses:
With Khan Manas among them,
Together they numbered forty.
The forty companions, born as lions,
Who never withdrew a thrusting spear,
Never fled downcast from the enemy,
Girded with warriors' belts—
Though seven thousand foes should come,
One alone would attack them.
Told to take a cap, they cut off a head:
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Like white-headed winged steeds among horses,
Almambet and Chubak among them,
At the people who had come to Talas
All alike were astonished.
The steel weapons in their hands
They gripped, their straps wound round their wrists.
The champions, wolves of war,
Bound battle-axes to their waists.
All their weapons of war
They prepared and stood ready.
The sovereign prophet's faith was pure;
A golden throne remained in Beijing.
Almambet of Azizkan
Now turned Sarala's head,
And to stately Chubak
Spoke his counsel:
“If the enemy has come from Mecca,
You attack toward Mecca.
If the enemy has come from Beijing,
Let me mount the horse Sarala
And attack toward Beijing.
Holding a spear, breaking their ranks,
If death comes this year, falling to a shot,
Until the swaying dry stalks ripen,
Until the autumn gossamer flies,
Fighting six months without rest,
Let me die if my hour has come.
If I have the means, this enemy's
Midst I shall dye with blood.”
Sarala with the cowlike tailbone
Almambet wheeled round;
He turned Sarala's head.
Almambet, accustomed to exchanging shots,
Against the many foes who had arrived
Now stood prepared.
No man's strength could overcome him in a grapple,
No lion's tooth could pierce him;
With piercing roars and ringing battle cries,
Chubak of Akbaltay,
Who threw the whole world into confusion,
Who wore an enormous, ungainly blue coat,
Who rode a horse like Kögala,
That dark, fierce-tempered rogue,
Chubak of Khan Baltay,
Against the many foes openly approaching
Now stood prepared.
Ajybay of Khan Argyn,
Who knew sixty different tongues,
Went out to meet them
And began speaking gently:
“You handful of Muslims,
You have driven your men here;
Your skilled spear fighters
You have sent ahead!
Is there a mountain you cannot cross?
With you, Muslims,
Is there an enemy who has come to exchange shots?
Is there a pit dug in your path?
You have made ready;
Are there foot soldiers challenging you?
You have gathered in great strength,
You have prepared yourselves.
Is there a mountain lying unawares?
Is there an enemy from Beijing?”
Then they said:
“Reckless Manas,
If our strength suffices this time,
We have come to seize Manas.
'We shall raid,' we said, and came
For his mares in Keng-Kol.
From Manas we shall exact
Vengeance for the distant past.
Do not block my road;
Let my troops pass.
We have come to show
Something truly extraordinary!”
Ajybay, Argyn's son,
Turned back with a shout,
Calling to Chubak of Khan Baltay,
'Where are you?'
And beating the drum,
He gave word to the companions,
Striking the war drum.
Almambet of Azizkan
Sarala beneath him
He whipped on right and left,
Spurring him on either side.
Syrgak of Khan Ulak,
With a horse like Kök Chebich,
With two swift horses,
Charged close with a roar.
Calling 'God!' as his battle cry,
Hero Syrgak rode into the fray.
Veering with Kögala,
Starting a clash the moment he saw them,
A polished spear thundering in his hand,
At Chubak's looming form
The earth reeled and thundered.
Behind him the forty companions
Rode together with a thunderous roar.
'The forty leopards will reach them,' he thought;
'If the forty companions take the notion,
These people of mine who have come
They will sweep through and slaughter.'
“My beloved forty companions,
It is their khans who are fools;
What fault lies with the common people?
If you rashly attack and kill them,
We too shall die one day.
On the coming Day of Resurrection,
When the Prophet sits as judge,
What answer shall we give?
Those skilled among the spear fighters,
Before leopard Manas
Let us seize and bring,
The mighty men who led them.
Forty companions, your prowess is beyond comparison;
For you to destroy and slaughter,
There are no Chinese people here!”
They heeded Bakay's words.
Almambet of Azizkan
And Chubak of Akbaltay
The companions who had charged
Barely managed to restrain.
The Muslims who had come of their own accord,
Rashly coming to pick a fight,
Now fell into hardship.
Almambet of Azizkan
Drove off a thousand;
Chubak of Akbaltay
Took a thousand under guard.
All the remainder
Dismounted in a flurry,
Threw down their battle-axes,
And stood drawing a line.
The Muslims who had come themselves,
From Khan Manas's companions,
Were about to see a spectacle;
Khan Manas's companions
Were about to bring them trouble.
Riding a horse like Sarala,
Wearing yellow leather trousers,
Holding a spear without a hair tassel,
Girded with a bare sword,
Golden-crested, with a tasselled sash,
Azizkan's only son
Rode into their midst.
Chubak of Khan Akbaltay,
Did you see him drive them off?
The soldiers of the twelve khans,
Making them all dismount,
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Before hero Manas,
Did you see them bring them bound?
Then Khan Bakay, the rich man's son—
The ground before the court was dusty;
There were some twelve thousand souls.
All went submissively,
Driven like livestock!
When they reached Khan Manas,
Did you see their lives grow wretched?
All alike gazing helplessly,
Did you see them lose their speech?
At that time Khan Manas,
Thundering in fury,
Roaring like a surging lake,
Equipped with sharp steel,
Girded with a diamond-hard sword,
Finding no mountain to charge against,
Finding no enemy to fight,
Khan Manas, scarcely restraining himself,
Poured forth his wrath,
Appearing like a devouring fiend,
His face like buttered wheat,
His eyes like low-lying mist.
At that time, against Manas
Even twelve nations had made no assault;
None remained to speak a word against him.
He stood cutting and eating horse-rump meat.
'Not merely the Muslims—I shall crush
The Chinese,' he said.
Considering his renown,
Could Manas ever have his fill of blood?
Let alone the people who had come,
Would Manas spare the nations?
Before another moment passed,
The forty companions, born as lions,
Upon a carpet patterned in gold,
Manas, born a precious one,
They brought and seated.
To the many people lying bound
They lifted and carried him.
When they saw Khan Manas,
They quivered, unable to speak;
All those people
Lost their words and only murmured.
The seven khans, finding no escape,
Trembled toward one another.
At that moment Khan Manas,
Roaring in anger, said:
“Twelve Muslim khans,
Did I scatter the goods in your saddlebags,
For you to treat me like this?
Did I chase off your wild asses?
Did I scatter the goods in your chests?
Your fathers' graves, seven khans!
Did I raid and drive off your milk animals?
The forked rises and hollow-backed ridges,
You know I crossed them.
The Muslims of the whole world,
You know I cared for them.
Since Manas became Manas,
Since he gained the name of warrior,
You do not know how I surpassed others.
In the face of hostile enemies,
Standing like a banner at your borders,
You do not know how I barred their way.
Iron pieced together is not sharp steel;
Khans cobbled together are not a people.
Plain iron is not sharp steel;
Khans raised apart are not a people.
Deceit lies in their eyes;
If these khans have the strength,
They are ready to strike me down.
Crookedness lies in their words;
If the seven khans get their chance,
They mean to do it to me.
Your fathers' graves, forty companions!
Bind the seven khans
And drive them over here!”
At that moment, of the seven khans,
Not one found his tongue.
Of Manas's wrath
They were afraid and made no answer.
Could they turn aside Manas's words?
Once he had ordered, 'Drive them!'
Could the companions fail to drive them?
Ajybay of Khan Argyn
And Chubak of Akbaltay,
'Drive them all here!' they said,
As they passed the order.
Moving sideways like a swift horse,
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshtek,
Folding his hands and bowing,
Came to Manas and began to speak,
Speaking softly and deliberately:
Here are the Nogay people with their sovereign,
Here is the dark pit that has been dug;
Here is the brine to boil us in.
If you would bind us, here are our hands;
If you would drive us, here is the road;
If you would slaughter, here we are!
Through ridges with towering passes,
We came shooting argali and eating them.
'While lion Manas is among us,
With the renowned land of Beijing
Let us exchange shots,' we said, and came.
'The Chinese with red tassels
He will slaughter for us,' we said, and came.
Beijing, beyond Chalyar’s reach,
He will bring within our measure,' we said, and came.
'Beijing, untouched by His Holiness,
If hero Manas finds the means,
He will conquer for us,' we said, and came.
With a hemp sash and broad boots,
Kongurbay, khan of the monks,
That fearsome fellow, at full length,
He will lay out for us,' we said, and came.
'The shot-proof garments Kanykey made
Let us put on,' we said, and came.
'The Kalmyks dwelling in Khangay,
While Khan Manas is among us,
Let us attack,' we said, and came.
'The teeming people in the east,
Let us engage in conflict,' we said, and came.
'While peerless Manas is among us,
The swarming Chinese,
Let us exchange shots with them,' we said, and came.
'For the warrior to wear far from home,
Let us be a coat,' we said, and came.
'If he sets out on a distant journey,
Let us be his army,' we said, and came.”
Then Manas said, “Agreed!
What Zhamgyrchy has said
Is counsel for us all.”
Giving six swift horses,
Giving a letter small as a palm,
“Let my elder Koshoy come,
Of Kakanchyn's Beijing,
Let him see the great turmoil!
Has it not been spoken of?
The twelve Muslim khans,
Let him drive them all here.
Let the leopard turn his head this way.
My sacred elder,
The Muslims' red banner,
Against the massed Chinese,
Tell him to hold it for us.
After my elder Koshoy,
Aykhojo of Andijan,
Let him heed these words of mine:
For all these people here,
Tell him to serve as imam.
In the sky the blue-mottled clouds were gray;
Hero Er Töshtük of Eleman—
We were friends for the world to come.
Let no mistaken message be sent;
Hero Er Töshtük of Eleman
Must not be left out of this journey.
Whoever travels without ritual washing,
Fold him at the waist and crush him!
Whoever speaks in protest,
I shall slaughter and spill his blood,
Gathering his four kinds of livestock.
If he refuses to obey me,
I shall slaughter him before his appointed hour.
Whoever gives a retort,
I shall seize him by the back,
Have him dragged along the road
And torn apart by great dogs.
Muslims, if this is so,
What I have said is wise counsel.
From this Beijing before us,
Whoever holds back is an infidel, I say.
Let him not open a path into the hills;
Let the Muslims who hear this
Not flee to their separate homes.
Forty companions, born as lions,
You lived at ease in Talas;
The khans who turned back from Beijing
You drove here under guard.
Let them not scout a path through the hills,
The Muslims, their death-hour come,
