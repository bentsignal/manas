Let me leave it to you.
Now that my beard is white,
For how many years have I been khan?
Without giving you my throne,
What wish could I fulfill!”
The golden crown upon his head
He craftily had me wear.
The golden throne beneath him—
He seated me there to kill me.
With cunning, Karykan
Made his people acknowledge my rule.
This became the occasion to save my life:
Then there entered my dream
A barefoot wandering holy man.
“Do not wear the golden crown;
Karykan is deceiving you.
Do not mount the throne before you.
Beneath the golden throne
Is a great carpet patterned in gold;
Even soaked in water forty years,
Its face would not fade a whit.
This golden throne of Beijing
Has never fallen to another's lot.
When the Scales star sets,
When dawn splits and breaks,
If you doubt it, lift and look
Beneath the gold-patterned carpet.
Dug beneath it
Is a pit some forty fathoms deep.
Would a khan behave like that?
The golden crown, the precious throne—
Would the man who mounts it prosper?
If you look along its side,
No winged bird could fly through,
No wild ass could leap across;
Of adamant steel and iron
A mesh has been forged there.
If you look farther on,
One who spares none he grapples,
Who, eating a pig all alone,
Is not filled by a single helping—
There is a mighty fellow like Madykan.
He has a house built in Beijing.
Deliberately to slaughter you,
The Chinese have held ready
An officer named Madykan.
You will see all these things.
In the Chinese realm's Beijing,
If you are khan for twenty days,
Helplessly, Almambet, you will die!”
When he spoke thus,
I started awake from sleep,
And pondered the dream I had seen.
The deep pit they had dug—
It was truly a ravine.
Devising his scheme, Karykan
Had gathered the Chinese multitudes;
It was true he would turn enemy.
The reason for his hostile intent:
He had selected his champions,
Stretched a mesh of iron,
Conferred in furtive agitation,
And drawn out all the Chinese troops.
Fortifying Great Beijing's outskirts,
Your Chinese had made
The way out secure.
To kill your Almambet,
A great many Chinese
Set down writing and swore an oath.
Their countless people roared;
To the idol forged by hand
They babbled their worship.
At that time Beijing
Was seething full of Chinese.
Since my arrival in Beijing
Nineteen days had passed.
At that moment the Chinese
Month of the Chagaan spring festival had arrived.
When the Chagaan festival month came,
A copper cauldron with forty handles
They hung up in the street.
Inside that cauldron,
Forty mules and forty pigs,
Forty cats and forty dogs,
They slaughtered and put inside.
Forty of every kind of animal
They brought there.
A copper idol big as a hut
They would hang up and fasten.
Around that idol,
The Chinese would swarm and crowd.
Fashioning it with their hands,
Calling their bronze their god,
The thick-haired Chinese multitudes
Would busy themselves like this.
At the fortress in Beijing,
When the Chagaan festival day came—
My words were a lament—
In my mother's household,
To boil her food,
There was a Chinese servant.
He came running up,
Bending his knees to the ground,
Paid homage in the Chinese fashion,
And bowed a number of times.
He put a written letter in my hand,
Quickly, with great haste, he gave it.
It was a letter written in Chinese.
When I read the letter,
My mother's name Altynay was there.
My mother had written it weeping and lamenting;
She had written words within the letter.
When I read her words,
My dear mother,
She had written thus:
“Until the Five Wild Sheep stars set,
I turned five times to nurse you.
My champion Almambet, my only one,
Until the Six Wild Sheep stars set,
I turned six times to nurse you.
You widened my narrow womb,
You emptied my stone-hard breasts,
My spring flowing in the valley,
My lamp granted near life's end!
Why remain in Beijing?
Before reaching your prime,
You will soon die.
She who writes this weeping and wailing,
Your mother has died now, she says.
Come to attend me, she says.
A dark, unfortunate wretch like me,
Bury with your own hands,” she says.
When I heard this news,
Clouds gathered in the sky, Manas;
Sixty anxieties fell on my head at once.
When I heard this news,
My red blood swirled and boiled.
As I stood under guard,
My poor soul could not hold together.
If my sixty mothers died there,
If my own mother Altynay
Now died of her grief,
I had come and settled in Beijing;
Of the Chinese under their emperor,
However many of their fathers I slaughtered,
I had become like an extinguished fire.
Hearing this news,
“Let me die rather than live!
The Chinese with rank-buttons on their caps—
Let me shatter them and spill their blood!
Before this day is out, I, unfortunate one,
Let me reach and see my mother!
The humiliation the Chinese inflict,
If it falls on my head, I shall face it.
Karykan, khan of Beijing,
If that infidel comes into my hands,
I shall spill his blood like water!”
I turned, my poor soul troubled,
My waist bent like a fiber,
Toward Karykan's golden throne
I gathered myself to spring.
Karykan's golden throne,
The plane trees planted all around it—
My friend Manas, my goshawk,
Unable to withstand my wrath,
The plane-tree grove collapsed.
Beneath those plane trees,
The one who sat guarding the throne,
Karykan's forty guards—
I slaughtered those forty guards.
Leaving not one alive,
I killed them all.
Such was Almambet's wildness.
One regret remained; what could I do?
Karykan was not there.
Killing six of his khans,
I came out after wreaking havoc.
Karykan's smooth musket
I took away as a gift.
Karykan's weather-stone
I took with me then,
Thinking I would need it for my life.
My knees bent toward the earth;
I ran out through the doorway.
Before me, Sarala
Was standing saddled.
Gathering myself, I leapt astride,
My armor bending upon me.
Bringing down the gate,
Renouncing my idol,
Leaving my Chinese homeland,
I raced away on Sarala.
By then noon had passed;
The sun leaned toward the west.
The bright sun in the sky
Had begun to decline.
Roaring saltwater lay there;
Between Tash-Köpürö and Beijing
Was a journey of forty days.
Having shattered Beijing,
I left after noon.
When the sun sank to its nest,
I reached my mother.
Help came from Heaven;
God shed His light, I thought.
Tash-Köpürö, forty days away—
Now, when I think of it,
The angel Gabriel
Must have drawn in the earth's measuring-rope.
That difficult forty-day road,
The wondrously swift Sarala
Covered in half a day.
Fearing my horse would collapse and die,
Lose his mane and tail,
And suffer as never before,
I grasped his sacred mane.
He had a mane like a lead-rope;
Sarala, the swift creature,
Had tethered himself.
On that day Sarala
Was ready to fly.
As the sun sank slowly,
I reached my mother.
Around Azizkan's great pillar
I looped the lead-rope.
I thought her dead, but she was alive.
A curse upon such living!
Through sorrow and anguish,
She was as one of the dead.
When I saw my mother,
I did not stretch my neck and turn.
With no thought of becoming Muslim,
A wretched soul Chinese from the first,
Bending my knees and bowing,
I paid homage in the Chinese fashion.
Altynay, my dear mother,
Did not look at my fair face.
Turning sharply away,
Jerking round in the opposite direction,
She did not come to greet me.
She poured no bowl, gave me no food,
Not food, not even a black stone.
In Tash-Köpürö's fortress,
When it was time for people to sleep,
She laid out white down,
With its head toward the prayer-direction.
My mother made a bed; I lay down.
When my eyes closed in sleep,
I found myself lying in sunlight.
As I lay there, in my dream,
Cleansed of the dark religion,
I was entering the faith.
A holy man's staff was in my hand,
A white turban was on my head.
I ran barefoot, painfully onward.
May no mortal suffer it:
I came upon a road of calamity.
If I tried to walk, I screamed;
My poor legs, all of them,
Were stripped up to the knees.
If I tried not to walk, I screamed;
My fly-like soul was scorched.
When I looked to my right—
May a faithful young man not die!
The torment that I saw,
May no human being receive it!
When I looked to my left,
Seven doors appeared.
When I saw those seven doors,
Tears poured from my eyes.
Those who said, “I'll go and see,”
Champions who vaunted their strength,
Those who consumed another's due—
It was a place where the ignorant lay.
When I looked closely then,
It was the hell of torment.
God subjected me to suffering.
Just then I encountered
That old white-bearded holy wanderer.
I thrashed up and down.
“Explain its meaning to me,”
I asked the holy man.
The holy man stood and said:
“First profess your faith, my son.
While you still can, Almambet,
Turn to the Islamic faith, my son.
Seen today, gone tomorrow,
Distracting you in this way,
The mischievous world deceives you!
My son, look to your right:
Palaces built there, cool houses,
Apples joined in pairs,
Beautiful girls arm in arm—
Let me explain these things.
The palaces and cool houses
Are allotted to faithful servants;
That is where Muslims will dwell.
The many apples joined together,
Which quench thirst when eaten,
Are the food that Muslims will eat.
The beautiful girls arm in arm,
Granted to the faithful,
A virgin each day, though lain with each day—
On the coming Day of Judgment,
Those are the wives Muslims will receive.
As you wish, pass away an infidel, my son;
You will go to the ravine on the left,
If you die a Muslim.”
Saying this, the holy man
Vanished from sight.
I awoke with a start,
And turned onto my right side.
It was a dream seen in sleep;
It was God's doing.
Bright dawn had broken;
Light had come upon the earth.
My mother Altynay,
Her head upon the prayer-rug,
Facing the direction of prayer,
Was immersed in great delight.
In the Muslim manner, my mother
Was reciting her prayers.
My heart was deeply disturbed;
Chinese from the first, a wretched soul,
“With her backside raised,
What kind of prayer is she praying?” I thought.
It looked ugly to my eyes.
When she had said her prayers,
When she folded her prayer-rug,
I told her what had happened.
I related to my mother
The dream I had seen that night.
Then my mother said this:
“O my son, I was the wife of Light;
Pure in spirit from birth,
You were the son of Light.
Lingering among the Chinese,
What wish will you fulfill?
Grabbing me by the hair, you dark night,
Into that hell
You will drag me with you.
Speak, my son, profess it, my son!
Draw your horse's reins, my son.
Go to the white palace, my son;
Bathe your body in water, my son.
Afterward come back,
And see your mother's face,” she said.
A washing ewer filled
With water she put in my hand.
A razor made of steel—
“Cleanse yourself front and back,
Then come afterward,” she said,
And gave that into my hand too.
My mother drove me out, and I went.
I went to the white palace;
I bathed my body in water.
With the razor made of steel,
I cleansed myself front and back.
Then to my mother
I came walking back.
My mother's shining white face
I saw only afterward.
My friend Manas, leopard,
I, your Almambet standing before you,
Entered the faith in this way.
Because there is a Judgment Day,
Because of the hair-thin bridge,
I came to my people without looking back.
Khan Manas, I renounced my idol,
I left my Chinese homeland.
I scattered what I had gathered,
And came seeking faith.
I fled my people, calling them unbelievers;
I sought the faith as dearer to me.
You have deeply wounded my heart,
Calling me an infidel to my face.
Let me leave that aside.
The countless Chinese, people of forty khans—
Let me begin where I shattered them.
Finding no place in the rough country,
Finding no people to turn to,
I, wretched soul, went to Khangay.
I called upon Khangay's khan,
And told him, “Become a Muslim.”
When Khangay would not agree,
I killed the khan of Khangay,
And turned back and came.
Let it be, Manas, leopard!
My sun clouds over in the sky;
When I remember vast Beijing,
Sixty anxieties come upon me at once.
The Chinese in Beijing had plenty;
But your champion, Sultan Almambet,
Had no kinsman to support him.
If my words sound like lament to you,
These Chinese standing here—
Divide them into a thousand groups:
If in even one of them
There were someone to follow me,
Would I not strengthen my claim,
Would I not mark out my own borders?
Renouncing my idol,
How could I have abandoned
My vast Chinese people?
Through this unfortunate one's wildness,
I fled from Beijing and came,
For want of a supportive companion.
Seeking my Muslim faith,
I laid low many of their khans.
In the Chinese realm's Beijing,
Unable to find the Muslim faith,
How many beautiful women and girls
I made scream and weep!
Of so many Chinese,
I laid their companies low.
Of the Chinese who faced me,
I brought down many warriors.
I rode on Sarala,
And came to my mother Altynay.
Then my mother said this to me:
“My only one,
Do not begin a war against Beijing.
Take him as your companion;
Do not abandon your Mazhik!
The Chinese robe, having no choice,
Your Mazhik wears.
‘When will my Almambet come?’
Your Mazhik pines and wonders.
If you leave your Mazhik,
You do not know his foolishness.
If what is told is true,
Will he not say, ‘He was a dog’?
When you reach the afterlife,
May he not make you a black donkey to ride!
Do not begin a war against Beijing;
If you fight against Beijing,
Never abandon Mazhik.
Along the broad street
Is Mazhik, tending cattle.
Heed my words and go to him;
Mazhik of the pure faith,
Bring him back as your companion.
Your Mazhik, tending cattle,
Held under guard among the Chinese,
He is that unfortunate warrior.
If you seek vengeance against Beijing,
He is the lion to take as companion.
Pure in spirit from birth,
He belongs to the people of Islam.”
This account of Mazhik
I heard from my mother.
Unable to endure my burning heart,
I went down the broad street.
When I passed along the broad street,
All the cattle had stampeded,
Fled into the streams,
And taken shelter there.
His blue-piebald horse saddle-sore,
My dear champion Mazhik,
Tending cattle among the Chinese,
Causing my heart deep pain,
With his Kalmyk saddle as a pillow,
Had fallen asleep.
When I looked, it was Mazhik himself.
He wore a greasy black coat.
Your jovial Mazhik
Looked just like a Kalmyk.
When I saw hero Mazhik,
“Would a Muslim be like this?
Would anyone rightly call this a Muslim?
Let me gouge out his eyes,
Make him a feast for vultures,
Black Kalmyk Mazhik,
Let me slaughter him here!”
My double-edged sword
I drew from its sheath.
To cut off his head,
I approached Mazhik.
Mazhik awoke from sleep,
Sprang up, turning about,
And poured out his greeting.
When I heard Mazhik's greeting,
My bones melted utterly.
Greeting me in his distress,
Mazhik then stood
Pleading with unfortunate me:
“Parted from the people of my home,
What am I, tending cattle?
You, pure in spirit from birth,
Me, unfortunate Mazhik,
What are you, giving me no thought?
What darkness, what a night!
Will a day come to reach the Muslims?
When I have reached the Muslims,
Will there be a day to die,
With them leaning together and wailing,
All of them Muslims,
A day when they will bury me?”
Hero, blue-maned Khan Manas,
When I heard Mazhik's words,
My world closed painfully about me.
From among the cattle-herders,
Not hero Mazhik alone,
I gathered forty companions, leopard.
Together with brave Mazhik,
Forty lions had come to me.

I trained them all to full strength.
To act against Kongurbay,
In earnest, my friend, I laid plans.
“If I should clash with the Chinese,
If I should fight Kongurbay,
The forty companions who look to me
Have no horses to ride,
Have no coats to wear in battle.
Will the Chinese seize my forty companions?
With no coats to wear,
Will they all be slaughtered?”
Thinking of this then,
I struck Sarala on the thigh.
He was a mighty khan of the Chinese;
I went to General Ay.
For the sake of my forty companions,
I shattered General Ay's forces.
On the ridge of It-Ölbös,
I laid two thousand of his warriors low.
I brought turmoil upon the Chinese.
Then I drove away
Swift horses for my forty companions to ride.
Some I mounted on
Karabayyr steeds of battle;
Some I mounted on
Horses with sieve-like lungs and copper wings.
Some I mounted on
Mountain roes with wild rams' necks;
Some I mounted on
Horses with wild ewes' necks and copper forelegs.
Some I mounted on
Horses with trap-like rumps and owls' feet;
Some I mounted on
Horses with bull-camel forelocks and sickle heads.
Some I mounted on
Horses with wild asses' legs and camel-calves' eyes.
For red spears and sharp steel,
I mounted them on steeds fitted for these.
For forty days alike in battle,
Without gulping a drop of water,
I mounted them on steeds that could endure.
Double mail, with lark-eye rings,
With collars of gold and sleeves of copper—
I clothed them in the finest coats.
Never held back when swung,
Never turning back from a blow,
I placed hungry steel in their hands.
Satisfying their desire when fired,
Its menace too great to withstand,
Sending many thousand enemies fleeing—
I slung such guns upon their backs.
With points steeped in poison,
I placed spears in their hands.
I thought that I would die
For the sake of my forty companions.
I meant to strike
The arrogant Chinese,
Their vast and countless host.
I turned Sarala's head.
Karykan's weather-stone,
A bowl of plain water—
I struck it with a breath-blown charm.
Beating the surface of the earth,
Striking my stirrup,
Calling three times, blowing three times,
Whistling three times,
When I drew my brows together,
Clouds lashed down from the sky,
Enclosing the face of the earth.
Clouds smothered all from above.
Caught up in the rain,
Up into the sky flew
Many Chinese, shrieking.
Pines fell and mountains flew;
When the fierce storm swept through,
Lofty towering cliffs flew away.
Many Chinese raced toward home,
Running alongside their herds.
Many Chinese kept dying,
Unable to reach their homes,
Clinging to the gates.
Reciting spells, for the Chinese
I turned the summer day to winter.
The thinly clad Chinese
I made shiver in the cold,
Made them whimper like girls.
Cunningly working weather magic,
Day and night for thirty days,
The devastated Chinese—
Thus I slaughtered them.
Upon those daughter-selling Chinese,
I brought a dreadful slaughter.
Leading them before me,
Bringing my forty companions to strength,
I went to my mother.
My mother stood there crying out;
The tears flowing from both eyes
Streamed down mingled with blood.
“Do not begin a war with Beijing.
If you fight against Beijing,
Do not leave your mother alive.
Dig my grave wide before you go.
Breathe once against my breast,
Cry out, ‘Mother!’ in lament,
And bury me with your own hands.
If you flee to the Burut,
And I remain here plunged in trouble,
Will not the infidel Chinese
Gather together?
According to their own religion,
Will not the many Chinese put me in a chest?
Will they not hollow out a little niche,
Their monk babbling his prayers,
And bury me upright?
One day, after all, I shall die.
Rising from the chest, bewildered,
When the questioners come,
In that black night,
What answer shall I give?”
When my mother spoke like this,
I loosened Sarala's reins.
From the forest of the blue bend,
Sarala's mother,
A bay mare, I caught.
On a swift horse like Sarala,
I pulled the rear girth tight,
Shortened the crupper,
Clothed her in a shot-proof coat,
And mounted her on the bay mare.
The pure gold I had gathered,
I thought that I would scatter.
Renouncing Beijing,
I thought that I would flee.
When I said I would flee, she refused;
My mother stood in my way.
“Go to your father Azizkan,” she said.
“If he listens to you, bring your father
Into the Muslim faith,” she said.
“If your father becomes a Muslim,
Gather holy men before you go.
With your own hands, my foal,
Solemnize my marriage to your father.
For now, my foal,
Do not start talking of flight.
If you are going to flee, my foal,
Do not leave Aziz alive.
If Azizkan listens to you,
Bring him here as your father.
If your father will not listen,
Split him open upon his throne.
If you should kill your father,
Cut off his head and tie it to you,
And come back here to me.”
When I heard these words,
My wretched soul seethed with fury.
With both my eyes blazing,
Sarala beneath me
I handed over to brave Mazhik.
I wrapped myself in a rich outer coat.
Leaning on my battle-axe,
I ran into the palace,
Into its evenly planted garden;
I strode at a run
To my father's throne.
My father Azizkan
Was a mighty khan of the Chinese.
Surrounding my father
Were a thousand executioners.
At that time Azizkan
Had already grown old.
A child's affairs are a grievous burden;
What could my old father do?
He longed to see me once.
When I came to his throne,
He left it, looming tall.
“Almambet, my son, my camel-calf!”
He stood there pleading, arms outspread.
Running his nails through my hair,
Stroking my forehead and eyebrows,
My father stood there pleading,
Stroking the top of my head.
While my father stood like that,
The sacred word, the holy Quran,
I drew from inside my robe.
Holding it, I spoke:
“I have found a book called the Quran.
I have found the single name of God.
The words I have spoken—
Do you understand them, Father?
Saying, ‘In the name of God,’ into the pure faith
Will you enter, Father?
What we see today is gone tomorrow.
This wretched thing is a boundless lie;
The mischievous world keeps deceiving us.
The pure faith that is lawful—
Shall we begin it, Father?
The Chinese with their dark religion—
Shall we leave them, Father?
The pure faith of the Muslims—
Shall we uphold it, Father?
Come, Father, I beg you.
What shall we do staying here?
In the west there are the Burut;
Let us go and join them.”
Hearing this then,
As though stabbed in a gland,
My khan father sprang up.
My incorrigible father would not mend;
He would not stop roaring.
“My son, do not abandon these Chinese.
Do not set out on another path.
The Beijing we know is a good land for us.
Perish with your Burut!
The Chinese people are better than the Burut.
You are setting out on a crooked path.
Your treasury full of masses of gold—
To whom will you leave it, wretch?
O son, can one prosper by becoming Burut?
Would a Burut leave his people
And become Chinese among us?
Having a girdle twisted from hair,
When he reaches three years old,
Having his private parts cut—
Can such a person be a man?
Can one prosper by becoming Burut?
Almambet, my only child, born near my death,
The yellow willows I had planted—
To whom will you leave your willows?
Built from yellow gold—
To whom will you leave your house?
Your horses on the hills, six great hosts—
To whom will you leave your herds?
Do not cross over to a worthless place;
Do not tread an evil road!
Having said this, my father,
Coughing again and again,
The bronze he had made with his hands—
My father, tottering about,
Brought it lifted up.
Refusing to heed my words,
My father brought disaster upon himself.
I let out a shout,
Struck once with my left hand,
And his idol cast in bronze
I snatched from his hand.
Seizing his god,
I spoke these words:
“What you fashioned with your hands—
Can your bronze be God?
Trusting and believing in this,
Can a person live and prosper?
Very well, Father,
If it is your god, let it be,” I said.
“If this is truly God,
Let it not allow me out the door;
Let it suck me down from beneath you,” I said.
The idol in my hand,
Against a black stone as large as a cauldron,
I hurled and smashed.
Trampling it with my left foot,
I ground it into the earth.
I wanted to go, but my path would not open.
I wanted to strike my father,
But he bore the name of father;
My hand would not kill him.
My knees buckled to the ground.
Unable to raise my hand against my father,
I ran out through the door.
On the bay mare with the cow-like rump,
My mother was then sitting.
At her forearm, a keen blade
Was hanging at that moment.
When I came running out,
My mother spoke these words:
“Almambet, you were the son of Light;
My foal, I was the wife of Light.
One who does not squat to urinate,
Whose sleeves do not touch the ground—
Can an infidel be a father?
An infidel of a false religion—
Can calling him father bring any good?
That wretch whose faith is dark,
Who has not had his privates cut—
Can calling him father bring any good?
Go back, my foal,
My pure one, flesh of my body,
My white goshawk surpassing the female hawk.
Gouge out both his eyes;
Upon his throne, your father
Break and shatter into pieces;
Slaughter him in his palace.
If you do not do as I say,
If you do not obey me,
I shall not forgive you the milk of my breast.
In both worlds,
I shall estrange you from my Lord.
On the coming Day of Resurrection,
I shall trample and crush you underfoot.
Before the Seven Stars had set,
Before the dawn had fully broken,
Seven times I turned to nurse you—
‘Give me back my white milk!’ I shall say,
Shaming you before the people,
Holding you fast and not letting you go.”
My mother, dear to me,
Her whip with its black leather flap
She seized and swung.
“Go slaughter him at once,” she said.
“Plunge his head in blood,” she said,

My mother did an astonishing thing:
She split the skin of my neck.
Blood flowed out from the cut,

I pleaded with my father.
He would not yield to pleading;
Since he refused, he came to ruin.
If I tell it again, it is the same tale;
I shall tell it again, Manas.
Again he spoke of the Chinese;
My father stood with arms outspread.
That same bronze idol,
Once again, the wretched man
Brought it, looming tall.
“Father, the guilt is yours alone.
Even unto death, you would not heed
The words I spoke!
You would not do as I said.”
Saying this then, from his hand,
His god cast in bronze—
