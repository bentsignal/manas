Those without courage stood bewildered,
Spears bent in their hands,
They thrust without sparing themselves.
The wounded lay strewn everywhere,
And still more lay dead.
“If this goes on, the Buruts
Will bring my people to their end!
The Chinese and Manchus
They will slaughter, leaving no descendants!” he said.
Kongurbay brought his numbers to bear;
The people of ninety tribes swept in,
A flood that overturned mountains swept in,
The countless Chinese now arrived.
Almambet, Chubak and warrior Syrgak—
Could they scatter earth upon so many?—
Were now hard pressed indeed!
The heroes went on fighting,
Hemmed in by the many dead,
Their horses up to their knees
Were dyed with red blood.
When the Chinese came in greater force,
Then Syrgak the leopard,
Without pausing, his leather belt
He girded, crying, “I trust to fate!”
The mass of Chinese and Manchus
He halted by attacking once again.
While Syrgak held them off,
Chubak plunged among them.
Fighting for nine days,
Chubak came out stained with blood,
Wounded in ninety places.
When mountain-like Chubak emerged,
Having come to this campaign of death
From broad Talas,
The great host of Burut Muslims
Had fallen into torment.
Argynkhan’s Ajybai,
Whose mount was swift Kartkurong,
Now sixty years into its maturity,
Not letting the foe seize his horse’s lead,
Not yielding to a determined enemy,
Plunging into the campaign,
He too entered and fought.
When Ajybai went in,
See the reeds, see the flood,
See the Chinese people surging in!
See the cannon shot, swifter than clouds,
See the bullets striking without swerving!
For six days he plunged among them;
Ajybai came out stained with blood,
Wounded in sixty places.
After Ajybai,
Shouting, “I trust to fate!”
Calling the battle cry, “Manas!”
For seven days he plunged among them,
Wounded in seventy places;
Yrchy Uul came out covered in blood,
Stiff, unable even to sway.
Old Kyrgyl, leader of the forty,
A mighty elder, proud among his forty,
Were it not for his perverse temper,
An elder skilled among spearmen,
Plunged among them for two days,
Was wounded in many places,
His head a bucketful of blood,
The whole of his body
Stiff, unable to stir,
And returned from the battle.
Baabedin’s son,
Baychoro, born a warrior,
Making Aksur prance,
Sending every man crawling,
Plunged among the Chinese.
He met no fitting opponent,
But encountered the foot soldiers.
For six days he fought,
Battling without sparing himself;
Aksur fell and collapsed,
And upon Baychoro the foot soldiers
All swarmed together.
Separated from his horse,
He fell into their hands.
Seeing this, that same
Lean-haunched Sarala
Almambet sent wheeling;
One of the foot soldiers
He struck down as soon as he reached him.
Reaching him, Almambet took alongside
Aksur, the horse beneath him,
And brought him out at a gallop.
On the plain shrouded in haze,
Amid the army countless as ants,
Shouting, “Baychoro!”
Almambet kept halting.
Among the countless Chinese and Manchus,
Ulak Khan’s Syrgak
Had wrought a terrible slaughter.
Scooping him up like a little bird,
Warrior Syrgak took Baychoro,
Snatched him from the Chinese,
And bore him swiftly away.
Azizkhan’s Almambet
Brought Aksur, whom he was leading,
Round broadside to him;
Your champion, warrior Syrgak,
Mounted him safely on Aksur
And led him on his way.
Syrgak and Almambet, their names in accord,
Fearless men, born lions,
Unwearied in their struggles,
Untiring in their blows,
Would not let the swarming Chinese and many Manchus
Draw near them,
Would not let them reach the Muslims.
Shouting, “Islam!”
Slaughtering, destroying and scattering them,
They held the Chinese off.
When the Chinese pressed in harder,
Azizkhan’s Almambet
Roared in wrath,
Calling, “Come, Syrgak!
Great Beijing, which even holy Ali could not touch—
Saying we would fight it,
Having suffered so many wounds,
Unable to exchange shots with the enemy,
Shall we bleed away our lives?
