When Chyngteñir is born—
When he dons his broad coat,
When he takes the reins and mounts a horse,
Whoever provokes his anger
Chyngteñir will slaughter.
Ninety warriors will be his companions;
When your Kyrgyz rise surging against him,
They will be leveled with the black earth.”
“What you have said is fitting,”
“Your counsel is wise,”
Said great Kite when he heard this,
And throughout every land
Known to ear and seen by eye, he sent word.
To the many tribes of the Chinese nation,
He sent word among the people.
“May God spare us the sight!
The khan’s decree has come,” they said.
Dust rose and haze spread,
As orders drove the people onward;
He gathered the nation harshly,
And killed those whose death had come.
Women whose death had not yet come
Saw hell while still alive.
He had every mount harnessed,
And the entire population
Driven into Great Beijing;
Thus he assembled the nation by force.
He took Burul, the daughter of India,
And married her in lawful wedlock;
Only then did his spirit find peace.
His desire was fulfilled,
What he had sought came to him;
His grief was eased,
His troubled heart grew calm.
The spoken prophecy proved true:
In Beijing’s plane-tree garden,
Upon its blessed carpet,
By the ancient khan’s Zuu Well,
At the very bottom of the Zuu Well,
His destiny began its course.
Thus, to Kite,
She bore Chyngteñir.
“The words of Bilgich and Körgüch
Have come true,” they said;
“Heaven has granted us a peerless one.”
They slaughtered countless beasts for the feast,
And gave the child
The name Chyngteñir.
When Chyngteñir was born,
The sands scorched and burned,
As though hell itself had appeared.
In his right hand was black bread,
In his left hand yellow gold—
Thus Teñir descended, gripping them.
Those versed in omens read the signs,
And gave him their wholehearted attention.
The great diviner Ylangyr,
Uncle Ylangyr, saw him.
“No good will come of this,” he said,
“There has never been a warrior like him.
Not only the Kyrgyz—the whole world
And many nations will he lay waste;
He will drive and slaughter
All the eighteen thousand worlds.”
“Burkum himself has bestowed him,” they said,
For Burku was their god.
When such a child was born,
How could the Chinese fail to rejoice?
In their rejoicing, those Chinese
Surged in masses while dust
Flew upward to the sky.
Such was the spectacle they witnessed,
Such a clamor that the heavens resounded,
Such were the countless wonders.
“He shall become khan of Chinmachin,
This Chyngteñir himself,” they said,
“A fortress protecting the nation,
One whose reach will span the world;
A mighty gray-maned hero has come.”
They lost themselves in joy,
