Your people, ruined and distressed,
Pleaded, “We submit as your slaves.”
Falling captive and crying out,
Your great people stood there.
To the boy Koyonaaly
They offered their horses.
“I will not slaughter the people needlessly,” he said,
And yielded to their plea.
To all the great people
He swore he would not slaughter them.
His sworn father, Khan Manas,
Had given him a tent as a gift;
He pitched it upon the pass.
With collars of gold and sleeves of brass,
Double mail and lark's-eye links,
The bulletproof coats—
The warrior took them all off.
Pillowing his head upon the white saddle,
His head toward the qibla,
Praying to his Allah,
The bulletproof armor
He drew snugly over himself.
Your warrior fell asleep.
He was twelve years old.
Against that blazing plane tree,
Janaaly the swine bore enmity.
“Beside the rib bone,” he said,
“Right at the head of the heart.”
With a flick, his bullet flew.
The only child Koyonaaly
Was struck by the bullet of fate.
Falling in his flowering days,
He perished while still young.
Then his father Kokboru
Dropped straight from Kuukazyk's saddle,
Doubled over, his legs drawn together.
Having gone to Talas and returned,
Bereft of the one who would cherish her,
His mother Külnaary
Sank down there.
Tears mingled with blood
She poured out there.
Calling, “Manas, favored by Khizr,”
Struck from the ridge-side,
His little Koyonaaly
He lost to that Janaaly.
By the people of Chambyl-Bel,
Struck from the right side,
The only child, twelve years old,
Was taken midway on the road.
Having gone to Manas and returned,
From his kingly child,
He was parted midway.
Looking, he could not see him;
The boy's body could not be found.
“How can I reach my land?
My only child, here—
How can I leave without burying him?” he said.
Kokboru wept bitterly
And was overcome with anguish.
Külnaary came behind him;
She was Khan Karacha's daughter.
She was the most skillful of women;
Consider her: she was a seer.
She was a handmaid rich in wisdom.
Külnaary, Boru's wife,
Whose wisdom surpassed others':
“To your friend, Sultan Manas,
Did you not consent, my sovereign?
Your only light from the nest—
When he asked for your light,
To your friend Manas, your lion,
Did you not give him, my sovereign?
Will grieving for your only child bring good?
O warrior, can grief be repayment?
You gave your only child as a gift;
Can your promise become two?
Your falcon, your only light,
Has attained his desire.
This is God's decree:
Manas, your confidant and dear friend,
Has taken the falcon with him as a companion.”
So, grieving thus,
Kokboru went back to his people, they say.
With anguish lodged within him,
“My only child Koyonaaly has died,” he said.
To Khan Manas's funeral observances,
Kokboru did not come, they say.
“Because Kokboru has not come,”
Toshtuk did not come, they say.
Since Toshtuk did not come,
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan,
That khan did not come, they say.
Those who had made their promise firm,
All the khans—
And so, all of them failed
To come, they say!
