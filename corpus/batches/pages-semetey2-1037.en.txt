He had battered against crag and stone,
A clattering brute he was.
Man, like a black camel in winter,
A snarling brute he was.
Once enraged, he spared no living soul,
Once he drank, he could not sate himself with blood.
The swaggering wretch
Never abandoned his mischief—
Ümötöy, Köksö’s son.
Ümötöy stood there working himself into a fury,
As though he would sweep all before him.
To see how fiercely he raged,
He seemed ready to end it then and there,
And carry off the Kyrgyz of Sary-Arka
As plunder.
He made his weapons ready,
And fastened on his battle gear,
Like a cloud returning with rain,
His wrath spread across his face.
He looked ready to attack,
Ready to grapple in combat,
Stepping out from the massed host,
The hair atop his head standing on end,
The hair on his face bristling,
His eyes dimmed and shimmering.
When you looked at his jaw and head,
They ground like a millstone.
Father, his strength was extraordinary,
And his valor was no small thing.
To see him in his frenzy,
He seemed able to arrive before an arrow.
If you set a man alone before him,
He seemed certain to catch and carry him off.
He was noble, and he was brave,
A truly grim, dark warrior.
The poor son of his father
Stood firmly by his word.
He had gathered an army from the Kazakhs,
And prepared himself well;
The Kazakhs of Sary-Arka
Called him Ümötöy the strongman.
He mounted the ridge with purpose,
Swaying there in proud confidence,
And stood face-to-face,
Ready to attack Choro.
God’s sun in heaven grew overcast,
And Külchoro, guarding his honor,
Was beset by sixty anxious thoughts.
His heart was torn and distracted:
“If I stiffen in anger
And thrust, my spear will surely reach him;
Ümötöy, son of Khan Köksö—
Alas, he will be crushed to dust.
Then they will say, ‘You slew your own kinsman,
You killed him for nothing.’
Renouncing the milk with which she nursed me,
Before Almighty God
And openly disowning me,
My dear mother Kanykey may say,
‘May my breast milk strike you down!’
Alas, my dear mother Kanykey—
In her anger, the unfortunate woman
Might suddenly curse me.
My dear elder Semetey
May say, ‘You attacked a foolish youngster;
What have you become, little brother?’
Man, may he not put me to shame!
He may ask, ‘How could you seize and slaughter
Our maternal cousin, our guest?
You truly are a brave man,
But what have you done
To a guest who came among your people?
He is no Chinese and no enemy,
My formidable lion Külchoro;
This deed of yours is not sane!’
Saying this, my elder
May strike me to the heart with cutting words,
And my poor soul will truly burn—
