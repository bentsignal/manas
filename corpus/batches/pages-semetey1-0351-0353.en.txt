He must have come this way;
He must have known for certain you were coming.
Skirting the foothills of Uch-Koshoi,
He must have been hunting marmots.
Against the wild sheep on the hills
He must have chased and slaughtered them.
The deer in the thickets
He must have stalked and torn apart.
This year Kumaik
Must have chased wild asses over the plain,
And torn apart those he met,
Grown fat and recovered his vigor.
Roaming the hills without rest,
He must now be wandering.
“If what I have said is true,
My orphan, left by the magnanimous hero,
Is about to come,” he thinks.
“My only one, left by the magnanimous hero,
When will Semetey come?” he thinks.
He must now be waiting to meet you.
When Kumaik sees you,
Though he would speak, he has no tongue;
Kumaik will break into howling.
When he comes close to you,
He will stop and crouch, howling.
He is wondrous, unlike other beasts;
His tears will pour down.
He has a voice like a lion.
Kumaik, left by your father Manas,
Has six different kinds of fur.
His hindquarters are narrow, his chest broad;
No hunter is like Kumaik.
He never drew back from a tiger;
Your father's Kumaik
Never retreated from a bear.
He destroyed whatever he bit,
Delighting his master and making him laugh.
That is the mighty dog you unleash;
If you bring him, my foal,
He is the treasure left by your father, magnanimous Manas.
Tie him by the collar and bring him along;
Bring him to your bereaved mother.
My only one, that precious Kumaik,
Let me see him with my own eyes.
My long-awaited only child,
Bring him to me.
My foal, leaving him behind would bring disgrace;
Kumaik, left by your father, is entrusted to you!
Trotting from the foothills, crying out,
Kumaik will come crouching,
His tears welling up,
Kumaik will come shedding them.
Circling around you,
He will sniff at your eyes.
Kumaik, left by your father,
Will sniff your body.
A sage beyond other beasts,
If you cause Kumaik misgivings:
“His offspring will never prosper rightly,” he may say.
“My noble falcon, wolf-maned Manas—
He is not exactly like him,” he may say.
Let him not cross the little pass,
Nor bite you in vexation!
When you meet Kumaik
And embrace him,
When he faithfully serves you,
If you look to your right,
A dock-tailed dun mare will appear.
Catch the dun mare and slaughter her;
She has rib fat a handspan thick.
Leave Kumaik beside her.
Such is the story of Kumaik.
Let me leave that here;
Listen once more, my son,
Let me begin with what comes next:
As I sit speaking and lamenting,
My words to God are a lament.
On your wolf-maned khan father's hand
Was one whose flights delighted the soul:
His falcon Akshumkar.
When your father Manas died,
For six days no sun shone;
Dark night fell upon the day.
When your father Manas died,
The people did not stand indifferent.
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan,
Valiant Toshtuk, son of Eleman,
Zhamgyrchy of the Eshtek—
All the mighty heroes wept.
For your father, gone to Judgment,
The Muslims did not stand indifferent.
All was well with him;
Your father was a lion upheld by his patron.
A soul like your father, the one God
Will never create at all.
When that father of yours died,
When this head of mine met turmoil,
Breaking his golden jesses,
Akshumkar tore them apart.
Vanishing from the world,
Akshumkar fled.
Without meeting a single person,
Without anyone knowing,
Akshumkar disappeared.
He must have ranged across the earth,
Akshumkar, left by your father.
To Jerusalem and Baghdad
He must have gone for five years.
In Mecca and Medina
He must have spent seven years.
Those five years and these seven
Must have made twelve full years.
This year Akshumkar
Must have taken thought of you.
“King Manas, the wolf-maned—
If I fail to honor his spirit,
To the orphan left by Manas,
If I do not give one of my offspring,
He will know my baseness,” he thought.
“On the day of the Last Judgment,
He will turn me into a black donkey and ride me,” he thought.
“He will grant my wish,” he thought.
“The orphan I watch for will come,” he thought.
To the hollow of a winding mountain,
To a flat where no saker lays,
“If what I say is true,”
My orphan, he must have gone and built a nest.
At Chatyr-Kol, at the head of the Naryn,
With the pale kumpai falcon living there,
He must have joined bodies,
And mated.
Lying in wait in the foothills, upon birds and beasts
He must have wrought slaughter this year.
He must have driven and pursued swans.
As the snow melted into scattered patches,
The falcon must have laid its offspring.
Waiting in the foothills, Akshumkar
Must have known you would come this year.
From the river with sandy channels,
Akshumkar, left by your father,
Must have carried swans.
From the cliffs above the ravines
He must have carried geese.
He must have scattered his breast feathers,
Brooded for thirty-two days,
And hatched his eggs.
His offspring must have broken their shells aright.
By now his young
Must have become mottled black.
If you bring Akshumkar,
It is the time to take him.
When you arrive, his young
Will be at the pinfeather stage.
His prowess is well known to the people—
Your father, gone to Judgment,
Manas, left him as an inheritance.
That is the bird to set upon your hand,
That is the treasure left by your father Manas.
Let me describe Akshumkar:
His talons are iron, his jess plate copper;
His girth is two ells.
His eyes are black, his legs blue-speckled.
He never recoils from what he sees;
He needs no more than a bucketful of meat.
He never draws back from striving;
He needs no more than a platterful of meat.
He does not fold his tail and swoop;
He lets no swan fly from the lake.
He does not fold his wings and swoop;
Akshumkar lets no goose fly from the lake.
Bring it in a feeding bag;
Without letting his feathers grow foul,
I shall take good care of him.
Bring him to your mother!
Such is the story of Akshumkar.
Let me leave that here;
Listen once more, my son,
Let me begin with what lies beyond:
Beyond is Sary-Ozon,
Along the broad Talas;
Your father, gone to the other world,
Made that place his home.
Along that Talas
Stands your mother Kanykei's white palace,
Its doors iron and solid tin.
Though your mother was a woman,
Her splendor was considerable.
While your dear father was alive,
To your mother's fortress
No enemy made his way;
No one dared approach.
Its guards were beyond counting,
Its watchmen more numerous still.
Human noses and ears
They wore as amulets.
Before the door
There were seven jinn and spirits,
Forty swordsmen,
And sixty spearmen.
While your khan father lived,
I, your unfortunate, wretched mother,
Had so many guards.
My fortress must have fallen and faded;
The white palace where guards stood
Must have crumbled into a cattle enclosure.
All the plane trees must have been felled;
God must truly have struck it.
My orphan, its true sacred power cut off,
There is no croaking raven,
No cawing crow,
No living soul at all;
Talas must lie deserted.
Abyke and Kobosh, the six villains,
Must have fought without tiring,
Against Bakai, left by your father Manas,
And made sport of him.
Cutting off heads and spilling blood,
There must have been a terrible slaughter.
Noble Bakai, the renowned,
Has a spirit unlike any other.
Going out to exchange shots, valiant Bakai
Must have slain sixty of Abyke's men,
And become bloodstained.
With five hundred households in his charge,
Bakai must have gone and settled in a stronghold.
Building a fort where he headed,
Bakai must have blocked the road and held firm,
And gained an ill name.
Without yielding to the six villains,
Your noble uncle Bakai
Must have attained his aim.
My uncle, King Bakai's—
