I wrenched myself free once more.
When I tried to go, he barred my way.
‘After all, he is my father,’ I thought;
My hand would not strike him head-on.
The keen steel sword at my side
I seized by its hilt,
Drew it from its sheath,
And swung it in a slanting stroke.
Holding my father firmly,
From atop the golden throne
I struck him backward.
His eyes glittered like stars;
His dear head, large as a basin,
From atop the golden throne
Fell with a clatter.
The words he stammered
Were lost in his babbling.
I drenched his golden throne in blood.
‘He will not heed my words,
He has refused to become a Muslim,’ I thought.
Though an unbeliever, he was my father:
I cut off his head and killed him.
My very soul was aflame.
Listen, Manas, my leopard!
My father's thousand champions,
Not letting a single one live,
There within that palace,
I cut off all their heads.
Lifting my father's head,
Into my mother's presence
I ran at full speed.
When she saw my father's head,
A thousand times forgiving the milk of her breast,
My mother then said this:
‘Never turn back toward Beijing,
My leopard, my lion, my noble falcon!
Do not lose your fragile life!
If you go, you will not escape unharmed;
You will not return alive from Beijing.
The place where your shirt was taken off and washed,
Where your unfortunate mother bore you,
Where the blood of your umbilical cord fell;
Where the lion gave you protection,
Where you came down crying out,
A great poplar, broad as a hut,
A plane tree sprang up and stood.
That plane tree, that standing poplar—
“If you fight against True Beijing,
I shall not leave my Almam alive;
I shall keep him here,” it says.’
While my mother spoke like this,
A curse on being alone!
Among the teeming Chinese multitudes,
In Beijing of Kakanchyn,
Counting my mother too,
With all our struggles, forty-three souls—
We did not cease to talk and take counsel.
Weighing things this way and that,
Forty-three people gathered,
And from the land of Great Beijing
We thought to depart.
While we were taking counsel thus,
Preparing to leave,
Is what I tell you not a lament?
Karykan, the khan of Beijing—
Even now I marvel:
Could he have possessed such knowledge?
He learned it from his sacred book,
And saw it in the divining shoulder blade.
Having learned it, Karykan
Shouted from his throne,
Summoned his champions:
‘A fire burns within my core,
An enemy rises from my own midst!
I wondered who it was, and here he is.
I thought none would be born, yet one was born!
As I wondered who the enemy was,
It was Almambet, Azizkhan's
Son of filthy piss.
I tell you all, champions:
Do not let him cross the Altai ridge;
To that great troublemaker in Talas,
Khan Manas, do not let him flee!
If you let him escape to Manas,
Hear what I tell you:
Within five or six years,
Coming to bring affliction,
The hero Manas in Beijing—
You will see him wage war!
If Manas comes to Beijing,
You Chinese multitudes had better
Die now, before that day!
Almambet, to the far side,
Without seizing him here—
If you Chinese let him get away,
Do not come near me!
Kongurbay and the governors at your head,
Do not appear before my eyes!
If Almambet falls into your hands,
Bind both his hands and bring him;
Without killing that pig,
Drive him alive to Beijing.
Bound, trussed, and shackled,
Torment him along the way.
Whoever brings Almambet bound,
I shall make a man of splendor.
Here in this very Beijing,
I shall set a golden crown upon him,
Seat him upon the golden throne,
And over the Chinese peoples of forty khans
Give him rule, allowing none to stir;
I shall make him khan of Kakanchyn!’
Saying this, he readied the fierce warrior,
Prepared his arms and equipment.
The Chinese multitudes gathered together;
They mounted him on Algara
And sent Kongurbay forth.
After him, Karykan,
Saying, ‘Fight without tiring,’
Sent Ezkara.
Then, equipping him in turn,
Karykan sent
His Kalmyk, like a lama,
His champion called Great Joloy.
From the flank, Borukoz
Also came up,
