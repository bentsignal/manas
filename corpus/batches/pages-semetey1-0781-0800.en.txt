At the sight of the falcon,
Were frightened out of their senses.
None would raise its head;
Each thrust its head into the water.
The rush of wings was heard;
They tumbled this way and that,
Their heads buried in the waterweed,
Those living creatures remained there.
Your valiant orphan received him;
What had broken was joined,
What had scattered was gathered.
What had been lost was found,
His honor was restored.
When he received Akshumkar,
All the valleys and mountains
Seemed to be running;
His father, gone to the other world,
Seemed to have risen from the dead.
His fierce elder Bakai,
The old man who had seen much—
See his readiness!
Of your sage elder Bakai,
See the resourcefulness!
Without many words, without speaking,
The old caravan-leader Bakai,
The sage who had seen so much,
From his belt-pouch
Your elder took a cord.
It was a cord plaited of silk;
He fastened it to Akshumkar.
While Bakai was doing this,
That brave son of Manas said:
‘Since God Most High has granted this,
Since Akshumkar has come from his hidden realm,
Bringing his young one,
How can we hold anything back?
My father, who went to the other world—
Dedicating it to his spirit,
Let us dedicate Aktulpar as a sacrifice.’
When he mentioned Aktulpar,
As though pierced by an awl,
Brave Bakai sprang up:
‘Alas, orphan, heed me!
If you do not, it will be a disgrace.
Out of fear, your grandfather
Entrusted Aktulpar to you.
If we slaughter the horse entrusted to us,
If we put him to death,
How can we prosper?
We shall be a laughingstock to the people.
Your grandfather Temirkan
Bears the name of father;
Once we slaughter his horse,
He may curse us.
Once you slaughter his horse,
It will be a great shame.’
Then Bakai said:
‘Let me tell the story of this Aktulpar.
Your grandfather Temirkan,
Who ruled Bukhara,
Was a famous, splendid khan,
But he was miserly with livestock.
Born to your elder,
Beginning with your mother Kanykei,
There were twelve daughters.
Among all the people
He had spread his offspring.
The eldest of his daughters,
With a waist like a sheath, a face like the moon,
The renowned beauty Akerkech
Was Kökchö's wife.
She was the finest of women,
Temirkan's firstborn daughter.
Bringing eighty breeding she-camels,
Akerkech once pleaded in distress
And asked for Aktulpar.
He would not give Aktulpar;
He would not consent to her request.
Because of Aktulpar,
That daughter went away and became estranged.
Burulkan, whom Muzburchak married,
Was also his daughter.
Because of what happened,
Over the matter of Aktulpar,
That daughter too went away and became estranged.
Ormonbek of Tekeche,
And Oiumkan, whom Ormonbek married—
Ormonbek was his son-in-law.
Temirkan had thus stood in the way;
Because of Aktulpar,
That son-in-law became estranged.
Stop, orphan, hear my words.
Because of Aktulpar,
Against that very Temirkan
Twelve sons-in-law made war.
They parted in resentment.
They died without looking upon one another.
When your khan father was alive,
When prosperity endured,
We heard a rumor from the people:
The golden-forelocked, fiery-belted
Only son of Khan Azozil,
For the sake of his faith,
Had fled his people and wandered;
He had disputed the khanship in Beijing,
They had fiercely pitted strength against strength.
Many Chinese giants had died,
They had shed blood.
To Kakan Chin’s Beijing,
We heard he had brought turmoil.
Having strayed thus from his people,
We quarreled fiercely:
“There is no one who understands this of mine,
Why should I remain here?” he said.
“I shall go among the Burut,” he said.
He killed his own father,
His mother passed away a martyr.
We heard that, turning away from China,
He entered Khangai.
Unable to gain a foothold in Khangai,
He slaughtered and destroyed its khans;
We heard he came out again.
From there, driven onward,
We heard he came to Altai.
Unable to find room in Altai,
He killed Khan Artyk;
We heard he came to Sary-Arka.
When he reached Kökchö in Sary-Arka
And settled beside his camp,
Because of what had happened,
Because of Akerkech,
Kökchö, the son of Aidarkan,
We heard he beat him and departed.
Disappointed in Kökchö,
He moved to Talas,
Settled there and became a friend
To your father, Sultan Manas.
Your lion father Manas—
When God granted his desire,
When Almam came from China,
Ajybai and Khan Bakai were there,
All the wise warriors were there.
To the lion who has come from afar,
If we do not allot a horse,
God will punish us severely,” we said,
“Everyone will gossip,” we said.
We pondered and turned the matter over,
Loaded wealth upon eighty camels,
And said, “Go and ask for Aktulpar.”
Your mother Kanykei
We then sent to visit her parents.
At that time my dear child Kanykei,
The loveliest of the dark-complexioned,
The most radiant of the Muslims,
Was a young wife of seventeen,
And a servant beloved by God,
The youngest of twelve daughters.
Her hair was braided down her back;
More than all sixty sons
He loved her.
Kanykei, the lord's wife,
Went to visit her parents.
The wealth of eighty camels,
Temirkan gathered it all
And placed it in his treasury.
He would not agree to her request,
Would not give Aktulpar.
Leaving her to whine like a howling dog,
He sent your mother away empty-handed and dejected.
Your fierce father, Khan Manas, said:
“That wretched shrew will never mend!
When she is sent to visit her parents,
She is not worth one of her father's yearlings!”
Then he brought great sorrow upon her,
He would not lie in her embrace.
She camped along the river,
And was a neglected wife for six months.
Your mother Kanykei has her story:
She ceased calling Temir father
And became estranged from him.
Do not spoil what can be settled.
If you are a worthy person, my orphan,
Do not destroy Aktulpar.
He was a khan struck by God,
A man miserly with livestock.
Please heed what I say,
My dear orphan;
Take Aktulpar back
And return him safe and sound.
Neither drawing close nor making enemies,
Bring this joy to him.
Your grandfather’s blessing—
Ask well and receive it.
Go safely and return soon;
Go without delay and return happy.
The swift Narbuudan is beneath you;
Rein him in on stony ground.
To your poor widowed mother,
Say, “I have seen the accomplished Khan Bakai,”
And convey my heartfelt greetings.
Your mother Kanykei bears a grievance:
“Your grandfather's face,
I shall not see while alive, even if I die,”
She once made that vow.
Once, poor Kanykei,
Showing the depths of her misery,
Set ninety-year-old Chiyyrdy
Upon her shoulders,
Bound her firmly against her back.
“A gift left by the warrior,” she said,
And in the fold of her overskirt
Bound him securely in her care.
When her great wealth had scattered,
When she fled Talas on foot,
When she fled, finding no place,
Never resting two days
In any one place as she fled,
Without overshoes, in felt stockings,
The poor creature wept in sorrow.
Without boots, in felt stockings,
The wretched creature wept aloud.
She set her face toward Bukhara,
Carrying both of them alike.
The old woman coughed upon her back;
Your mother carried the old woman and the child,
Grieving as she went.
When I, Bakai, learned she had gone,
I sent Kumaik racing after her trail.
At the reed-grown lake
I caught up with her alone.
I gave her two fat horses,
I gave her two leather bottles of milk.
I reached her and slaughtered for her
A pale, white-blazed wether.
Your poor mother will never forget
What happened then.
I clothed her in a robe of costly fabric,
Mounted them on two cloud-like horses,
Wept and sobbed as I made things ready,
Told her to go to Bukhara.
My heart was torn,
The tears poured from my eyes.
As I stood in great sorrow,
Kanykei spoke a word,
Then my dear child Kanykei,
Parted from Khan Manas,
Trembled beneath her troubles.
Her large, dark head,
Her bloodied face scratched,
Her dark hair loose,
The grief within her had not eased.
It was thirty days since he died,
Not yet time for his fortieth-day feast.
My dear child Kanykei said:
“I shall die before my appointed death.
With my large, dark head,
How can I face my elder?” she said.
Her voice reached God;
She fell face down.
My child Kanykei said this,
Told of a horror unknown to others:
“My dear elder!
How shall we attain our desire?
I left Bukhara weeping.
Because of Aktulpar
I renounced him as a father.
I no longer called it a blessing.
That sickle-nosed man
I no longer called father.
How can I go to my father?
A widow, standing so proudly,
How can I look him in the face?”
Kanykei stood and said:
“Why should I go to Bukhara?
Sage elder, give me your blessing;
Let me carry my orphan
And go into Kokand.
When black-hearted Kanykei
Lay there saying this,
When she plunged us all into torment,
I turned Köktulpar's head,
Beat my own head,
And stood weeping aloud.
The beard upon my chin
I plucked out, leaving not a hair.
I struck my own head
And made this spectacle.

My wretched child, you will not mend;
You will not abandon your fierceness.
If you go to Kokand, they will do no good;
They will not leave my orphan unharmed.
When a woman's husband dies,
Her own family should be close to her.
Make your way toward Temirkan,” I said,
And hastily sent her on her way.
Do not blunt your steel;
Do not destroy your white steed.
In the city of Bukhara,
My dear orphan,
It would be a disgrace to linger,’
He said, telling him this,
Making many things known,

On his way toward Bukhara.
Your orphan, the warrior's son,
When he had set out from Bukhara—
‘Kanykei, grown old,
And Chiyyrdy, aged,
Opened their palms and spread their hands.
“O God!” they cried, spreading them wide,
Praying and weeping much.
Both my mothers wept,
Wept, “Come back without delay.”
My poor mother Chiyyrdy,
An elder of ninety-five!
The saint who bore my father Manas
Had raised a piercing cry.
She had counted the days,
Written down the days,
Bound the time with a limit:
“Ten days to go there,
Ten days to stay among the people,
Ten days to return,
Three periods—thirty days.
If you do not return in thirty days,”
Those were my mother's words,
My poor mother said, “Before my appointed death,
I shall die.”
Hunched like a kneeling camel,
My mother, bowed down,
Let her tears stream,
My mother poured them out.
If I am two days late,
Will an untimely death take her?
Will she die in sorrow?
Her appointed time is thirty days.
Will my mother die,
And bring calamity upon us?’
He traveled in haste,
Covered much ground, sparing no horse.
Narbuudan beneath him,
When the orphan boy mounted,
Taibuurul that day—
I do not know; if it is true—
That day was in his prime.
When his sacred mane was grasped,
He was ready to stretch out
Upon the long road,
Ready to speed like a winged creature
And fly.
For twelve years
He had grazed at liberty.
That Semetey would come,
Though an animal, a living creature,
He had known that year.
The armful of tail
Of that roan horse you have seen
Streamed out strand by strand.
When the poor boy mounted,
My precious Taibuurul
Brightened altogether.
Now your orphan, Manas's son,
Had grown to maturity as an orphan,
And thus joined his people.
He had grown up and become a warrior,
The equal of a warrior's son.
Everyone who saw him was moved:
The departed, fierce Manas—
He looked exactly like him.
Khan Manas's Akolpok
The orphan boy now put on.
With one set of nine buttons,
From mountain-like Manas,
The armor left as an inheritance,
He fastened its buttons.
Its ties were one set of nine;
He fastened its ties.
Rejoicing, ‘O God!’
He put on Akolpok.
The gray belt-pouch left by Manas
He girded and fastened at his waist.
The young lord, the leopard,
His father who had died long ago—
He had become like Manas.
Zulpukor, left by his father Manas,
The precious lion's son,
Rejoicing under Khyzyr's blessing,
Dressed in his father Manas's likeness,
The inherited Zulpukor
He hung upon his left side.

‘Zulpukor, left by the bloodthirsty hero,
If I flee an enemy I face,
May God strike me down!
If you slip from where I strike,
May God strike you down!
I shall spread your fame among the people.
When you meet the enemy in combat,
I shall drench you in red blood!’
He hung his sword in its place.
A mirror upon the shoulder when slung,
When turned against a fighting enemy,
The comfort of this mortal life,
It was a possession left by his father Manas.
His father's Akkelte
He hung upon his shoulder.
Hurrying in haste, Semetey
Now set off toward Bukhara.

He shone in all his beauty.
The fierce hero's Akshumkar, big as a leather pail,
Gleamed upon his right hand.
The sight of him moved the heart;
The young son of Manas
Showed his warrior's bearing.
Kumaik, left by Manas,

No man could look upon his face.
Against the orphan's strength

With trust in fate, if Allah helped him,
Though the Chinese came in throngs,
None could endure the boy's fury;

The sight of him moved the heart.

He appeared like Manas.
As great as a mountain,
Your orphan, Manas's son,
Everyone who faced him,
He seemed able to chew up and eat alive.

He urged his horse toward Bukhara,
Your orphan, the son of Manas,
Appearing like a lion.

Passed over knolls and hollows,
Crossed hill after hill,
Traveled the rugged passes.
He traversed steep, deep ravines;
The leopard, Manas's son,
Crossed the rounded mountain heights,
Around the hillock-strewn passes,
Winding on with determination,
To the spur of the mountain's end,
To the jutting headland,
He had just come and gained a footing,
Aktulpar, whom he had been leading,
Your accomplished young lord

The horse Buurul beneath him,

Do not ask how the hero fared!
When pits and mounds appeared,
Let me tell you of his horse:
His back bent like a trap;

The brave orphan, Manas's son,
Rejoiced in delight.
Then the boy thought:
‘Today I have mounted him for the first time.
This creature, the horse Buurul,
Is truly a noble steed.
Today his noble nature has been revealed.
The dark earth his hooves strike
Splits apart on every side.
If he travels a full six months,
His noble nature will show.’
When Semetey looked alongside,
Where the hoof struck squarely,
A hollow opened like a hearth pit.
Where the hoof scraped in passing,
A mark was gouged and cut.
The ground where the creature stepped flew apart.
Clods as large as cauldrons
Whistled above your lord's head,
Racing against flying birds.
He traveled far upon his journey,
Covered much ground, sparing no horse.
The white reins plaited in rounded knots
He gripped tightly with both hands,
Making him strain as though a husband had died,
Struggling against the steed's mouth.
He traveled toward Bukhara,
Covered much ground, sparing no horse.
Wide Ak-Chii of great Bukhara—
That was Khan Temir's land.
For a noble steed traveling without rest,
It was a journey of six days.
When he reached Ak-Chii,
He had traveled without sparing him,
Running swiftly with a thunderous rush.
Dark mud and flowing sweat
Streamed down below the stirrup.
The horse Buurul beneath him
Sped ever faster as he went,
His strength kept growing.
Khan Temir's Aktulpar,
As though stricken with a twisting sickness,
The creature staggered.
Your boy's horse Buurul—
No beast could match his walk,
No wind could match his trot.
He rushed like a cloud,
Roared more fiercely than the wind,
Tossed his forelock toward the sky.
As the horse Buurul went onward,
He plunged his hooves into the ground.
Where he stepped, the dark stones
Scattered like ground meal.
Khan Temir's Aktulpar
That day could not match Buurul:
His walk could not match his walk,
His trot could not match his trot.
Aktulpar was left behind.
When Aktulpar could go no farther,
He firmly drew in his reins.
When he came into view,
When unseen, he became drifting blue smoke,
Rushing as though he would fly.

If your orphan, Manas's son,
Did not arrive that day,
The anguish of the Day of Judgment
He would have to face.

Your mighty mother Chiyyrdy
Was about to die.
Taking no food, eating nothing,
She had brought this torment upon them.
‘When will my orphan come?’
She lay there wailing.
‘God will grant us the orphan;
If he is alive, he will come,’
Your dear mother Kanykei said,
Enduring this anguish.
The bowed old woman Chiyyrdy
Had reached ninety-five years.
Sorrowful Kanykei,
Your dear, unfortunate sister-in-law,
Had only to look at old Chiyyrdy
For her hunger to be satisfied.
The support left by her Khan Manas—
The sight of her satisfied her hunger;
She had no one else like her.
‘Let her be my refuge,’ she said.
Your poor sister-in-law Kanykei
Had set her upon her shoulders
And brought her away.
Then Chiyyrdy,
Saying, ‘The orphan has gone to his people,’
Was torn with worry.
Tears poured from her eyes,
Both her eyes festered,
Her whole body trembled.
Not in the grave, if one called her dead,
The elder of ninety-five
Lay huddled there.
Unable to rise, the old lady
Had sunk down.
If that Semetey did not come,
‘Why should I go on living
In this world?’ she said.
Stubbornly, she resolved
To endure hunger.
From this false world
She resolved to die.
‘When will the orphan come?
When will I see his face?’ she said.
Refusing to listen, hunched over,
She lay huddled there.
‘When will the orphan come?’ she said,
And sank down.

While she brought this torment,
Your dear sister-in-law Kanykei,
Her knees bending to the ground,

Kanykei hoped

And, if she saw him, give the news
To Chiyyrdy.
