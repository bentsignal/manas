He had a ruby split,
And set it in the glass.
He worked long and carefully to make it right,
Adjusted for a six-month journey's reach.
He had jewels set around its handle.
“Your payment remains with me,” he said,
And had horses driven over as payment.
Having come to settle in Talas,
Through his craftsmanship Bolokbai
Had become a companion of Manas.
Made by that esteemed and gifted man,
The great spyglass he took in hand.
He turned its ring,
Fixed his gaze intently,
Turned its screw,
Brought it round to his eye,
And held it there to look.
He directed his gaze toward Talas,
And everything happening in Talas
He saw in full.
“An ember has sprung from my own hearth!
While I am away at Beijing,
Over the many people of Talas
Abyke and Kobosh have made themselves lords.
Mounting a hill, they swagger:
‘Now Manas is dead!
From Beijing, whence none return,
How could Manas ever come back?’
They climb the hill and strut,
Shouting toward the people.
Abyke and Kobosh, those two scoundrels,
Raise the battle cry ‘Kobosh!’
They have run wild.
On the Argyn and Kyrgyz of Talas
They have imposed a merciless levy:
‘Give seventy camel-loads of tea!
Give seven thousand yearlings suckled by barren mares!’
Unable to bear their taxes,
The Argyn have moved away.
‘Alas, while the blood-shedding hero was here,
Without giving even a cup of tea,
Without giving a single storm-starved yearling,
We used to lie at ease,
Living splendidly in broad Talas.
Mare's milk by day, maidens by night,
Playing the circle game of striking and dodging,
Weaned lambs and their tasty heads
Bubbling away at a rolling boil—
So we used to live in Talas.
Since the hero Manas died,
Moonless, as long as a year—
Such is the night!
When power falls to a scoundrel,
Such is life!’ they say.
The Kyrgyz and Nogoy have joined together,
And they too have fled elsewhere.
Those men have made havoc,
Plundered the goods,
Broken into the treasury, sprawling at ease.
For the poor, the destitute, the needy
They have shown no regard in their destruction.
They have taken fine furs and otter,
Divided them down the middle,
Without fearing the evil of such waste,
And used them for their trouser legs.
Lolling like slaves whose master has died,
They have made themselves sultans.
Then dear, fierce Kanykei,
Not twisting her rope-like hair,
Unable to stand there screaming in grief,
From her dear quail-like eyes
Tears fell in separate streams;
From her blackcurrant-dark eyes
Tears flowed a whip's length.
‘When affairs go awry,
Such is the night!
When the dogs' turn comes,
Such is life!’ she said.
Her long black braids
She bound firmly upon her crown.
An old fur cap left at the rear camp
She pulled down over her head.
On a black, white-blazed courser,
Kanykei in her haste
Leapt up bareback.
Taking a horse-catching pole in hand,
She charged the six scoundrels.
Riding the black, white-blazed courser,
Making it prance,
All those wicked men
She struck down and sent crawling.
She turned her horse's head,
And all the wicked men
She drove into Talas.
She rode down from Keng-Kol.
All the goods they had plundered
She gathered up completely.
The gold they had pillaged,
Kanykei gathered that too
And put it back in its place.
Of the saddled courser,
She let no sign of its fullness show;
Of mountain-like Manas,
She let no sign of his absence show.
Raising her head high as a banner,
Making herself, a woman, into a warrior—
When I left Talas,
Abyke brought disorder.
Though a woman, Kanykei
Over this very Talas
Has taken command.”
After seeing this,
He rumbled in fury.
Your blood-shedding leopard Manas
Stood watching, speaking in a low voice:
“After I have passed away,
You will scatter what you gathered;
You will flee to your father Karakan.
My poor one, utterly ruined!
When my eyes close,
Left without support, Kanykei,
You will suffer affliction;
You will flee toward Bukhara.
Ill fortune is written on your brow;
A hard life lies ahead of you,
