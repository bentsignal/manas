I passed the time weeping and weeping.
King Temir in Bukhara,
Vowing never to see his face till death,
I went away weeping and weeping.
That hook-nosed black Sart,
That scowling old wretch,
I renounced him as my father.
From the city of the six clans,
I went away weeping and weeping.
Vowing never to see his face while alive,
Thus I turned away from my father.
Because of Aktulpar,
Wretched soul, I became his enemy.
You have gained steel; do not blunt it;
Oh, my dear foal, let me cherish you!
When you ride your Buurul,
Do not lose Aktulpar.
His heart had other desires;
Your elder, King Temir,
Was such a miser over livestock.
Aktulpar was his one horse;
Your maternal grandfather, King Temir,
Because of Aktulpar,
Had grown estranged from twelve daughters before me.
Such is the story of Aktulpar.
Let me leave this here;
Again, my son, another tale,
Again I shall tell more words;
Let me begin further back:
“Your royal father was a sultan, a king;
Believing whatever words came his way,
Your father Manas, the leopard,
Was such an open-hearted soul.
A soul created like your father,
He never grants to mortals again.
Chubak, Akbaltai's son,
Drawn into trouble and offended,
Because of Kyrgylchal,
Because of the forty companions,
Calamity and grievous words between them,
Because of one damned piece of gossip,
A star was eclipsed in the sky;
Chubak, Akbaltai's son,
Taking offense at your father,
Escaped away among those Oygut.
“This year I have left Manas,” he said,
“I have turned from my true friend;
Tormenting me for no reason,
That damned Kyrgylchal!
I have left my cherished friend, King Manas;
Parted from his twin,
Manas has been left alone.”
Chubak, Akbaltai's son,
Who threw the whole world into turmoil,
He too was a hungry lion, poor soul,
With shrill battle-cries and clamorous shouts,
A mottled banner, a red standard,
In his playful childhood
He set the world in an uproar.
Alai is no equal to Talas;
To your father, Sultan Manas,
Chubak, Akbaltai's son,
Was not a hair's breadth inferior.
When Chubak set out toward his father,
Taking offense at King Manas,
When Chubak returned toward his people,
Raiding the Argyn and throwing them into turmoil,
Upon your bloodstained father Manas,
A calamity no one had seen,
He thought to bring it down.
“If I can, Talas
I mean to leave behind;
Unless I scatter what I have gathered,
May I die unworthy of the name Chubak,
Unless I flee this Talas.
Manas, my friend unto Judgment,
If I defile his marriage bed, can I prosper?
If I am struck on the crown, can I rise?
That friend of mine, Manas,
If I defile his pillow, can I prosper?
If struck again in my side, can I rise?
Whom I held in a mother's place,
The woman Kanykei, whom I made my companion,
If I took her, could I prosper?
Would I be a Muslim in the faith?”
Raging and grieving,
Chubak the leopard, offended,
To the Chinese on your flank,
Sent a letter at the right opportunity,
Sent the letter with all haste:
“Ezkara, king of the Manchus,
Ylama, king of the Chinese,
His wrestler like mighty Zholoi,
There will be great strife in Talas.
Let him come without fail, that
Mighty Ylama the Kalmyk.
Iron-eared giant Kutan,
This is the moment God has given;
This is the time to surround him.
Bloody-whipped Shypshaidar,
You were King Sooronduk's son;
This is your chance to bring slaughter.
Chongtiken, Mangkush's son,
Who felled whoever appeared,
A mace of twelve batmans
Bound tightly at his waist;
If his prowess is true,
Let not one man remain in Beijing.
Tuktuu and Muzkindik,
Hairy-soled, the great wrestler,
Let him take vengeance for that.”
The most skilled of spearmen,
The finest of them all,
When the people head to the uplands,
In the season when horses' tongues turn black,
Kongurbai, Alakoon's son,
If he is truly a warrior,
“Let him come at this time,” he said.
Because of Chubak's letter,
Because of Kyrgylchal's mischief,
Six swift steeds went;
To Karykan of Beijing,
A letter was delivered by hand.
Earth flew up and fine dust burned,
The ground was torn and dust flew;
He beat his booming drum.
When he heard this news,
Karykan, king of Beijing,
Gathered his warriors.
Beaded plumes and coral stones,
Their governor khan mingled among them;
The king of Kaldai mingled among them.
Dressed in their berenzhi garments,
The people of Beijing mingled among them;
Of the vest-clad Kalmyks,
The strong stood apart;
Of the Chinese officials with hat buttons,
The best stood apart.
With iron ropes, clad in mail,
Of the Kara-Khitai and Manchus,
All dagger-handed,
Human noses and ears
They wore as amulets,
All dressed in black coats;
Making no distinction between permitted and forbidden,
The Solon people, countless in number.
Their oxen aged, and they themselves died;
When one died, their custom
Was to share the dead man's livestock equally.
Dressed in mottled red,
Rejoicing as they went,
Never wearing trousers at all,
Never squatting to piss,
They rode untamed horses,
Were strangers to women,
An idol made of bronze,
Saying it was God,
They hung it high toward the sky.
With cotton belts and wide boots,
He gave him an innumerable army of monks,
Dressed him in armor no bullet could pierce,
Mounted him on Alkara,
And sent Kongurbai forth.
“If you can, Kongurbai,
Take vengeance for your father;
Capture Manas alive,
To a dungeon forty ropes deep,
Bring him and cast him in;
Upon that damned Burut,
Take your vengeance thus.”
The sky was clear, the earth clouded,
Not a blade could be seen on the ground;
Earth flew up and fine dust seethed,
Not a single person could be seen.
Spearheads clashed together,
Men's heads knocked together,
Surging in ranks,
Look at the reeds, look at the flood,
Then look at the Chinese host sweeping over everything.
Look at the embers striking through the haze,
Look at the bullets striking without swerving.
Look at the giant no horse could carry,
Look at the wretch on foot with his battle-axe.
Look at the ants, look at the worms,
Look at the people who will surround you!
The army of Beijing set in motion,
About to capture your valiant father,
About to lock him in a pit,
Some three months before, I, your mother,
Had learned this news.
“If the unbelievers sweep over us,
The pillar of the Muslims,
Surely they will destroy it,” I thought.
“Will they dig my pit?
If the Chinese press hard,
They will bring my misfortune to the boil.”
My heart was terribly troubled,
Tears poured from my eyes.
Unable to bear my distress,
I camped along broad Talas.
Your father would not lie with me;
I was a neglected wife for six months.
Brave but without sense,
Your foolish father thought:
“Ak-kula, this year,
I shall fatten properly,” he thought.
Letting Ak-kula loose,
While your father lay without a care,
I had the face of the earth searched;
Without letting one person know,
Without letting a single soul notice,
I had Ak-kula brought in.
I had sand spread beneath him,
Fed him barnyard millet from the rice fields,
Had him exercised to a sweat each day;
Feeding him barley from a hanging feedbag,
I had him exercised to a sweat each month.
“When he joins the spear-fight,
A spear's point will pierce him,
He will meet an untimely death,” I thought.
I made its lining of silk,
For six months I worked its patterns with my teeth.
Its collar gold, its sleeves brass,
Double mail, with lark-eye rings,
A kele cloth coat, a roomy vest,
The handsome Ak-olpok,
That would keep him from defeat in battle,
That no arrow's point could reach,
That thrusting steel could not penetrate,
