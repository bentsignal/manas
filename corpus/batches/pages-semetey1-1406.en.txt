From his father, gray-wolf Manas,
Your young hero was born to surpass him;
His opponent was Kongurbai,
And he had come to Beijing.
He who dwelt in Middle Keng-Kol
Tethered his dapple-gray steed.
If an enemy came out shooting,
He drove them before him.
Whoever stirred his anger,
He took between his teeth and chewed;
No one who faced him remained alive.
He wronged no mortal;
That was your hero himself,
At the time he came to Beijing!
In Bakai's old age,
He had taken him as his support!
His uncle, old Bakai,
Was a saint who had seen so much.
Khan Bakai led the way
Through steep, narrow mountains
And along winding roads.
Bakai traveled without turning,
Riding far without sparing the horse;
He crossed lands of pale haze
And passed over rugged ridges.
How could your hero hold back?
Saintly Bakai reached
The projecting spur of the mountain.
He now turned Kökcholok's head;
Gray-wolf lion Bakai
Spoke on without wearying
To hero Semetey.
My dear young hero!
That dark, towering shape
Is the pass where the wild ram lies;
That blue expanse straining the eye,
That blue peak shaped like a skewer—
That is the pass from which the guardian watches!
That tangled spring and black ridge
Are where the accursed wild ram
Guards its life.
That hellish Akkulja
Stops upon the mountain summit,
Grazes at Toguz-Töbö,
Drinks water from Keng-Bulak,
And faces the plain to chew its cud.
From all that I have told you, it appears
To be the trusted ear
Of the Kalcha, khan of the monks.
If it happens to hear news,
It gallops along the slope;
That swine, ablaze with rage,
Carries news in every direction.
Your gray-wolf uncle Bakai
Stood measuring the ground.
They dismounted and ran,
Striding awkwardly;
They made ready and took in hand
Akkelte and Kökchybuk.
The heroes struck together
Chinese flint and liver-stone,
And set fire to their matchcords.
The purest of marksmen,
His forked gun-rest stood ready.
When they reached the pass,
Let me tell of the hero's state:

And your uncle Bakai then
Implored Semetey:
