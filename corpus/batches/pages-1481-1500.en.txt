The lions Almambet and Chubak,
And Ulak Khan's Syrgak,
Prepared upon the flanks;
Where no running wild ass could cross,
At the heart of ninety mountains,
They gathered and came out to meet them.
The Muslims who had come from afar
Prayed to their God.
The sandy plain before them
Teemed like an anthill;
The Chinese multitude came sweeping,
Teeming like grains of sand.
The Great Beijing of Kakanchyn
Was a place for battle.
Looking upon their looming host,
They were truly the people of forty khans.
Spreading broadly out before them,
Covering the land like a forest,
The Chinese multitude was a flood.
Dividing off in a gray mass,
He set the gray-horse riders apart.
Those who would sweep in unchecked,
The mule-riders, he set apart.
Clothing them in iron armor,
Those who would catch the fleeing,
His warriors, he set apart.
His men who drew the bow,
His champions who spared no life,
He gathered all into one division.
Those who stood and fought without fleeing,
His Dagger-Hands, he set apart.
Those who would tear and devour people,
His butchers, he set apart.
The eye could not reach their end;
They came surging like a lake.
Such was their multitude
That words could not encompass it.
From the dust of the marching Chinese,
One could not tell if it was day,
Nor could one tell if it was night.
The sky was clear, the earth overcast;
People veiled the face of the earth.
Lance points struck together,
Men's heads knocked together;
As though a war-drum were being beaten,
Battle-axes and swords clanged.
The massed Chinese and many Manzhuu
Came pouring into sight;
No reckoning could reach their number.
At the spur where the mountains ended,
The multitude came into view.
Driving them all before him,
Alöökö's Kongurbay,
Their great bull-camel, came into view.
The swordsmen were at the front;
Those who would not spare their lives a hair,
His mighty men, were on the edges.
His marksmen who never missed
He had placed upon his flanks.
Those who would stand and fight,
All his fearless men,
He had placed in the very front.
All his inferior men
He surrounded in the middle,
Keeping them within their ranks.
That the Muslims had arrived,
That all stood prepared,
Kongurbay had seen.
“A Burut will come to single combat
And grant me my turn,” he thought.
To the giant governor riding Karager,
“Go out and fight a duel!” he said,
And placed him at the very front.
That the governor was approaching,
Brave Almambet now saw.
“Forty companions of Khan Manas,
Let one of you go out alone!”
He turned his head and stood.
Egizkhan's youngest son,
A servant beloved by God,
Who had come and settled in Talas
As a companion to Manas,
The warrior and wrestler Baychoro,
Before Almambet
Turned Aksur's head now.
Towering like a white wild ram,
For Almambet's blessing
He stood asking and pleading.
“If you say go, I shall go;
Give me your blessing to receive.
To the champion of the Chinese
I shall go out alone.
If my appointed death has come, let me die.
With the giant governor who came to duel,
Let me try a bout of grappling.”
When Baychoro said this,
Almambet gave his blessing.
Almambet and Chubak, the two lions,
Serek and Syrgak, the paired heroes,
Old Kyrgyl, head of the forty,
And Shuutu with his felt-roof-sized hat,
Yraman's Singer Son,
And unshakable Agayar,
As though they were about to part forever,
Spread their palms,
To the one God who created them
Entrusting him as a sacred charge.
They all gave their blessing,
The massed army watching,
Young and old alike.
The assembled Muslims,
“How will it turn out?” they wondered,
And stood gazing in silence.
The hero-born Baychoro,
Taking his horse as his life's companion,
Taking Almambet's blessing,
Struck his horse Aksur once;
Crying aloud, “Islam!”
The banner fluttering on his lance,
He charged with steady gaze.
He was a very brave man;
His courage was exceptional,
Equal to Kongurbay's.
If a day of hell befell the Chinese,
He was a man who could bar its way.
He too began to charge,
Thundering like the heavens;
Those who heard could not bear it,
Their whole bodies shuddered.
The field where they charged
Had no furrow made by a plow,
No hole dug by a marmot.
Without looking to either side,
The two lions charged,
Not sparing their fly-like lives.
They met and thrust their lances;
Their horses buckled,
They shoved back and forth;
Their lances at the middle
Snapped clean in two.
Seizing battle-axes,
They fought with them,
Neither letting the other strike home,
Parrying with the backs.
They shifted their shields,
And the heroes found their openings,
Striking with battle-axes.
When they came closer still,
They slashed with swords.
When they came right up close,
They seized each other's collars,
Pulled from horseback,
Braced and wrenched,
Until no clothing remained on them,
Tearing and ripping it away.
Baychoro, strong of arm,
Showed his valor:
The governor huge as a hut
He tipped headfirst from his seat,
Laying him across the sacred mane.
When Kongurbay saw this,
His shout split stones;
Unable to contain his wrath,
“I shall torment the Burut!
From Baychoro's hands
I shall wrest him free!” he said.
A wrestler called Zhöökülük
Struck his mule with the whip,
And reached Baychoro now.
The brim of the black felt hat—
Have you heard of him,
Khan Manas's Syrgak,
Whenever others were in distress,
The darling who would rescue them?
Not letting him reach Baychoro,
He arrived like a bullet.
As soon as he reached the wrestler,
From his mule's back
He drove him down with a thrust.
“This is how one gives support!”
The governor who had come to duel
He seized by the bottom hem.
On the blade-flat plain,
The fellow huge as a hut
He dragged like a goat carcass.
Arkesken within its sheath,
Pulling it sideways, brave Syrgak
Drew out his sword now.
The governor being dragged
He cut into separate pieces.
When they saw these two,
When both alike had died,
Would the Chinese stand and watch?
Seven thousand of their governors
Charged and came sweeping in.
Countless governors of their finest
Also came sweeping in.
When the Chinese poured upon them,
How could the Muslims hold back?
They too swept into battle:
Their khans Muzburchak and Kökchö,
Their champions who stood prepared,
Their broad-chested men.
Four thousand troops began to charge,
Their champions who would not turn back.
Infidels and Muslims
Mingled and fought,
Dyed in scarlet blood,
Doing all the arts they knew.
Wearing a coat of blue ribbed cloth,
Riding the steed Kögala,
Khan Baltay's Chubak,
Whose roar none could withstand,
Whose strength none could match in wrestling,
Entered among them, attacking.
Azizkhan's Almambet,
His frail life put under strain,
“If they grow furious, things will go ill;
While Khan Manas is absent,
If the Chinese multitude sweeps upon us,
They will spare no Muslim,” he thought.
Sarala with cowlike lean haunches
He sent racing, wheeling round;
The Manzhuu and Chinese he encountered
He slaughtered and shattered.
Karykan's weather-stones
He took from his belt-pouch,
The stones that changed the weather.
Bringing them together,
Into a bowl of dark water
He carried and placed them.
Rattling words in Kalmyk,
Rumbling words in Chinese,
The spells that changed the weather
He recited, every one.
In the opening and closing of an eye,
Before one could say this or that,
Clouds were driven from the sky,
Crowding the earth's surface.
Hail fell over all the foothills;
Rain fell over all the lowlands.
The moon vanished in faint gloom,
The sun vanished in dusk.
Turning summer's day to winter,
The thinly clad Chinese
He made whimper like girls.
Among the Chinese chilled by cold,
Almambet and Chubak together,
Brave Syrgak charging alongside,
The three entered battle.
Through their middle, Almambet
Gouged a path of destruction.
Around their edges, brave Syrgak
Slaughtered and felled them.
Those who came before him,
Champion Chubak smashed.
Wading through blood like water,
The massed Chinese and Manzhuu
The three keen warriors crushed.
They slaughtered with a shout,
Raising the cry, “Islam!”
The Chinese, as many as a cow's hairs,
They threw into utter confusion,
And tore the battlefield to shreds.
The three mighty ones slaughtered and destroyed;
The place of battle turned to blood,
Dust hid the sun.
Behind them entered battle
Muzburchak, Kökchö and the forty companions,
With so many other heroes.
All of them entered together,
Giving strength to the three keen warriors.
The Chinese came in multitudes;
The Muslims, unafraid of numbers,
Fought them face to face.
All the lances shattered,
The brave men fell,
Heroes reeled and collapsed,
Horses' backs broke;
Many corpses lay there.
So many splendid steeds died,
Men of great valor died;
The dead piled up like mountains.
The deep-rooted Chinese, the vast people,
Were stirred from their very foundations.
When a thousand Chinese died,
Another thousand joined them.
The sky and heavens turned over;
Like a mirage into the sky
Flew the grass twisted up from the earth.
The open field where they fought
Had no hill as large as a saddle-pad,
No hollow that would hold an ankle-bone.
An open plain, a sandy desert:
Muslims and Chinese faced each other;
This was where they shattered one another.
The eye could not catch them when it looked;
Like tumbleweed flying,
Corpses lay every which way.
Of Chinese and Kyrgyz,
One could not tell which was which.
Their keen warriors swallowed blood,
Were dyed in scarlet blood;
Many heroes were destroyed.
Both eyes gouged out,
All the living swallowed dust,
The dead lay swallowing blood.
The warriors went on fighting,
Over the bodies of the dead
Leaping back and forth.
All the men of valor
Laid whips upon their horses,
Made their mouths gape like pits,
And fought, wrenching at one another.
Trampling corpses as though they were sand,
Azizkhan's Almambet
Had gathered his full strength
And fought in earnest.
Raising the battle-cry, “Manas!”
Akbaltay's Chubak
Tore the Chinese to shreds,
Roaring like a lion.
The Chinese multitude could not stand firm,
Could not muster strength against them;
They turned about and fled.
Behind them, the three lions
Drove them into Beijing,
Scattering them like falcons.
Through the middle, brave Chubak,
Who never knew death,
Gouged his way in.
Their elders Muzburchak and Kökchö,
Sweeping upon the Chinese,
All the Muslims advanced.
Alöökö's Fierce One,
The Chinese people's keen sovereign,
Was a hero with lake-deep wisdom;
Among Chinese, Manzhuu and Kalmyks,
He was the foremost lion.
The plain where they fought
Stretched many days' journey.
It lay at one far edge of Beijing,
Where no living soul stirred,
Known only to wild asses.
The horses sank into sand,
The middles of saddles broke;
After many days of battle,
Many splendid steeds were exhausted,
So many people were weary.
When he saw their weariness,
Kongurbay, who had been reckoning,
“They have reached the chosen place;
Buddha has granted my wish.
All the Muslims
Have come to the place of death,” he thought.
Calling Dagger-Hand,
“Take ten thousand troops with you;
Against Almambet and Chubak,
Go out before them and sweep upon them!”
He shouted his command.
Let me tell you of Dagger-Hand:
When he reached the age of six,
His father and his mother
Had him reared apart.
The children at six years old,
When they rose in the morning,
Were made to straighten up and wash their hands;
They were made to strike flour for a hundred days,
Made to strike sand for a hundred days.
Such a man was called Dagger-Hand;
If he struck an assailant once,
He would split him open.
Against Akbaltay's Chubak,
Six thousand troops swarmed.
Azizkhan's Almambet,
Not yielding to those who swept upon him,
Against the hero-leopard Chubak,
Though they swarmed, allowed them no passage.
For many days and many nights,
Almambet went on slaughtering.
Almambet, hard at work,
Turned right and shot once,
Felling them in tens and fives;
Turned left and shot once,
Felling so many on the left.
The Chinese he encountered,
Like people struck by lingering sickness,
He slaughtered, drawing his bow.
The eye could not catch them when it looked;
Infidels and Muslims
Mingled amid the dust;
One could not tell which was which.
Flying earth and clouds of dust,
Scarlet blood flowing without cease;
Immersed in scarlet blood,
So many people lay destroyed.
Not caring a whit for those slaughtered,
Not valuing them at half a penny,
Upon the Chinese people of forty khans
Torment fell without mercy.
Troops of every kind arrived;
The Chinese were stirred from their foundations.
People came in tens of thousands,
Gathered from many places,
In immense, uncountable numbers.
In Beijing where Karykan stayed,
They forged a fortress of iron;
A great Buddha image as large as a house
They had bound and suspended.
His lamas and shaven priests
He had all assembled.
He gathered the people without exception,
And made them swear an oath unto death:
“With the moon dark and night overcast,
Fight in the darkness!
Though the Chinese multitude be slaughtered,
Fight, not one of you left out!
All older than seventeen
And younger than fifty-five,
Gather without a single exception!
If your strength begins to fail,
Like a flood that flows away,
Do not remain alive; perish!”
The Chinese, detachment upon detachment,
Divided into their detachments;
The Manzhuu, lineage upon lineage,
Divided by their lineage.
The Chinese were a vast multitude.
The Chinese from different places
Divided by their regions.
The troops of many kinds still arriving,
As though each came out to single combat,
Fought, sweeping over the earth.
Before the Muslims,
To the army fighting on this side,
He gave his people courage:
Khan Kongurbay shouted,
“Sweep upon them, massed army!
Without sparing your lives,
Here I stand, your leopard!
Fight without stopping, champions!
Here I am, your mountain-like shield!
Do not flee, champions!
They have no lofty towering crag,
Who struck terror through the world—
Their mountain, magnanimous Manas, is absent!
They have no support to lean upon.
Consider it, vast Chinese people:
Their lion, champion Manas, is absent!
Fight, champions,
Until the pliant stalks ripen,
Until autumn's gossamer flies!
If we fight hard for six months,
Their horses will lose blood,
They themselves will lose strength.
Let us fight in earnest;
Those who truly show their valor,
Let us seize alive!
Those whose guilt is great,
Into pits forty cubits deep
Let us cast for forty years!
Without killing the Buruts,
Let us take revenge in this way!
Let us tread upon their heads,
Plunder and ruin their possessions,
Scatter them as far as Mecca!
Ruin the Buruts, take booty,
Break their royal camp, open a road,
Take many beautiful girls!
If they fall into our hands, the Buruts'
Uppity unruly men,
Let us gouge out their eyes!
All their poor, meek people,
Let us seize without killing;
‘Pay tribute for forty years!’
Let us torment them with that demand!”
Thus Kongurbay
Set forth many schemes.
When they heard such words,
The emperor's Great Beijing
Gathered courage and thundered;
All swept forward with a roar.
Roused to courage, rushing,
As though to sweep upon and devour them,
Upon the little band of Buruts
Death seemed to come before its appointed hour.
Almambet and Chubak's valor
Had no equal in the world.
But he who would enter like a lion,
Magnanimous Manas, was not here.
They had no support to lean upon;
Their lion, champion Manas, was absent.
It was a cruel day of distress.
When they saw the Chinese array,
They drew their horses' reins;
The Muslims, their resources spent,
“We are dead!” they said, making last bequests.
“Seen today, gone tomorrow:
Such is this wayward world!
When seven wolves attack one creature,
Will they leave its carcass?
They have truly swept upon us;
Will they spare our fly-like lives?
Pitch-black night has fallen.”
The Buruts' courage has fled;
A day of sorrow has fallen upon them.
While the Buruts stood trembling,
He who never rested on his elbow,
Who never took a moment's sleep,
Who never relaxed his stern gaze,
Who never withdrew what he had extended,
His sword resting against its sheath,
Who, when meeting a mighty enemy,
Drenched him in scarlet blood,
Who girded his waist in earnest,
Who, even against an army thick as flies,
Entered alone and attacked:
The brim of the black felt hat—
Have you heard of him,
Khan Manas's Syrgak,
To the grieving host of troops,
The darling who gave courage.
“Look at yourselves, you Muslim multitude!
Are there any people like you?
Can one prosper who calls you men?
The decree God has written,
Sooner or later you will meet.
Even if you flee to Talas,
When your appointed death comes, you will die!
Will one whose time has come not die?
If he does not die, will he not live his days?
Fleeing, driven from behind,
Chased like dogs by the enemy,
Slaughtered before our appointed death:
Can we behave in such a way?
Stand by me, Muslims!
Let me smash their Great Beijing
And drive them toward Karykan!
Laying the Chinese out stiff,
Let me slaughter them without exception!
Your shamelessness has no equal!
When they mass and sweep upon you,
To say, ‘I shall get away now,’
Your great Manas is not here!”
Taking courage, clashing,
The multitude swept into battle.
Against the massed Chinese and Manzhuu
They entered battle again.
Akbaltay's Chubak
And Azizkhan's Almambet
Roared into the field.
Amid the hazy dust,
With the countless massed army,
Setting blood against blood,
All stood without fleeing.
Chinese and Muslims
Stood upright, face to face,
Made blood flow on the battlefield,
Stepped over corpses like stones.
Against that vast throng of infidels,
They stood fighting without fleeing.
All the splendid steeds reared;
The lances, not a handspan left,
Shattered into fragments.
Shot-proof armor was torn,
Blood flowed, pouring like water.
Infidels and Muslims
Divided into separate groups.
Not entering battle, arranging formations,
Separating out his true heroes,
Alöökö's Kongurbay
Was keeping to the side.
Raging without pause,
Driving all his champions,
Kongurbay herded them in.
His cowards who turned back,
Chasing them with cracking blows,
Kongurbay beat forward.
He set his finest men apart;
His champions nimble on horseback,
Those he placed in a separate group.
Furious and preparing,
His voice drove lives away.
When Kongurbay roused their courage,
Without looking around,
As though they would trample everything,
Not sparing their lives at all,
The Chinese came sweeping upon them:
The leopard-heroes saw them now.
That outstanding Syrgak,
If you looked at his valor,
Was different from all mankind.
He was twenty-one years old,
At the age to fear no enemy.
Kökchebich, shaped like a roe deer,
Bounded and sprang;
Heading straight for Kongurbay,
The fearless man began to charge.
Those who came close,
He swept away with his sword;
The Chinese farther off,
He felled with his lance.
Like a mountain collapsing,
Shattering those he encountered,
Brave Syrgak went slaughtering.
Making the Chinese crawl,
Those who could split an eyeball with a shot,
All their marksmen,
When Syrgak came near,
Drew bows and sent a rain of arrows;
Upon brave Syrgak,
They shot, pouring arrows like hail.
“If death comes, I shall die;
To the lion-champion Syrgak
I shall give support!”
Almambet, that precious keen warrior,
Charged in behind him.
Akbaltay's Chubak,
Surging with Kögala,
Stretching like a lion,
Also entered battle.
When thought finished, they sprang up anew,
Growing stronger from their roots:
The endless Chinese multitude.
His shout splitting stones,
When Chubak entered without restraint,
Alöökö's Kongurbay
Gave his champion orders:
“Six thousand of the Dagger-Hands,
Sweep upon Chubak!
That rash Burut,
Now put an end to him!”
Heedless of pits and mounds,
Not reckoning death or doom,
He fought without restraint.
Sending his horse Kögala racing,
The Dagger-Hands who came before him,
Your lion went slaughtering,
Tearing them from their horses,
Tossing them like fur caps.
Like jackdaws struck by a saker falcon,
He scattered and drove them.
Like small birds struck by a hobby,
He slaughtered the Chinese multitude.
The giant governor and Kongurbay
Now saw this battle.
From the pale, mottled sandy plain,
Kongurbay came thundering;
Those who saw him shuddered.
That fellow skilled among spearmen,
That capable fellow versed in enemy tricks,
Reached brave Chubak now.
Chubak upon Kögala,
Unprepared, from the flank,
He struck once with his lance,
Drove a mighty blow and passed.
The hero left his saddle,
His foot left the stirrup.
Kögala's sacred mane
He could not seize in his hand;
Against the gold-treaded stirrup
He could not brace his foot.
His two legs spread wide;
While Chubak swayed in a daze,
The capable, mighty giant governor
Began to charge Chubak.
“From Kögala's back
I shall drive him down with a thrust!”
He came racing,
His hems and sleeves rattling;
The flame coming from his mouth
Burned and hissed like fire,
His broad, door-sized teeth,
When the governor clenched them,
Crumbled like millet.
Holding his lance steady,
He came crying, “Come, come!”
His battle-cry of “Come!”
Came eating at ears and noses.
Ulak Khan's Syrgak thought,
“The God who strikes will strike us!
When this governor arrives,
He will thrust Chubak down,
Quenching him like a fire.
While I remain alive and breathing,
What aim could I achieve?
From that wretch the governor, Chubak
Let me rescue!”
Thus brave Syrgak spoke.
Kökchebich beneath him
He whipped from side to side.
“Let me arrive before the governor!”
His dark eyes from their sockets
Jutted out and flashed.
Kökchebich beneath him
Lowered his head toward the ground.
If you looked at his speed,
Three times more than before
His flesh had warmed to the race.
Before one could say this or that,
In the opening and closing of an eye,
The wrestler called Governor,
Syrgak reached in his charge.
As soon as he reached the governor,
From the gray mule's back
He drove him down with a thrust.
Thundering like a mighty mountain,
The giant governor fell from his mount.
Before a Dagger-Hand, prepared,
A champion on foot, could arrive,
Buudayyk Khan's Muzburchak,
That precious hero who never turned aside,
The champion, the giant governor,
He passed and cut his head off.
Cotton sash and broad boots,
Kongurbay, khan of the shaven priests,
Amid thick battle and great dust,
Muzburchak, Kökchö and Bokmurun:
He reached these three now.
Reaching them, he smashed them all alike,
Thrust them down and passed.
Before the Fierce One could recover,
Golden forelock and fiery sash,
Azizkhan's Almambet
Arrived like a bullet.
As soon as he reached the Fierce One,
From Algara's back
He thrust him down and passed.
Kongurbay fell from his horse,
His spectacles gleaming,
The flask-sized bead-stone
Flashing upon his head.
Many thoughts filled his heart:
“If God would grant me this one!”
The fierce-maned Chubak's
Tears flowed in ten streams.
“As soon as I reach the Fierce One,
Let me cut his head off!
The dark blood from within him,
Let me scoop and swallow in handfuls!”
Your Chubak came charging.
Before letting Khan Chubak reach him,
A thousand Dagger-Hand champions,
Who had him in their sights,
All thousand arrived together.
As soon as they reached the Fierce One,
They mounted him upon Algara
And carried him away.
Buudayyk Khan's Muzburchak,
As they were about to seize him alive,
The Chinese multitude gathered,
About to put him in their cage.
Ulak Khan's Syrgak,
In such a hard battle,
The darling who would not turn back,
Struck Kökchebich with his whip.
He arrived faster than an arrow.
Seven thousand Shibee, a thousand governors:
Alone he entered among them.
The governors across his path
He swept down with straight thrusts.
The governors surrounding him
He drove back with a shout,
And reached Muzburchak.
Upon arriving, your Syrgak
Grasped him once by the right shoulder,
Set him upon his right knee.
That extraordinary, precious lion Syrgak,
Showing his lion's nature,
Carried Muzburchak away
And mounted him upon Telkyzyl.
At that time, Muzburchak,
When he had fallen from his horse,
When they had surrounded him,
His head had become a bucket of blood.
His entire body
Was numb and unable to move.
With difficulty, mounting him,
Leading him along this way,
When brave Syrgak brought him out,
The Chinese in their joy
Had seen their lucky day dawn.
Aydarkhan's Kökchö,
When he had fallen from his horse,
They put camel-hide headgear upon his head,
Mounted him backward upon a horse,
Before Karykan
To take him as a gift,
They bound him alive.
Seeing this, brave Syrgak
Shouted, “The faith of Islam!”
Raised the battle-cry, “Manas!”
Upon the massed Chinese army
He charged, laying them low.
Through their middle, brave Syrgak
Mowed and drove his way in;
Along their edges, brave Shuutu
Came slaughtering in support.
On one flank, old Kyrgyl
Came tearing his way in.
Baychoro and Agayar,
Making a waste of the Chinese,
Drove their way in.
Girding themselves to risk all,
The Chinese moving in bands,
They broke and scattered their bands.
Mountain-sized Kökchö's
Hands they untied.
Rescuing Kökchö,
Brave Syrgak brought him out.
Rekindling his extinguished fire,
Kökchö of the Kazakhs,
Reviving his deadened life,
Safe and sound, to his horse
He brought and mounted him.
The massed Muslims,
Seeing Syrgak's valor,
All burst into tears together.
Grateful, they spread their hands
And loudly gave their blessing.
Kökötöy's Bokmurun,
When the fierce-maned Fierce One reached him,
When he fell from his horse,
All the Chinese thronged,
All their swordsmen
Swept upon him in a mass.
Falling while he fought,
His wing bent back,
Parted from Maaniker,
The Chinese swarming upon him,
Despairing of his life,
Kökötöy's Bokmurun
Stood weeping with ringing sobs.
“If I would walk, I have no feet;
I am like a snake.
I have no tail to wag in fawning;
I am like a wild ass!
I knew orphanhood at the beginning;
I lost my dear father
When I was twelve years old.
