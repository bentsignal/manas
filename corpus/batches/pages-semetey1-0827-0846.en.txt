As large as a mountain,
Fierce in strength, bitter of face,
He seemed ready to devour whoever appeared.
The sound of his approach
Rumbled as though the earth were splitting.
“Manas’s orphan slave,
Even if you have seventy lives,
Now you have died by my hand,
Now you have met Judgment!” he cried.
He thundered in arrogant boasts;
At the champion’s boasting,
The earth rumbled and split.
Watching his wrath,
The heart could not help trembling.
The champion of Hindustan
Was named Orokjan.
Orokjan’s size
Was that of a little hill.
Having fought in many lands,
He was an experienced man.
Among spearmen he was skilled;
He too was an accomplished master,
Resourceful and adept.
Sparing no effort, that champion
Cried, “Come here for single combat!”
Kyljeerde’s head
He turned and rode forth.
Shouting loudly, his spear
Leveled, he came forward!
As though an evil spirit had seized his wife,
As though fleeing with pursuers close behind,
As though pursuing someone close ahead,
As though his wife had borne a son,
He hastened in great agitation.
From the broad riverbed without a pebble,
No foe would escape his spear.
Your sultan, Manas’s son—
When Orokjan had just arrived,
He turned Buurul’s head,
Without fretting over brow or eyebrow,
And stood watching somberly.
“Let the champion take his turn;
Let him have no regrets.
If this champion has the strength,
Let him slaughter me before a boar;
Semetey held his ground.
When Orok charged and thrust,
“My brow! My eyebrow!”—
Semetey did not recoil.
Beyond his fierce father Manas,
He was a hero born greater still.
The painted spear with its thickened iron head
He grasped it ready in his hand.
Your hero bent and hunched his back,
His eyes bulged,
His ribs stood out.
Without looking around him,
Your orphan, son of the ocean,
Spared not his own life.
When the boy leveled his spear,
Orokjan stood before him.
Manas’s son reached him;
From Kyljeerde’s back,
The moment he reached Orok,
He pitched him down as he passed.
Zulpukor, left by his father Manas—
Grasping it by the hilt,
Drawing it from its sheath,
He cut off Orok’s head.
Of all the thousand champions,
Those remaining alive—
Their own possessions across the field
Many scattered and abandoned.
Each in whatever direction he faced,
Many fled away.
Their uproar scattered like dust;
All the champions went singly,
Unable to regain their composure,
Struggling for their lives,
The champions wandered one by one.
Fleeing in distress, the champions
Before the kushbegi
Ran in agitation.
All the champions who arrived
Maligned him to the kushbegi:
“He is no ordinary orphan boy.
All eighteen thousand worlds—
He would give them no peace.
The imposing wolf has died;
Many who faced him have died.
Khans Janaaly and Karach have died;
Champion Orokjan has died too.
Manas’s orphan son
Has Buurul beneath him.
Not merely us, the peoples of four khans—
Upon all eighteen thousand worlds
He has the strength to wreak slaughter.
Broad-chested, with a mighty breast,
He is a son worthy of a hero.
Beyond his fierce father Manas,
He is born a greater terror.
Whoever grapples with him will not prosper;
Whoever clashes with him will not remain unharmed.
We have seen the boy’s terror;
We have stepped upon
A dragon’s tail.
We have taken fright at the boy.
His renown is great, his face is grim,
Fierce and resolute as a lion,
No man can go to match him;
None who challenges him can remain alive.
He merely played a little;
Thirty champions died together.
These eyes, may they be gouged out, saw it.
Those whose death had come were caught;
All whose death had not come
Barely escaped by fleeing.
If you keep contending with him, this orphan
Will plunder the fine cloth;
Will he not cause terrible havoc?
Destroying you,
Slaughtering us,
With thimble mouths and camel-calf eyes,
With roving dark eyes,
‘He will take the girls as plunder,’ we thought;
We greatly feared this being.
A thousand champions, as though we would not spare him,
We thrust our spears and joined battle,
And fell needlessly into torment.
He could humiliate the khojas,
Drive off and slaughter the mullahs.
If you keep contending, this orphan
Will bring us to our end,
Will slaughter us entirely.”
Hearing the champions, Kozubek—
This khan lost his composure.
Never satisfied with begging and consuming,
Never abandoning his greed,
“That bastard of Kyrgyz stock
Will bring calamity,” he thought.
“The peoples of four khans, every one,
He will slaughter today,” he thought.
“If this orphan continues thus,
Their wide robes flapping,
Their breasts bared,
In terrible haste,
They fled awkwardly to the palace,
Their hearts troubled,
Their chests exposed,
Their noses flaring,
Fleeing in frantic haste.
Scolding their sentries,
Bolting their doors,
They fled into the palace;
Kokand shut itself within the palace.
Driving the champions, shouting,
Those left alive
Semetey came pursuing,
Sweeping them toward Kokand.
Sary Khan, with eyes like little bowls,
Took the road and sped forward,
Shouting “Semetey!”
Urging his horse, he reached him.
Sary Khan came up,
Took hold of the bit,
And spoke words of wisdom:
“My spring before me,
My lamp upon which I rely!
Your father Manas, that surpassing lion,
Your falcon who has died—
When Muslims insulted him,
He would not rise in anger or heed it.
His gold by the pood, his gold coins—
Even if Muslims burned them,
He would not regard it as worth a hair!
Even if they humiliated him like a sheep,
He would pay the Muslims no heed!
Are not their khans fools?
My darling orphan,
What guilt have the many people?
Is not their leader a swine?
What guilt have the other people?
Be patient, orphan, be patient!
In the end, one day we shall die.
If we kill innocent people,
On the coming Day of Resurrection,
How shall we answer for their blood?
If you contend with Khan Kokand,
What desire will you fulfill?
Will you slaughter its people?
When you make your people wretched,
When you slaughter Kokand’s people,
When you bring them all to their end,
Persisting in your stubbornness,
When you slaughter this people,
We shall be deeply steeped in their blood.
On the coming Day of Resurrection,
We shall become black donkeys.
If what is told is true,
On Judgment’s narrow path,
‘He slaughtered the innocent,’ they will say;
Will they not come demanding justice for their blood?”
Your uncle Sary, with eyes like little bowls,
Teaching him what he did not know,
Thus bringing him to heed,
The orphan son of Manas,
Taking the lead rope, led him,
Cutting across to a narrow road.
Teaching him what he did not know,
Thus setting him upon the road,
He took the orphan away.
Having made a spectacle upon the road,
Having wrought havoc in Kokand,
Sated with blood and hardened,
Renowned as a fierce leopard,
They had left Bukhara,
And passed toward Talas.
His mother Kanykei’s Aman-Jol—
They now followed Aman-Jol.
The orphan did not weary of the journey;
They reached the Syr-Darya.
To the banks of the Syr-Darya,
When they drew near,
After enduring much grief and torment,
When they reached the place she had crossed,
In winter’s fiercest cold,
Forty-two times zigzagging through that water,
When they reached the place she had forded,
Then your mother Kanykei—
Would God take her merely for lamenting?
She had prayed for her only child.
The ice then frozen to her calves—
Could it ever leave her very bones?
When she saw the road she had come,
Her heart was deeply troubled,
Tears poured from her eyes,
Her ribs seemed to come apart,
Her heart burned with grief.
She stood gazing, aching,
And to the orphan left by Manas
The meaning of the Syr-Darya
She told with sobs:
“Once, when I scattered my countless possessions,
When I fled Talas on foot,
My child, for your sake,
I roamed along the mountains’ edges.
‘The only one left by the hero,’ I said,
In the fold of my wraparound skirt
I tucked you securely and fastened you,
Chafing my heels,
Aching in the flesh of my calves.
The child cried from hunger;
I, your mother, a wretched slave,
Sobbed, finding no way out.
Unable to ford the Syr-Darya,
Unable to reach my desire,
Allah’s sun in heaven clouded over;
Sixty worries fell upon me together.
My mother upon my back,
You, my child, at my waist,
I carried you both without wearying;
Your old grandmother coughed on my back.
Would God take me merely for lamenting?
The ice frozen to my shins—
Could it ever leave my very bones?
Unable to cross this water,
Unable to reach the place I sought,
The flesh of my soles wore away and split;
The dark blood flowing from my calves
Streamed down the length of a saddle strap.
My voice reached God;
I fell and crawled upon the earth.
Noble Bakai, the valiant one,
Your father’s dear companion,
His companion unto Judgment,
His comrade blessed by Kydyr—
Your fierce father Manas’s
Dear adviser,
Noble Bakai, the falcon,
Brought two swift horses,
Brought two skins of milk.
On the banks of the Syr-Darya,
Your noble elder Bakai’s
Wether slaughtered for our food—
How can they leave my memory,
The deeds of those six scoundrels?
Jaiyk-Kechüü and Tash-Kechüü—
Your royal elder Bakai,
The sage who knew them all,
Kindling the extinguished fire,
Reviving the dead,
Toward Khan Bukhara
The sage set us on the road.”
Tash-Kechüü, which Bakai found,
The familiar ford the sage found—
Your formidable mother Kanykei
Now took Tash-Kechüü.
Leading all the pack animals in a line,
They safely crossed,
And set out upon the open plain.
Taking no rest by day,
Taking no sleep at night,
Never lying down for a full sleep,
Never letting the swift horses weary,
Thus they moved toward Talas.
Traveling across the open plain,
Going far and tiring their horses,
Loading their pack animals,
Having openly departed Bukhara,
They moved toward Talas.
Over ridge after hilly ridge,
Over ridges of the open plain,
At Chiyyrchyk and Salar-Ata,
They prayed as they passed.
Turkestan, hazy in the distance,
The great shrine of Tümön-Ata—
Leaving none of them unvisited,
They paid reverence as they went.
Crossing passes, moving along the road,
Seeing many wondrous ridges,
Seeing sacred places,
Into jagged ridges,
Into gaping places,
They now came.
They saw a ridge rise from the level plain;
As though a person had made them,
They saw hills in various places.
Traveling day and night,
They moved on with a rumble.
Traveling for many days,
Moving far, no little distance,
They journeyed eagerly without wearying.
A wondrous place no person had seen
Came forth to meet them.
It had steep banks and hills,
Places marvelous to behold,
Hillocks and ridges.
Looking closely at this land,
There were places beyond human telling.
Seeing craggy, jutting mountains,
Seeing places no person trod,
To a pass like a saddle ridge
They now climbed.
When they climbed onto the ridge,
Do not ask the hero’s state;
When they climbed that hill,
Do not ask your wretched mother’s state!
When she beheld the land,
She spoke of what its places meant.
“Showing through the haze—
Oluya-Ata and broad Merki.
Oh, my orphan, what can I do?
The past comes into my mind!

Kara-Balta and Sokuluk
Show dark and beautiful.
Your mother, whose fortune has withered,
Pours tears from her eyes.
My child whom I gained at death’s door,
What is seen today is gone tomorrow;
Such is this shitty world!
That gaping gorge there—

Your mother will tell much of her sorrow.

My voice reached God,
My very bones ached;
I cried out, but God did not take me.
The place where she scattered her wealth,
The place where your mother fled on foot,

All these places,
Leaving none, she crossed.
That gray, backward-sloping hill—
That is the pass called Chakmak.
Weary from fleeing, exhausted,

The gray round hill, leaning back—
That is Jorgo Pass.
Sobbing in grief, unable to cross,
I was the one who suffered torment.

I could not cross Jorgo Pass;
Sobbing in grief, I could not walk.
I could not cross Chakmak Pass;

On the face of the earth, my precious life

My heart plunged into darkness and pain,

My child, for your sake,

Your mother Kanykei said this,
Told of terrors no one had heard,
