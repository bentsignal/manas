Gathered Urbu's army,
And assembled it in Talas.
He who dwelt in Sary-Arka,
Called a weighty, formidable hero,
Who husked his millet, stones and all,
Who praised his wife like a maiden,
Whose lattice walls were made of wood,
Aidarkan's hero Kokcho,
Kokcho's son Umotoy—
Stinking sour milk and blue-green dried curds—
From the shouting Kazakhs,
They had brought Umotoy's army;
The villains Abyke and Kobosh
Had assembled it.
Khans who would act with hostility,
Men who would fire shots,
They had gathered them all.
Golden-crowned Muzburchak,
Muzburchak's son Buyanbek—
Saying, “Avenge your father,”
“Manas's orphan son—
Offer him as a sacrifice,”
They had brought him there.
His son Chynkozho,
Kozho, whose deeds too were vigorous,
Saying, “Manas's orphan son
I will offer as a sacrifice,
I will avenge my undying grudge,”
Had joined the gathering.
He who ruled Alty-Shaar,
Manas's enemy from of old,
Alabek, khan of the nomads—
They had brought his army.
They camped along Urbus,
Raising gray-mottled pennons and banners,
They filled the place like ants.
All their khans,
Their elders who ruled the people,
Their religious elder in a mottled robe,
Their mullah who studied lessons,
Abyke and Kobosh's royal camp—
Stirred up in their royal camp,
All of them gathered together,
Swearing an oath unto death,
Making their promise firm,
Manas's only orphan—
To gather and seize him,
To offer him as a sacrifice,
To put him to death,
To kill him and cut off his life,
To make him like an extinguished fire—
All the khans who had come,
Their elders rich in wisdom,
Made their promise firm,
Swore an oath unto death,
Chewed white wheat flour together,
Swore to one another on the Quran,
Slaughtered a white-gray mare,
A holy Quran the size of a horse's head
They placed against their chests.
Manas's orphan son—
Whoever would not seize and slaughter him,
They called a hero only in fine words,
Called his seven forefathers unbelievers.
Muttering and roaring,
From their tumult
The earth split and rumbled.
And beside Kobosh,
All forty companions,
Their elders led by Kyrgyl,
The skilled among the lancers,
The finest, rich in wisdom,
Their lions with elephant-like limbs,
Their unsparing heroes,
Those who would not recoil from stone,
Those who would not turn back at death,
Their broad-chested warriors,
Their stately champions—
Into the midst of that seething army
Khan Sary Khan came.
Khan Manas's companion,
The leopard Shuutu—what did he do?
He said this to Sary Khan:
“The command Allah has laid upon us—
You have come to see it,
Before your appointed death, at their hands
You have come to die!
More formidable have grown these dogs'
Trenches they have dug;
Do you see them making ready,
Their armies that will sweep over us?
For an elder like Baimyrza
They will not go and render service;
To Manas's orphan son
They will not stand as kin.
Rein in over rocky ground,
To Manas's orphan son
Go and deliver my greeting.
A great dug pit confronts him,
Manas's orphan son—
If these men sweep over him,
What will happen? I ask.
Great peril has come upon his dear head.
An unjust claim has arisen;
Saying, ‘If Semetey comes, I will slaughter him,’
Saying, ‘I will put him to death,’
Fierce enemies have come from every side.
They sent throughout Sary-Arka,
Summoned the sons of the Kazakhs,
Sent throughout Alty-Shaar,
Summoned Alabek's army.
He who dwelt in Eki-Kemin,
Called a hero like an ogre,
They sent to brave Urbu,
Summoned his army;
Muzburchak's Buyanbek—
They summoned his army too.
“They have a trench dug,
They have such a seething army,
They have an orator with a bewitching tongue.
Uncle Sary, hear my words:
There are many who will deceive you.
Do not yield to their deception,
Do not enter their royal camp,
Do not dismount from your horse,
Do not die an untimely death.
Ready your battle-axe,
Sharpen your sword and prepare.
What a time, what a day,
What a night will it be?
Drinking spirits and panting,
All the warriors
Around Abyke and Kobosh
Struggled as if mad.
Drinking kumys, growing heated,
Their bones and skin growing hot,
Wiping sweat from their brows,
Against Manas's orphan son
They swore vengeance, pounding the ground.
Their eyes blazed like fire,
They bit through their lips,
Roared in fits of rage,
Unable to stand still, raving,
Shouting ‘Kobosh!’ as their battle cry.
Speaking words that would not stand,
Making their promise firm,
Shouting with all their might,
They had plunged into tumult;
Without sobering at all,
They had plunged into uproar.”
While they were roaring together,
Dear Shuutu went to them.
These are the words Shuutu spoke:
In the midst of the council,
Shuutu said this:
“Riding a gray stallion, bearing a banner,
At the sight of his bearing
The Kyrgyz people rejoiced.
His spirits and his kumys
He made flow across the threshold.
In a single filling of the cauldron
He boiled the meat of forty yearlings;
For the destitute poor and needy
He spread out food and meals.
The rich man's son Baimyrza—
That generous man has died.
He has become one who will not return or turn back;
His horse renowned for speed,
Carrying a letter the size of a palm,
His bowl-like eyes shining,
Great Sary Khan has come.
Do not ask what Sary Khan is like:
His words are sweeter than other men's,
He has one eye on his forehead, as large as a bucket.
He rides a horse no bullet can catch,
Wears a coat no bullet can pierce,
Is clad in blue padded armor.
Had you seen Sary Khan,
He springs like a blue tiger.
His right shoulder-blade is broad;
He is a lion who can rout the Oirats.
He is skilled among the lancers;
His size is equal to a mountain's,
Enough to overcome anyone who faces him.
With a warrior's frame and a heart of stone,
Terrible in majesty, mighty in fury,
He has the appearance of a dragon.
The hero, brave Semetey's
Envoy has come: give him an answer!”
Those who heard this news—
Eloquent Urbu, the fierce wolf hero,
His tongue sugar, his heart venom,
Ran out of the royal camp.
When he saw Sary Khan,
His eyes blazed like fire;
Readying his weapons,
He girded on his Arkesken sword,
To seize Sary Khan,
To offer him as a sacrifice,
To bring the end of the world upon him,
Without speaking with him,
To cut off his head,
Urbu came prepared.
Behind that Urbu,
Kokcho's son Umotoy,
Muzburchak's Buyanbek,
Came rushing together too.
All forty companions
Came again to seize him,
Their elders led by Kyrgyl;
They rushed upon your khan,
The whole seething army.
When the army swept toward him,
He knew they were enemies.
Once he knew they were enemies,
Unflustered, unhurried, unconcerned,
All of them—
What did your dear uncle do?
He counted them as nothing.
At the head of the advancing army,
Dear Urbu readied himself
To seize Sary Khan.
When Urbu came near,
Within a lance's length,
We are one of those who reason;
We are an envoy serving as mediator.
Please stand a little farther away,
Listen from where you are!
You come running in a bluster;
This conduct of yours is unseemly.
I see in my mind that you are an enemy.
Stand where you are!
I have come as a messenger,
Look and listen to my words.
Your wrestlers rush upon me;
You look as though you mean to bind me.
Your fury is extraordinary.
Your stature and your bearing,
Your appearance on horseback—
You have shown a hostile face!
Drinking kumys and shouting,
Drinking spirits and panting,
All of you raving and struggling
To seize me,
To offer me as a sacrifice;
Manas's orphan son—
To charge headlong and strike him,
You have gathered and assembled troops,
Shouting, gathered vast numbers.
I have seen this today.
Your manner has turned wicked;
Our fury has risen mightily!”
High-backed, yet low of stature,
Tuuchunak beneath him—
He urged Tuuchunak once,
Took his lacquered lance in hand,
Before he had finished turning it,
Attacked brave Urbu;
The skilled Sary Khan
Reached brave Urbu.
“This side of the liver,” he said,
“One edge of the lung,” he said,
“The rim of the ribs,” he said,
“The top of his heart,” he said.
The skilled Sary Khan
Arrived before an arrow could;
Where he reached Urbu,
He struck hard as he passed.
Sary Khan thrust unerringly;
Khan Urbu was shattered.
From Algara the horse's back
He shot upward with a jerk;
From the shoulder-blade behind,
Sary Khan's lance
Jutted out with a jutting snap.
Having finished off brave Urbu,
He passed farther on;
Meeting him face to face,
He reached Tazbaimat.
Your uncle Sary did not tire;
Brandishing his lance,
He struck true as he passed.
Tazbaimat, Manas's companion,
Whatever enemy he faced,
Was accustomed to come out for single combat.
When Sary Khan thrust his lance,
Sary Khan's lance
Was the first to reach its mark.
Baimat, who posed as a hero—
His helmet of blue iron
Flew off to the ground.
In a terrible hurry, Tazbaimat,
Before he could take his shield in hand,
Before he could save his life,
Your ready uncle Sary,
Drawing his cutting sword from its sheath,
Arrived before an arrow could;
Where he reached Baimat,
He cut off his head as he passed.
Kokcho's son Umotoy
Rushed upon his flank;
Muzburchak's son Buyanbek
Made ready on this side.
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Drawing bows like falling rain,
Firing shots like hail,
Rushed at him from every side.
“Seize the bald man, seize him!”
“Seize him and kill him!”
Kyrgylchal came bellowing,
Shouting “Kobosh!” as his battle cry.
(King Manas's name had faded;
It had become “Giant Kobosh.”)
Kyrgylchal, head of the forty,
The mad old man who grew proud at forty,
Who thought up a thousand misdeeds—
Manas, that fleet steed,
Had never said, “He has caused trouble.”
What did dear Sary do?
Toward Kyrgylchal,
Without looking up or down,
Without thinking he might die,
The extraordinary hero Sary Khan,
To Tuuchunak beneath him,
Applied his whip left and right,
Spurred him on both sides.
Sary Khan was skillful;
Now he reached Kyrgylchal.
Stirring strife throughout the world,
Was he not your uncle who divided it?
Fighting every enemy,
Was your uncle not accustomed to it!
When he saw Kyrgylchal,
Without looking around,
Without sparing his life at all,
Without looking from side to side,
Without sparing his life a hair's breadth,
Roaring in fury,
Shouting “Manas!” as his battle cry,
“The middle of the beard,” he said,
“The edge of the gullet,” he said.
What did your accomplished uncle do?
Kyrgyl, whom you saw,
He reached and struck as he passed.
Without moving or wavering,
Without shifting from the spot he fixed his gaze on,
The point of the lance passed through,
Reached the gullet;
The first neck vertebra and the back of the skull
It grazed as it passed.
What did your Kyrgylchal do?
From Narkyzyl's back
He fell with a rumble.
Two companions and one hero
Had died at the khan's hands.
Making Tuuchunak rear,
Dear hero Sary Khan
Shattered whoever he encountered.
Moving back and forth,
Sating themselves on blood without restraint,
Drenched in dark blood,
They fought without fleeing,
Battled with their lances,
Shouted their war cries,
Drew bows and fired,
Plunged from one fierce excitement into another,
Like Chinese and Kyrgyz,
They faced one another.
Suffering hardship,
They fought fiercely face to face,
Mounted stallions from the tethering line,
Took up poles from the ground,
Cried, “Oh, my patron!” and struck.
Now there was a terrible battle.
Bowshot rattled,
Arrowheads whistled;
They meant to devour your uncle.
Seven wolves and one sheep—
When they attack, will they leave its carcass?
One accomplished man—
If they beat him, will they leave his body?
Five thousand men led by Kobosh
Rushed at his throat;
Six thousand led by Abyke
Made ready on one side;
All forty companions,
Rushed upon Sary Khan.
“Seize the bald man, seize him!”
“Seize him and kill him!”
“Saddle his Tuuchunak!”
“Gouge out his two eyes!”
There were white banners,
There was shouting tumult;
Like tumbleweed flying,
No one could tell who was who.
Now there was a mighty battle.
Of the companions left by Manas,
Tazbaimat and old Kyrgyl had died;
Of the khans they had summoned,
Eloquent Urbu had died.
Because all three had died,
Abyke charged in fiercely.
If you ask about Abyke,
He was an outstanding marksman;
If you ask about Kobosh,
He was a thundering fellow.
Khan Manas's forty companions,
Told to take a cap, cut off a head;
Told to shed blood, spilled stomach contents;
Told to drink water, drank poison.
Skilled among the lancers,
Experts who knew the enemy's tactics,
Shouting “Kobosh!” as their battle cry,
The forty companions thrust their lances.
Tuuchunak and Sary Khan
Were caught in their midst!
Do not ask what Tuuchunak was like:
His tail was sparse, his mane scant;
He would not let an onager escape across the desert.
At the sight of such encircling enemies,
He was immensely swift, intelligent and practiced.
When a lance was thrust, he did not flinch;
He also had the steadiness of a working mount.
When his mouth was given freedom, he thundered away;
He also had a racer's speed.
The wind could not catch his trot;
No beast could match his pace,
An extraordinary racing creature.
Allah's sun clouded over in the sky;
When the villains pressed around him,
Sixty hardships descended on his head at once.
There was a pit already dug;
All the villains stirred.
Great sorrow lay before him.
When he looked around,
There was a net no bird could escape.
The sun was eclipsed and darkened;
Upon your uncle Sary's head
Sixty cares descended at once.
The villains had overwhelmed him;
Upon your uncle Sary's head
They had brought the end of the world.
“If I fall into these dogs' hands,
Will I die a wretched death,
Will I see Judgment Day?
That plump, gray-mottled hen-goshawk,
That gray hawk, that morning star—
Will I see Semetey?
Bakai, my refuge, is not here;
The Argyn, my support, are not here.
Am I to die in disgrace?
Saying I would be a messenger,
I have come to be cut down.
Intending to come as a mediator,
I have come to be taken.
If I keep fighting them,
I shall die a wretched death.
Whimpering like a young puppy,
I shall die with difficulty.
By setting myself against them,
Why should I try to match them?
My ocean, orphan Semetey—
Let me flee more quickly,
Let me die a more honorable death.
My noble uncle Bakai—
Let me see his face.
Lamenting, let me tell my sorrow,
And then let me die!”
So saying, Sary Khan
Took his dark-sided heavy whip,
Whose blow could deafen the ears,
Seized it and swung it,
Made the courser cry out
With a stroke across its flank.
White reins woven in a special braid,
Reins fitted to Tuuchunak—
Steadying him, he let the reins go.
The heroic khan's Tuuchunak
Lowered his head to the ground,
Took strides like a mountain sheep,
Dust billowing behind him.
Clods from his four hooves
Whistled a lance's length into the air;
The ground he trod burst apart.
At first they saw him once,
Then they saw him once again;
After that, who saw him?
Before they could utter a sound,
Before an eye could open and close,
A blue-mottled cloud gathered in the sky;
Sary Khan, born a fierce wolf,
Fled in plain sight and escaped.
To Chachykei's fortress,
To Kanykei's royal camp,
Below Tort-Kul,
To the side of lord Bakai,
When the dawn broke clear,
When light fell upon the earth,
Your uncle Sary reached Talas.
Tuuchunak beneath him
Had grown hot in the flesh,
Tossing wildly and struggling,
Straining as though his man had died,
Had lowered his head to the ground.
Sary Khan upon him
Could not gather up the reins and check him;
He was in great difficulty.
He was covered in swirling dust;
His whole body, every part,
Was dark, dusky blood.
If you looked at Sary Khan,
His two flailing arms
Could not rise; they were paralyzed.
His dear head, as large as a herdsman's tent—
May this world be ruined!—
Was dark, dusky blood.
Fierce-wolf Bakai asked him for news,
Asked insistently about his suffering:
“Oh, Sary Khan, my hero,
What did you see?” he asked.
What he had seen and learned,
Sary Khan began to tell:
“Lion Bakai, hear my words.
The Argyn and the Kyrgyz—
We had come to see them;
Going as a mediator, I, Sary Khan,
Had gone to my death.
I saw cliffs no goose could circle,
I saw seething myriads of enemies.
I saw the land on reaching Urbus;
I saw heroes Abyke and Kobosh.
An envious multitude
Of people they had gathered;
Those who would split lungs and take gall,
Khans they had gathered.
There were trenches already dug,
Their army stirred in tens of thousands.
Seven banners came,
An army that bent the earth came.
If that army sweeps over us,
Great peril will come upon our dear lives.
The two of us, khan uncle,
They mean to seize if we fall into their hands,
Manas's sole surviving presence
They mean to put to death.
Strutting together and bellowing,
God has struck the people;
Shouting “Kobosh!” as their battle cry,
They ready their swift steeds,
As though an enemy had come from China,
They ready their lances,
Swaying in ranks along the edge,
Surging over the ground.
Their lancers divide into groups,
Their fearsome aspect showing.
The great army they have gathered
Spreads out, making the earth heave.
Kokcho, khan of the Kazakhs,
His son Umotoy,
Muzburchak's Buyanbek,
Urbu's army is there too,
Alabek of Alty-Shaar,
His army is there too.
They ready their lancers,
All their battle-axes
They place along the flanks.
Preparing their marksmen,
They spread them through the center;
Dividing into different groups,
Their appearance is menacing.
Seething and swarming, they divide,
Their fearsome appearance showing.
If we stand our ground without shame,
An army comes that will overwhelm us;
A dark day brings peril to our lives.
Flashing and glittering,
Spreading out and dividing,
With their swordsmen on the flanks—
My dear Bakai, listen!
Their strongest men are at the front.
Their lancers are on the flanks,
Their lions at the front.
Surging like ants,
They come pouring out;
Whether they are insects, one cannot tell,
They come seething out.
Those skilled in drawing bows
They have set apart again;
Their finest men of strength
They have set apart separately.
All their wrestlers
They have put in separate groups.
Driving all of them from behind,
Kobosh has brought them on.
Against the countless Chinese of Kakan,
As though setting out on a holy war,
The six villains are accursed;
God has struck them.
Abyke and Kobosh, two villains,
By name there are six villains,
Khan Manas's forty companions,
All forty came from forty places—dogs and swine.
They no longer heed the ancestral spirit.
When mighty Manas was alive,
All forty were companions who ravaged the world.
Holding pennons, raising banners,
Shouting ‘Khan Kobosh!’
The great trumpet booms,
The shawm clatters;
He leads the army along its way.
They have ceased to fear the ancestral spirit;
They attack Manas's orphan.
If the moon is shrouded in darkness,
What will become of the night?
Uncle Bakai, find a way!
If they overwhelm this orphan,
What will become of our days?
If an arrow comes unerringly to strike,
If a mass comes surging upon us,
I was your sorrowful hound;
A way to meet so many foes
You, Bakai, were the one to find.
You would join what had broken,
You would gather what had scattered;
You were mighty Bakai,
The equal of your mighty Manas.”
When Sary Khan had said this,
Bakai stood and spoke:
“Ah, Sary Khan, my age-mate!
Who has seen cliffs no wild game can cross—
Here stands your dear Bakai!
Who brought havoc upon the world—
Here stands Bakai!
When leopard Manas was alive,
When we scattered the unbelievers' herds,
When we opened the way for Islam,
Ah, Sary Khan, my age-mate!
You have made me suffer torment today;
You have made Bakai, in his old age,
Speak of days long past.
In noble Manas's own time,
When Bakai was about to die,
The great imam of Mecca,
Saying, ‘If my appointed death comes, I shall die,’
For the small band of Muslims,
Saying, ‘I shall serve as imam,’
He came from the Arab land.
The small band of Muslims—
The people filled the place, seething like insects.
Khan Koshoi of the Katagan
Was the one who held the banner.
Mighty Almam was there too,
The finest of the sixty,
The strongest of the forty companions,
Chubak and Syrgak were there together.
Kakan's people were a vast host—
Their numbers were like a black swarm of insects.
We were drenched in bloody gore;
For six months we fought without fleeing.
Going forth to single combat,
For seven months we fought without ceasing.
Kara-Shaar and Kambyl—
I was the Bakai who besieged them;
The Manchus, with crashing blows—
I was the Bakai who battered them.
Upon everyone I could reach,
I was the Bakai who wrought havoc;
The whole world, bringing it to submission—
I was the Bakai who took it.
Does not a man whose time has come die?
The fate that Allah ordains,
Must not a wretched mortal face it?
If an army comes to overwhelm us,
Will not Bakai deal with it?
When my mighty Manas died,
For twelve years I remained;
With those dogs Abyke and Kobosh
For twelve years I exchanged shots.
Where is my death,
My sight of Judgment Day?
Our kin, sprung from the same root—
We shall watch how far they go.
If they overstep the limit,
Then we shall fight.
Those unbelieving sons of Kazakhs,
Those wretches yelling their battle cries,
If we clash, we shall slaughter them.
If we fight, slaughter will follow;
Where shall we find brothers then?
If an army comes to fight with lances,
If our lives are in grave peril,
Then let us wreak slaughter;
Manas's orphan son—
Let us rescue him,”
